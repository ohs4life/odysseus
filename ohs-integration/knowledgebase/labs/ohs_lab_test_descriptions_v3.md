# OHS Lab Test Descriptions — Patient & Provider Guide (v3)

This guide explains 94+ common blood and urine tests in plain language. At the same time, it includes the medical detail doctors and chiropractors need to help patients reach their best health.

**Five levels for every test:**

- **Clinical Low** — below the lab range. Often means a real health problem.  
- **Functional Low** — in the lab range but on the low end. Not sick yet, but not at your best.  
- **Optimal** — the level where your body works its best. This is the goal.  
- **Functional High** — in the lab range but on the high end. Body is under stress.  
- **Clinical High** — above the lab range. Often means a real health problem.

**Reference ranges come from the OHS test ranges file and may differ by sex:**

- **B** \= applies to both males and females  
- **M** \= males only  
- **F** \= females only

**Two kinds of ranges you'll see:**

- **Lab range (clinical range)** — used by most labs. Being in this range means no disease is diagnosed, but it does not mean your body is at its best.  
- **Functional range (optimal range)** — a tighter range from research. Being in this range means your body is working its best and your future risk is lower.

## Cholesterol, Total

**What is this test?** Total cholesterol measures all the cholesterol in your blood. Cholesterol is a waxy, fat-like substance your body needs to build cells and make hormones. Your liver makes most of it. The rest comes from food.

**Why does it matter?** Total cholesterol is one way to check your risk of heart disease. But it only tells part of the story. You also need to know your LDL, HDL, and triglycerides. A total number alone is not enough.

**Reference ranges (mg/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–99  
- Functional Low: 100–154  
- **Optimal: 155–175**  
- Functional High: 176–199  
- Clinical High: 200 and above

**Clinical Low (below 100 mg/dL):** Very low cholesterol is not common. It can happen with an overactive thyroid, poor nutrition, liver disease, an infection, or cancer. It can also run in families. When cholesterol is too low, your body may not make enough hormones. You may feel tired, sad, or notice a low sex drive. Very low cholesterol has been linked to depression and bleeding in the brain (hemorrhagic stroke). To raise it: eat more healthy fats like avocado, nuts, seeds, olive oil, coconut, and fatty fish. Get enough calories and protein. Sleep 7–9 hours. If a thyroid or liver problem is the cause, work with a provider to treat it.

**Functional Low (100–154 mg/dL):** This is below the lab cutoff for "high" cholesterol, but it is lower than the range where your body works its best. It can come from eating too little fat, chronic stress, or overtraining. You may feel tired, have a low sex drive, or recover slowly from exercise. To raise it: add whole-food fats to your meals (nuts, seeds, avocado, olive oil). Eat enough calories. Get good sleep. A provider can check your thyroid, liver, and gut if it stays low.

**Optimal (155–175 mg/dL):** This is the goal range. It usually means your liver is working well and your other cholesterol numbers (LDL, HDL, triglycerides) are also good. To stay here: eat a plant-based diet full of fiber, leafy greens, berries, beans, nuts, and seeds. Exercise most days. Sleep well. Keep stress low. Get your other cholesterol numbers checked too.

**Functional High (176–199 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It often means your LDL or triglycerides are too high. It can be a sign that your body is having trouble managing blood sugar, your liver is sluggish, or your thyroid is slow. To lower it: cut back on sugar, white bread, white pasta, alcohol, and fried foods. Eat more fiber, plant sterols (found in nuts and seeds), and omega-3 fats (found in fish, flax, and chia). Exercise daily. Lose extra body fat if needed.

**Clinical High (200 mg/dL and above):** This raises your risk of heart disease, especially if your LDL or triglycerides are also high. Causes can be genetic, related to insulin resistance, or from a diet high in saturated fat and sugar. To lower it: work with a provider. Make big changes to your diet — eat a whole-food, plant-based diet with lots of fiber, beans, vegetables, and nuts. Exercise daily. Lose extra weight. You may also need supplements (like bergamot, plant sterols, or red yeast rice) or medicine.

## HDL Cholesterol

**What is this test?** HDL stands for high-density lipoprotein. HDL is sometimes called the "good" cholesterol. It carries extra cholesterol from your blood back to your liver, where your body gets rid of it. HDL also helps fight swelling (inflammation) in your body.

**Why does it matter?** High HDL is linked to a lower risk of heart disease. Low HDL is linked to a higher risk. HDL also gives you a clue about how well your body handles blood sugar and fat.

**Reference ranges (mg/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–39  
- Functional Low: 40–59  
- **Optimal: 60–85**  
- Functional High: 86–99  
- Clinical High: 100 and above

**Clinical Low (below 40 mg/dL):** Low HDL is a warning sign for heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and a condition called metabolic syndrome (a mix of high blood sugar, high blood pressure, extra belly fat, and high triglycerides). Causes include insulin resistance, belly fat, not exercising, smoking, a diet high in sugar and refined carbs, certain medicines, and your genes. To raise it: replace white bread, white pasta, and sweets with whole grains, beans, and vegetables. Add daily cardio exercise (like walking, biking, or swimming) and strength training. Lose extra belly fat. Quit smoking. Eat omega-3 foods (fatty fish, flax, chia, walnuts). Limit alcohol.

**Functional Low (40–59 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It is often a sign of early insulin resistance, extra belly fat, or a sedentary lifestyle. To raise it: build a daily exercise habit. Lose extra belly fat. Eat more fiber and healthy fats. Cut back on sugar, processed foods, and alcohol. A provider can check your fasting insulin to see if insulin resistance is the cause.

**Optimal (60–85 mg/dL):** This is the goal. It means your body is good at cleaning up extra cholesterol. You likely exercise often, eat a healthy diet, and have a healthy body weight. To stay here: keep up your exercise, eat whole foods, get good sleep, and limit alcohol. Periodically check your other cholesterol numbers.

**Functional High (86–99 mg/dL):** This is on the high side of the range. It usually means your body is making plenty of HDL. In most cases, this is a great sign. Very rarely, very high HDL (above 90\) may not work as well as it should, especially in people with heart disease or chronic inflammation. If you have heart disease and very high HDL, ask your provider to check how well your HDL is working. Otherwise, keep up your healthy habits.

**Clinical High (100 mg/dL and above):** Very high HDL is rare. It can be a sign of great fitness (endurance athletes) or, less often, a gene change. In some studies, very high HDL in people with heart disease has been linked to higher risk. If your HDL is very high and you have heart disease, talk to your provider. Otherwise, keep up your healthy lifestyle and check your other cholesterol numbers.

## Triglycerides

**What is this test?** Triglycerides are a type of fat in your blood. Your body makes them from extra calories, sugar, and alcohol you eat or drink. They are stored in your fat cells for energy.

**Why does it matter?** High triglycerides raise your risk of heart disease, fatty liver, and pancreatitis (a serious pancreas problem). They are one of the first signs that your body is having trouble with blood sugar and insulin. They are often high along with low HDL.

**Reference ranges (mg/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–36  
- Functional Low: 37–74  
- **Optimal: 75–100**  
- Functional High: 101–149  
- Clinical High: 150 and above

**Clinical Low (below 37 mg/dL):** Very low triglycerides are not common. They can be from a very low-fat diet, malabsorption (your gut not absorbing food well), an overactive thyroid, adrenal problems, or starvation. Very low triglycerides can make it hard to absorb vitamins A, D, E, and K. To raise them: eat more healthy fats (avocado, olive oil, nuts, seeds, fatty fish, coconut). Eat enough calories. If your gut is not absorbing food well, see a provider to find out why.

**Functional Low (37–74 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can happen if you eat very little fat or if your body is not absorbing food well. You may feel tired, have dry skin, or notice hormone changes. To raise them: add whole-food fats to your meals. Make sure you are eating enough calories. A provider can check your thyroid and gut if it stays low.

**Optimal (75–100 mg/dL):** This is the goal. It usually means your body is great at handling blood sugar and fat. To stay here: limit sugar, refined carbs, processed foods, and alcohol. Eat lots of fiber, omega-3 fats, and whole foods. Exercise most days. Keep a healthy body weight.

**Functional High (101–149 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It is one of the first signs of insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, and fatty liver. Common causes: too much sugar, white bread, white pasta, alcohol, belly fat, and not enough exercise. To lower them: cut way back on sugar, refined carbs, and alcohol. Eat more fiber (especially oats, beans, and ground flax). Exercise every day. Lose 5–10% of your body weight if you have extra to lose.

**Clinical High (150 mg/dL and above):** This raises your risk of pancreatitis (especially above 500), heart disease, and fatty liver. Causes include insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, drinking too much alcohol, an underactive thyroid, kidney disease, certain medicines, and your genes. To lower them: cut out sugar, refined carbs, processed foods, and alcohol. Eat a whole-food, plant-based diet with lots of fiber and omega-3 fats. Exercise daily. Lose extra weight. A provider can check for other causes and may suggest omega-3 supplements (2–4 grams a day of EPA and DHA) or medicine.

## LDL Cholesterol

**What is this test?** LDL stands for low-density lipoprotein. LDL carries cholesterol from your liver to the rest of your body. LDL is sometimes called the "bad" cholesterol because too much of it can build up in your artery walls and form plaque. Plaque can narrow your arteries and lead to heart attacks and strokes.

**Why does it matter?** LDL is one of the main markers for heart disease risk. But not all LDL is the same. Small, dense LDL particles are more dangerous than big, fluffy ones. The number of LDL particles (called ApoB) is an even better marker than the LDL mass.

**Reference ranges (mg/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–10  
- Functional Low: 11–34  
- **Optimal: 35–85**  
- Functional High: 86–100  
- Clinical High: 101 and above

**Clinical Low (below 11 mg/dL):** Very low LDL is rare. It can happen on a very low-fat or plant-based diet, with malabsorption, an overactive thyroid, liver disease, or certain gene changes. Very low LDL is usually good for your heart, but it can be linked to depression, bleeding in the brain, or cancer in some studies. If you feel unwell, see a provider. To raise LDL: eat more healthy fats and make sure you eat enough calories. If illness is the cause, treat the cause.

**Functional Low (11–34 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It usually means your body has very few LDL particles, which is great for your heart. This is common in people who eat a plant-based diet and exercise often. No changes needed. Keep up your healthy habits.

**Optimal (35–85 mg/dL):** This is the goal. It usually means your LDL particle number is also low. To stay here: eat a high-fiber, plant-based diet with whole grains, beans, vegetables, fruits, nuts, and seeds. Eat omega-3 fats and plant sterols. Avoid trans fats (found in many processed foods). Exercise most days.

**Functional High (86–100 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It may mean you have too many LDL particles, some of which may be small and dense. Common causes: insulin resistance, a diet high in saturated fat, an underactive thyroid, or your genes. To lower it: cut back on saturated fat (fatty meats, full-fat dairy, butter). Eat more fiber, plant sterols, and omega-3 fats. Exercise every day. Lose extra body fat. A provider can check your thyroid and blood sugar.

**Clinical High (101 mg/dL and above):** This raises your risk of heart disease, especially if you have other risk factors (high blood pressure, diabetes, family history, smoking, high ApoB, or high Lp(a)). Causes include genetic high cholesterol, insulin resistance, a poor diet, not exercising, an underactive thyroid, kidney disease, and chronic inflammation. To lower it: switch to a whole-food, plant-based diet full of fiber, beans, vegetables, nuts, and seeds. Exercise daily. Lose extra weight. Quit smoking. Manage stress. A provider may suggest supplements (bergamot, red yeast rice, plant sterols, psyllium) or medicine.

## Non HDL Cholesterol

**What is this test?** Non-HDL cholesterol is your total cholesterol minus your HDL. It includes all the "bad" cholesterol: LDL, VLDL, IDL, Lp(a), and chylomicron remnants. Many heart doctors think non-HDL is a better heart risk marker than LDL alone because it captures more of the particles that can cause plaque.

**Why does it matter?** It gives you a more complete picture of the cholesterol that can build up in your arteries. It is especially useful if you have high triglycerides, diabetes, or belly fat.

**Reference ranges (mg/dL, both sexes):**

- **Optimal: 0–109**  
- Functional High: 110–129  
- Clinical High: 130 and above

Note: This test does not have a Clinical Low or Functional Low category in the OHS ranges. Values from 0 up to 109 are all considered Optimal or better.

**Optimal (0–109 mg/dL):** This is the goal for most healthy adults. It means your body is managing cholesterol well. To stay here: keep up your fiber (25–40 grams a day from vegetables, beans, whole grains, ground flax), omega-3 fats, plant sterols, and colorful plant foods. Exercise, sleep well, and manage stress. Check ApoB and hs-CRP for a more complete picture.

**Functional High (110–129 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It raises your heart risk, especially with other risk factors. Common causes: insulin resistance, a diet high in refined carbs and saturated fat, not exercising, an underactive thyroid, or your genes. To lower it: cut back on refined carbs and saturated fat. Eat more fiber. Exercise daily. Lose extra body fat. A provider can check your fasting insulin, thyroid, and inflammation.

**Clinical High (130 mg/dL and above):** This raises your heart risk a lot. Causes mirror those of high LDL and triglycerides: genetic factors, metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance, poor diet, no exercise, an underactive thyroid, kidney disease, and chronic inflammation. To lower it: work with a provider. Make big changes to your diet — a whole-food, plant-based diet with lots of fiber, beans, and vegetables. Exercise daily. Lose extra weight. Manage stress. You may need natural supplements or medicine, especially if ApoB is high or you have other risk factors.

## CHOL/HDLC Ratio

**What is this test?** This ratio is your total cholesterol divided by your HDL. It shows the balance between all your cholesterol and the "good" HDL. A lower ratio is better.

**Why does it matter?** It is one of the best single cholesterol markers for heart risk. A lower ratio means a better balance. It is a stronger predictor of heart problems than total cholesterol or LDL alone.

**Reference ranges (ratio, both sexes):**

- **Optimal: 0–2.99**  
- Functional High: 3.0–4.99  
- Clinical High: 5.0 and above

Note: This test does not have a Clinical Low or Functional Low category in the OHS ranges. Values from 0 up to 2.99 are all considered Optimal.

**Optimal (0–2.99):** This is the goal. It means a great balance between your total cholesterol and HDL. To stay here: keep up your fiber, plant sterols, omega-3 fats, and a colorful plant-based diet. Exercise most days. Sleep 7–9 hours. Manage stress. Periodically check your other cholesterol numbers, especially ApoB.

**Functional High (3.0–4.99):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It means your total cholesterol is too high, your HDL is too low, or both. Common causes: insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, a sedentary lifestyle, belly fat, a poor diet, an underactive thyroid, and inflammation. To lower it: cut back on refined carbs and processed foods. Eat more fiber and omega-3 fats. Exercise daily (both cardio and strength training). Lose extra body fat. A provider can check your fasting insulin, thyroid, and inflammation.

**Clinical High (5.0 and above):** This is a strong warning sign for heart disease. It is usually seen in people with metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, or genetic high cholesterol. To lower it: work with a provider. Make big changes to your diet — a whole-food, plant-based diet full of fiber, beans, and vegetables. Exercise daily. Lose extra weight. Manage stress. You may need natural supplements or medicine, especially if you have other risk factors (high ApoB, high Lp(a), diabetes, family history).

## C-Reactive Protein HS (hs-CRP)

**What is this test?** hs-CRP (high-sensitivity C-reactive protein) is a marker of inflammation in your body. Inflammation is your body's response to injury, infection, or stress. Short-term inflammation helps you heal. Long-term (chronic) inflammation can damage your heart, blood vessels, and other organs.

**Why does it matter?** High hs-CRP raises your risk of heart disease, heart attack, stroke, diabetes, and many other chronic diseases. It is one of the best ways to check for hidden inflammation.

**Reference ranges (mg/L, both sexes):**

- **Optimal: 0–0.99**  
- Functional High: 1.0–3.0  
- Clinical High: 3.01 and above

Note: This test does not have a Clinical Low or Functional Low category in the OHS ranges. Values from 0 up to 0.99 are all considered Optimal.

**Optimal (0–0.99 mg/L):** This is the goal. It means low inflammation and low risk of heart disease and other chronic problems. To stay here: keep up an anti-inflammatory diet rich in vegetables, berries, omega-3 fats, and antioxidants. Exercise most days. Sleep 7–9 hours. Manage stress. Take care of your teeth and gut, since hidden infections can raise hs-CRP. Periodically check it.

**Functional High (1.0–3.0 mg/L):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It means there is some chronic inflammation in your body. Common causes: a poor diet (sugar, processed foods, trans fats, processed meats), belly fat, insulin resistance, no exercise, chronic stress, poor sleep, smoking, gum disease, gut problems, hidden infections, and autoimmune disease. To lower it: switch to a whole-food, anti-inflammatory diet full of vegetables, berries, beans, nuts, seeds, and omega-3 fats. Cut out sugar and processed foods. Exercise daily. Sleep 7–9 hours. Manage stress. A provider can check for hidden infections, gut problems, and food sensitivities.

**Clinical High (3.01 mg/L and above):** This means a lot of inflammation in your body. It raises your risk of heart disease a lot. Causes: active infection, autoimmune disease (like lupus or rheumatoid arthritis), severe obesity, advanced gum disease, recent injury or surgery, cancer, or other chronic inflammatory conditions. A short-term rise can come from a recent illness — re-check it once you feel better. To lower it: work with a provider to find the cause. Use anti-inflammatory foods, exercise, sleep, stress control, and supplements (omega-3s, curcumin, ginger). Treat any hidden infections or chronic conditions.

## Glucose (Fasting)

**What is this test?** Fasting glucose measures the amount of sugar (glucose) in your blood after not eating for at least 8 hours. Glucose is your body's main source of energy. It comes from the food you eat.

**Why does it matter?** It is the most common test for blood sugar problems. High fasting glucose is a sign of insulin resistance, pre-diabetes, or diabetes. Low fasting glucose is a sign of low blood sugar (hypoglycemia). Even "normal" fasting glucose can miss early blood sugar problems, so it is best to check fasting insulin and HbA1c too.

**Reference ranges (mg/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–63  
- Functional Low: 63.01–78  
- **Optimal: 78.01–94**  
- Functional High: 94.01–99  
- Clinical High: 99.01 and above

**Clinical Low (below 63 mg/dL):** This is called hypoglycemia (low blood sugar). It can make you feel shaky, sweaty, anxious, dizzy, confused, or even pass out. Causes: your body makes too much insulin, you skip meals, your adrenal glands or thyroid are sluggish, severe liver disease, certain medicines (insulin, sulfa drugs), or a rare tumor (insulinoma). To raise blood sugar: eat smaller, more frequent meals with protein, healthy fats, and complex carbs. Avoid eating sugar on an empty stomach. A provider can check your adrenals, thyroid, and pancreas.

**Functional Low (63.01–78 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can be normal in lean, active people, or a sign of reactive hypoglycemia (blood sugar drops after a high-carb meal), adrenal stress, or eating too few carbs. You may feel tired, cranky, or lightheaded between meals. To optimize: eat balanced meals every 3–4 hours. Make sure you have protein, healthy fats, and complex carbs. Don't skip meals. If symptoms continue, a provider can check your adrenals and thyroid.

**Optimal (78.01–94 mg/dL):** This is the goal. It means your body is great at managing blood sugar. To stay here: keep up a high-fiber, plant-based diet with protein. Exercise most days (especially strength training and walking). Manage stress. Sleep 7–9 hours. Avoid sugar and processed foods. Periodically check fasting insulin and HbA1c for a fuller picture.

**Functional High (94.01–99 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It is one of the first signs of insulin resistance and pre-diabetes. Common causes: too much sugar and refined carbs, a sedentary lifestyle, belly fat, chronic stress, poor sleep, and your genes. Many people in this range have high insulin (called hyperinsulinemia) and are 5–10 years away from diabetes. To lower it: cut out sugar, refined carbs, and processed foods. Eat 25–40 grams of fiber a day. Exercise daily. Lose extra belly fat. A provider should check fasting insulin and HOMA-IR.

**Clinical High (99.01 mg/dL and above):** This is the cutoff for pre-diabetes (100–125) or diabetes (above 126 on two tests). It raises your risk of heart disease, nerve damage, kidney disease, eye disease, and memory loss. Causes: long-term insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, chronic stress, a poor diet, no exercise, belly fat, certain medicines, and your genes. To lower it: work with a provider. Make big changes — a whole-food, plant-based diet, daily exercise, weight loss, stress control, and better sleep can often reverse early type 2 diabetes. You may also need natural supplements (berberine, chromium, cinnamon) or medicine.

## Hemoglobin A1c

**What is this test?** HbA1c (hemoglobin A1c) shows the average amount of sugar stuck to your red blood cells over the past 2–3 months. Red blood cells live about 3 months, so this test gives you a longer-term picture of your blood sugar.

**Why does it matter?** It is the gold standard for checking long-term blood sugar control. It is used to diagnose and monitor diabetes. It is much more useful than a single fasting glucose test because it does not change from day to day.

**Reference ranges (%, both sexes):**

- **Optimal: 0–5.7**  
- Functional High: 5.71–6.4  
- Clinical High: 6.4 and above

Note: This test does not have a Clinical Low or Functional Low category in the OHS ranges. Values from 0 up to 5.7% are all considered Optimal.

**Optimal (0–5.7%):** This is the goal. It means your blood sugar has been well-controlled for months. To stay here: keep up a high-fiber, plant-based diet with protein and healthy fats. Exercise most days. Manage stress. Sleep 7–9 hours. Check fasting insulin and fasting glucose for a fuller picture.

**Functional High (5.71–6.4%):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It is an early sign of blood sugar problems. It usually means your body is starting to resist insulin. Common causes: too much sugar and refined carbs, no exercise, belly fat, chronic stress, and your genes. Many people in this range have high fasting insulin and triglycerides. To lower it: cut out sugar and processed foods. Eat more fiber. Exercise daily. Lose extra belly fat. A provider can check fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, and post-meal glucose.

**Clinical High (6.4% and above):** This is in the diabetes range on the OHS scale. It raises your risk of heart disease, nerve damage, kidney disease, eye disease, and memory loss. Causes: long-term insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, a poor diet, no exercise, belly fat, chronic stress, and your genes. To lower it: work with a provider. Make big changes — a whole-food, plant-based diet, daily exercise, weight loss (5–10% of your body weight can make a big difference), stress control, and better sleep are foundational. You may also need natural supplements (berberine, chromium, cinnamon, gymnema) or medicine. A continuous glucose monitor (CGM) can help you see how food affects your blood sugar.

## Urine \- Glucose

**What is this test?** This test checks for sugar (glucose) in your urine. Normally, your kidneys filter glucose out of your blood and put it back into your body. So urine should not have any glucose in it.

**Why does it matter?** If glucose shows up in your urine, it usually means there is too much in your blood — more than your kidneys can handle. This is often a sign of diabetes. The test is not as accurate as a blood test, but it is a quick screening tool.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):**

- **Optimal: 0–3 (negative)**  
- Functional High: 3.01–6  
- Clinical High: 6.01 and above

Note: This test does not have a Clinical Low or Functional Low category in the OHS ranges.

**Optimal (0–3, Negative):** This is the goal. It means your blood sugar is well-controlled and your kidneys are working normally. To stay here: keep up a high-fiber, plant-based diet, daily exercise, healthy body weight, and stress control. Check blood sugar (fasting glucose and HbA1c) periodically.

**Functional High (3.01–6):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean early diabetes, gestational diabetes (diabetes during pregnancy), kidney problems, or pregnancy itself. To optimize: work with a provider. They will likely order blood tests (fasting glucose, HbA1c) to find out. Cut back on sugar and refined carbs. Increase fiber.

**Clinical High (6.01 and above, Positive):** Positive urine glucose means your blood sugar has gone above the kidney's limit. It is a sign of poorly controlled diabetes, gestational diabetes, kidney disease, or pregnancy. To address: work with a provider right away. They will check your blood sugar and treat the cause. Diet, exercise, weight loss, and sometimes medicine are needed.

## Urine \- Ketones

**What is this test?** Ketones are made when your body burns fat for fuel instead of sugar. They can show up in your urine when you are fasting, on a low-carb or keto diet, doing intense exercise for a long time, or when you have uncontrolled diabetes.

**Why does it matter?** Small amounts of ketones are normal during fasting, low-carb eating, or long exercise. Large amounts can be a sign of a serious problem called diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) if you have diabetes.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):**

- **Optimal: 0–0.99 (negative)**  
- Clinical High: 1 and above (positive)

Note: This test does not have a Clinical Low, Functional Low, or Functional High category in the OHS ranges.

**Optimal (0–0.99, Negative):** On a mixed diet, negative is optimal. On a low-carb or keto diet, small-to-moderate ketones are expected and show your body is in ketosis (burning fat for fuel). Many people use this for weight loss, mental clarity, blood sugar control, or seizure control. To maintain: keep up your current way of eating. Periodically check your ketones. Blood beta-hydroxybutyrate (0.5–3.0 mmol/L) is more accurate than urine.

**Clinical High (1 and above, Positive):** Moderate-to-large ketones in a person not on a low-carb diet can be from prolonged fasting, severe vomiting, an overactive thyroid, very intense exercise, alcoholism, or pregnancy with severe vomiting (hyperemesis gravidarum). Large ketones with high blood sugar (above 250 mg/dL) in a person with diabetes is a medical emergency called diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). To address: work with a provider to find the cause. If it is from fasting or a low-carb diet, this is expected. If it is from illness, you may need fluids and treatment. DKA requires immediate medical care with insulin, fluids, and electrolytes.

## Uric Acid

**What is this test?** Uric acid is a waste product made when your body breaks down purines. Purines are found in your cells and in some foods (red meat, organ meats, certain seafood, beer). Your kidneys remove uric acid from your blood and put it in your urine.

**Why does it matter?** High uric acid is linked to gout (a painful joint condition), kidney stones, kidney disease, metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance, and heart disease. Uric acid also acts as an antioxidant in your blood, so some uric acid is good.

**Reference ranges (mg/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–1.9  
- Functional Low: 2.0–3.6  
- **Optimal: 3.7–6.0**  
- Functional High: 6.1–8.6  
- Clinical High: 8.7 and above

**Clinical Low (below 2.0 mg/dL):** Very low uric acid is rare. It can be from a low-purine diet, severe liver disease, certain medicines (allopurinol, probenecid), or rare genetic disorders. Low uric acid can mean low antioxidant protection. You may feel tired. To raise it: eat more purine-rich whole foods (beans, mushrooms, asparagus). Support your liver. A provider can check your liver and antioxidant status (vitamin C, glutathione).

**Functional Low (2.0–3.6 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can be from a low-purine diet or low antioxidant levels. To raise it: eat more whole-food purine sources (beans, mushrooms, asparagus). Add antioxidant-rich foods (berries, citrus, leafy greens, green tea, vitamin C). A provider can check your liver and antioxidant status.

**Optimal (3.7–6.0 mg/dL):** This is the goal. It means your body is managing purines well. To stay here: limit high-purine foods in excess (organ meats, large amounts of red meat, certain seafood). Cut back on alcohol (especially beer and spirits). Drink plenty of water. Eat cherries, berries, citrus, and coffee (all help lower uric acid). Stay at a healthy weight and exercise.

**Functional High (6.1–8.6 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It is one of the earliest signs of insulin resistance, fatty liver, and metabolic syndrome. Common causes: too much sugar (especially high-fructose corn syrup), alcohol, organ meats, fructose, dehydration, insulin resistance, kidney problems, and certain medicines. To lower it: cut out sugary drinks and alcohol. Reduce high-purine foods. Drink more water. Lose extra body fat. Eat cherries, berries, citrus, and coffee. A provider can check your fasting insulin, liver, and kidneys.

**Clinical High (8.7 mg/dL and above):** This raises your risk of gout, kidney stones, kidney damage, high blood pressure, and heart disease. Sustained high uric acid can cause crystals to form in your joints (gout), kidneys (stones), and other tissues. Causes: long-term insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, kidney disease, your genes, a high-purine diet, alcohol abuse (especially beer), dehydration, and certain cancers or chemo. To lower it: work with a provider. Cut out alcohol, sugary drinks, and high-purine foods. Eat cherries, berries, citrus, and coffee. Lose extra weight. Stay hydrated. You may also need natural supplements (tart cherry, quercetin, vitamin C) or medicine (allopurinol, febuxostat).

## Protein, Total

**What is this test?** Total protein measures all the protein in your blood. There are two main kinds: albumin (made by your liver) and globulin (made by your liver and immune system). Proteins do many jobs — they carry hormones, fight infection, and keep fluid in your blood vessels.

**Why does it matter?** It gives you a quick look at your nutrition, liver function, immune function, and kidney health. Low protein can mean malnutrition, malabsorption, or liver or kidney problems. High protein can mean chronic inflammation, infection, or a blood disorder.

**Reference ranges (g/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–6.0  
- Functional Low: 6.01–6.49  
- **Optimal: 6.5–7.5**  
- Functional High: 7.51–8.0  
- Clinical High: 8.01 and above

**Clinical Low (below 6.0 g/dL):** This is rare. It can mean malnutrition, malabsorption (celiac, IBD, SIBO), advanced liver disease, kidney disease (losing protein in urine), severe burns, chronic infections, cancer, or rare genetic disorders. You may have swelling, muscle loss, fatigue, and slow healing. To raise it: work with a provider. Eat 0.8–1.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight (more if you are active or older). Good sources: beans, fish, eggs, poultry, Greek yogurt, bone broth. Address the underlying cause (gut, liver, kidney).

**Functional Low (6.01–6.49 g/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means you are not eating enough protein, your gut is not absorbing food well, or you have chronic inflammation. You may feel tired, recover slowly, or get sick often. To raise it: add more protein to your meals (beans, fish, eggs, poultry, Greek yogurt, nuts, seeds). Make sure you eat enough calories. A provider can check your gut, liver, and inflammation.

**Optimal (6.5–7.5 g/dL):** This is the goal. It means your body is making enough protein, your liver is working well, and you have a balanced immune system. To stay here: eat 0.8–1.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight spread across meals. Eat a colorful plant-based diet. Periodically re-check, especially with major diet changes or illness.

**Functional High (7.51–8.0 g/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It is often from high globulins (immune proteins), which can mean chronic inflammation, chronic infection, or a high-protein diet. Common causes: long-term infections (viral, dental, gut), autoimmune disease, chronic inflammation, and a high-protein or high-dairy diet. To optimize: address any hidden infections or inflammation. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet (vegetables, berries, omega-3 fats). Cut back on alcohol. Manage stress. A provider can check your inflammation, gut, and immune markers.

**Clinical High (8.01 g/dL and above):** This is uncommon and needs evaluation. Causes: chronic inflammation, chronic infections, autoimmune disease (lupus, RA, sarcoidosis), multiple myeloma, Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia, dehydration, or rarely liver disease. To address: work with a provider. They may order a protein electrophoresis (SPEP) and other tests. Treat the cause. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet.

## Albumin

**What is this test?** Albumin is the most common protein in your blood. Your liver makes it. Albumin carries hormones, vitamins, and medicines around your body. It also keeps fluid in your blood vessels and acts as an antioxidant.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you about your liver function, nutrition, and inflammation. Low albumin is a strong warning sign for serious illness and is linked to higher death rates in hospitalized patients. Higher albumin within the normal range is linked to better health and longer life.

**Reference ranges (g/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–3.5  
- Functional Low: 3.6–3.9  
- **Optimal: 4.0–4.9**  
- Functional High: 5.0–5.1  
- Clinical High: 5.2 and above

**Clinical Low (below 3.5 g/dL):** This is called hypoalbuminemia. It is a warning sign for serious illness, longer hospital stays, and worse outcomes from surgery. Causes: advanced liver disease (low production), kidney disease (losing albumin in urine), severe malnutrition, malabsorption, chronic inflammation, severe burns, and rare genetic conditions. You may have swelling, fatigue, and poor healing. To optimize: work with a provider to treat the cause. Eat 1.2–1.5 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight. Focus on easy-to-digest proteins. Support your liver and gut.

**Functional Low (3.6–3.9 g/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means you are not eating enough protein, your gut is not absorbing food well, your liver is sluggish, or you have chronic inflammation. You may feel tired and recover slowly. To raise it: eat more protein (beans, fish, eggs, poultry, Greek yogurt, nuts, seeds). Make sure you eat enough calories. A provider can check your gut, liver, and inflammation.

**Optimal (4.0–4.9 g/dL):** This is the goal. It means your liver is working well, your nutrition is good, and you have low inflammation. Higher albumin in this range is linked to better health and longer life. To stay here: eat 0.8–1.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight. Eat a colorful plant-based diet with anti-inflammatory foods. Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (5.0–5.1 g/dL):** This is uncommon and usually harmless. It often comes from a very high protein intake or mild dehydration. It is usually a good sign. No changes needed. Make sure you drink enough water.

**Clinical High (5.2 g/dL and above):** This is rare. It is usually from severe dehydration or, less often, a very high protein intake. To address: drink more water. If you eat a lot of protein, make sure your kidneys are working well. Most often, no changes are needed.

## Globulin

**What is this test?** Globulins are a group of proteins made by your liver and immune system. They include antibodies (which fight infection), complement proteins, and transport proteins.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you about your immune function, inflammation, and liver health. Low globulins can mean a weak immune system. High globulins can mean chronic inflammation, infection, or autoimmune disease.

**Reference ranges (g/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–1.8  
- Functional Low: 1.81–2.0  
- **Optimal: 2.01–3.2**  
- Functional High: 3.21–3.7  
- Clinical High: 3.71 and above

**Clinical Low (below 1.8 g/dL):** This is rare. It can mean immune deficiency, advanced liver disease (low production), kidney disease (losing globulins in urine), severe malnutrition, or rare genetic disorders (agammaglobulinemia). You may get sick often. To optimize: work with a provider to find the cause. Eat enough protein. Support your liver and gut. A provider can check your immune function, gut, and liver.

**Functional Low (1.81–2.0 g/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can mean mild immune suppression, low protein intake, or sluggish liver function. You may get sick more easily. To raise it: eat enough protein. Support your gut (where 70–80% of your immune system lives). Add immune-supporting foods: zinc, vitamin D, vitamin C, glutathione (from broccoli sprouts, garlic, onions). A provider can check your gut, liver, and immune markers.

**Optimal (2.01–3.2 g/dL):** This is the goal. It means a balanced immune system — strong but not over-active. To stay here: eat a colorful plant-based diet rich in antioxidants, fiber, and immune-supporting nutrients (zinc, vitamin D, vitamin C). Sleep 7–9 hours. Manage stress. Exercise most days. Maintain a healthy gut.

**Functional High (3.21–3.7 g/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It often means your immune system is activated. Common causes: chronic inflammation, chronic infections (viral, dental, gut, sinus), autoimmune disease, and a high-protein or high-dairy diet. You may feel tired, have achy joints, brain fog, or get sick often. To optimize: address any hidden infections or inflammation. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet. Cut back on alcohol. Support your gut. Manage stress. A provider can check your inflammation, gut, and immune markers.

**Clinical High (3.71 g/dL and above):** This needs evaluation. Causes: chronic inflammation, chronic infections (HIV, hepatitis, TB, EBV), autoimmune disease (lupus, RA, sarcoidosis), liver disease, multiple myeloma, Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia, and other paraproteinemias. High globulins with symptoms (fatigue, weight loss, night sweats, bone pain) need urgent care. To address: work with a provider. They may order a protein electrophoresis (SPEP) and other tests. Treat the cause.

## Albumin/Globulin Ratio (A/G Ratio)

**What is this test?** The A/G ratio is your albumin divided by your globulin. It shows the balance between your two main protein groups. A higher ratio usually means better health.

**Why does it matter?** It is a simple way to see if your body favors health (high albumin) or inflammation (high globulin). A low ratio is a sign of chronic inflammation or infection. A high ratio is usually a sign of good health.

**Reference ranges (ratio, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–1.0  
- Functional Low: 1.1–1.4  
- **Optimal: 1.41–2.0**  
- Functional High: 2.01–2.5  
- Clinical High: 2.51 and above

**Clinical Low (below 1.0):** This is a serious warning sign. It usually means chronic inflammation, infection, autoimmune disease, liver problems, or a blood cancer. Causes: chronic viral infections (HIV, hepatitis, EBV), autoimmune disease (lupus, RA), multiple myeloma, liver cirrhosis, kidney disease, and chronic inflammation. You may feel tired, have joint pain, and be at higher risk of serious illness. To optimize: work with a provider to find the cause. Treat any infection, autoimmune disease, or cancer. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet.

**Functional Low (1.1–1.4):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means chronic low-grade inflammation, hidden infections (dental, gut, sinus), or mild liver dysfunction. You may feel tired, have brain fog, joint stiffness, or get sick often. To optimize: address any hidden infections or inflammation. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet. Support your gut and liver. Manage stress. A provider can check your inflammation, gut, liver, and immune markers.

**Optimal (1.41–2.0):** This is the goal. It means balanced proteins, low inflammation, healthy liver, and a well-regulated immune system. To stay here: keep up a whole-food, plant-based diet rich in antioxidants, fiber, and lean protein. Sleep 7–9 hours. Manage stress. Exercise most days. Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (2.01–2.5):** This is usually a great sign. It usually means high albumin (good nutrition and liver function) and low-normal globulins. It is a sign of robust health. If it is very high (\>2.5), it can sometimes mean low globulins (immune deficiency). If you get sick often, a provider can check your immune markers.

**Clinical High (2.51 and above):** This is rare. It can mean very low globulins (immune deficiency) or very high albumin. Causes: hypogammaglobulinemia, multiple myeloma (rare, as globulins are usually high), or genetic immune deficiencies. To address: work with a provider to check your immune markers and consider further tests.

## Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP)

**What is this test?** ALP is an enzyme found mostly in your liver, bones, intestines, and kidneys. It helps with bone growth and bile flow. Bile is a fluid your liver makes to help digest fat.

**Why does it matter?** High ALP can mean liver or bone disease. Low ALP can mean malnutrition, an underactive thyroid, or a rare genetic condition. ALP is naturally higher in kids and teens (during growth spurts) and during pregnancy.

**Reference ranges (U/L, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–34  
- Functional Low: 35–69  
- **Optimal: 70–115**  
- Functional High: 116–144  
- Clinical High: 145 and above

**Clinical Low (below 35 U/L):** This is on the low end. It can mean an underactive thyroid, severe malnutrition, celiac disease, pernicious anemia, low B12 or folate, low zinc, or a rare genetic disorder (hypophosphatasia). You may feel tired, have joint stiffness, and weak bones. To optimize: work with a provider. Make sure you get enough protein, B12, folate, zinc, and magnesium. Support your thyroid (iodine, selenium, stress control).

**Functional Low (35–69 U/L):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can mean a slightly underactive thyroid, mild malnutrition, malabsorption, or low bone turnover. You may feel tired, have joint stiffness, or have weaker bones. To optimize: make sure you get enough protein, B12, folate, zinc, magnesium, and vitamin D. Do weight-bearing exercise for bone health. A provider can check your thyroid, gut, and nutrients.

**Optimal (70–115 U/L):** This is the goal. It means your liver and bones are working well. To stay here: eat a whole-food diet with protein, calcium, magnesium, vitamin D, and zinc. Do weight-bearing exercise. Limit alcohol. Avoid toxins that hurt the liver. Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (116–144 U/L):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean mild liver stress, increased bone turnover, or healing. Common causes: fatty liver disease, mild bile flow problems, healing bone fracture, intense exercise, certain medicines, low vitamin D (which can cause high parathyroid hormone), or rarely Paget disease (a bone disorder). To optimize: address the cause. Cut back on alcohol, processed foods, and sugar. Eat liver-friendly foods (leafy greens, beets, cruciferous vegetables, coffee). A provider can check your liver and bones.

**Clinical High (145 U/L and above):** This needs evaluation. Causes: bile duct problems (blockage, primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis), hepatitis, cirrhosis, bone disorders (Paget disease, osteomalacia, healing fractures, bone tumors), certain cancers (liver, bone, spread from other cancers), hyperparathyroidism, and certain medicines. To address: work with a provider. They will order liver imaging, GGT, 5'-nucleotidase, and possibly bone-specific ALP. Treat the cause. Cut out alcohol.

## Testosterone, Total, Male

**What is this test?** This measures the total amount of testosterone in a man's blood. Testosterone is the main male sex hormone. It is made mostly in the testicles. It helps with muscle, bone, sex drive, energy, mood, and thinking.

**Why does it matter?** Testosterone affects almost every part of a man's health. Low testosterone is linked to fatigue, low sex drive, erectile dysfunction, muscle loss, weight gain, sadness, brain fog, weak bones, and higher heart risk. High testosterone can be from supplements or rare tumors.

**Reference ranges (ng/dL, males only):**

- Clinical Low: 0–249  
- Functional Low: 250–391  
- **Optimal: 392–800**  
- Functional High: 801–825  
- Clinical High: 826 and above

**Clinical Low (below 250 ng/dL):** This is called hypogonadism. It causes fatigue, low sex drive, erectile dysfunction, muscle loss, weight gain, sadness, brain fog, weak bones, and higher heart risk. Causes: testicle problems (genetic, injury, infection, radiation), pituitary gland problems, medicines (opioids, anabolic steroids, some antidepressants), obesity, diabetes, chronic stress, poor sleep, alcohol abuse, environmental toxins, and low zinc or vitamin D. To raise it: work with a provider. Lifestyle changes can help a lot — weight loss, strength training, better sleep, less stress, less alcohol, and a whole-food diet. Make sure you get enough zinc, vitamin D, and magnesium. Some men need testosterone replacement therapy (TRT).

**Functional Low (250–391 ng/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. Many men in this range feel symptoms of low testosterone (fatigue, low sex drive, brain fog, mild sadness) even though they are told they are "normal." Causes: poor diet, no exercise, chronic stress, poor sleep, mild obesity, insulin resistance, slightly underactive thyroid, environmental toxins (BPA, phthalates), and some medicines. To raise it: focus on strength training (compound lifts like squats, deadlifts, presses, 3–4 times a week). Sleep 7–9 hours. Manage stress. Eat a whole-food diet with healthy fats, zinc, vitamin D, and magnesium. Lose extra belly fat (belly fat turns testosterone into estrogen). A provider can check your thyroid, cortisol, insulin, and nutrients.

**Optimal (392–800 ng/dL):** This is the goal. It means your testosterone is supporting energy, sex drive, muscle, bone, mood, and thinking well. Men in this range usually strength train, eat a whole-food diet with healthy fats, sleep well, and manage stress. To stay here: keep up strength training, sleep, diet, and stress control. Limit alcohol. Avoid endocrine disruptors (BPA in plastics, phthalates in personal care products). Check free testosterone, SHBG, estradiol, and PSA periodically.

**Functional High (801–825 ng/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It usually means your body is making plenty of testosterone. It can also be from testosterone therapy, anabolic steroids, or gene changes. In most cases, this is a good sign. If on testosterone therapy and levels are very high, work with a provider to adjust your dose. Check your hematocrit (high testosterone can raise it), estradiol, and PSA.

**Clinical High (826 ng/dL and above):** This is rare unless you are on testosterone therapy or using anabolic steroids. Very high testosterone can cause polycythemia (too many red blood cells), acne, mood swings, water retention, infertility, breast enlargement, and heart strain. Causes: testosterone therapy, anabolic steroids, rare tumors (testicular, adrenal), and congenital adrenal hyperplasia. To address: work with a provider. If on testosterone therapy, adjust your dose. If not, check for tumors. Stop any illegal steroid use. Check your hematocrit, estradiol, and PSA.

## Testosterone, Total, Female

**What is this test?** This measures the total amount of testosterone in a woman's blood. Women make small amounts of testosterone, mostly in the ovaries and adrenal glands. It plays a role in sex drive, bone health, muscle, mood, and energy.

**Why does it matter?** Both low and high testosterone matter in women. Low testosterone can cause low sex drive, fatigue, weak bones, and muscle loss. High testosterone can cause acne, extra hair growth, hair loss on the scalp, and irregular periods (often a sign of PCOS — polycystic ovary syndrome).

- Functional Low: 14–27  
- **Optimal: 28–56**  
- Functional High: 57–70  
- Clinical High: 71 and above

**Clinical Low (below 14 ng/dL):** This can cause low sex drive, fatigue, weak bones, muscle loss, sad mood, and brain fog. Causes: underactive ovaries (often from aging, surgery, or chemotherapy), adrenal insufficiency, certain medicines (oral contraceptives, anti-androgens, opioids), pituitary problems, chronic illness, and chronic stress. To raise it: work with a provider. Lifestyle changes can help — strength training, stress control, good sleep, a whole-food diet with healthy fats, and addressing the underlying cause. Some women benefit from low-dose testosterone therapy under provider guidance.

**Functional Low (14–27 ng/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. Many women in this range notice low sex drive, fatigue, or trouble building muscle even when other hormones look "normal." Causes: aging, oral contraceptives, chronic stress, poor sleep, underactive thyroid, adrenal fatigue, and certain medicines. Causes: PCOS (most common), insulin resistance, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, adrenal or ovarian tumors (rare), and certain medicines (anabolic steroids, DHEA). To address: work with a provider. Full workup typically includes DHEA-S, 17-hydroxyprogesterone, pelvic ultrasound, and insulin testing. Treatment depends on the cause — lifestyle changes, medicines (like spironolactone), or treatment of underlying conditions.

## Testosterone, Free, Male

**What is this test?** Free testosterone is the small amount of testosterone (1–3%) that is not attached to proteins in your blood. This is the testosterone that your body can actually use. The rest is "bound" to proteins (mainly SHBG and albumin) and is not active.

**Why does it matter?** Free testosterone is often a better marker of how your body is doing than total testosterone — especially if your SHBG is high or low. It tells you the testosterone that is actually available to your cells.

**Reference ranges (pg/mL, males only):**

- Clinical Low: 0–6.6  
- Functional Low: 6.61–11.99  
- **Optimal: 12.0–21.51**  
- Functional High: 21.52–26.49  
- Clinical High: 26.5 and above

**Clinical Low (below 6.6 pg/mL):** This is a sign of true testosterone deficiency. It causes the same symptoms as low total testosterone. It is more diagnostic if your total testosterone looks normal but your free testosterone is low — this often means your SHBG is too high (common in older men, people with underactive thyroid, liver disease, or on certain medicines). To raise it: work with a provider. Lifestyle changes (strength training, sleep, weight loss, whole-food diet, stress control) help. Address the underlying cause (thyroid, liver, SHBG). Some men need testosterone therapy.

**Functional Low (6.61–11.99 pg/mL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can be from high SHBG (most common in older men), insulin resistance, obesity, or low total testosterone. You may feel tired, have low sex drive, brain fog, or trouble building muscle. To raise it: lower SHBG if it is high (improve insulin sensitivity, treat thyroid, cut alcohol). Raise total testosterone (strength training, weight loss, sleep). Address the underlying causes. A provider can check your SHBG, insulin, thyroid, and liver.

**Optimal (12.0–21.51 pg/mL):** This is the goal. It means your body has plenty of usable testosterone. Men in this range usually have balanced total testosterone and SHBG, healthy body weight, and good habits. To stay here: keep up strength training, sleep, diet, and stress control. Limit alcohol. Avoid endocrine disruptors. Periodically check total testosterone, SHBG, and estradiol.

**Functional High (21.52–26.49 pg/mL):** This is usually a great sign of strong androgen function. It can also be from testosterone therapy or low SHBG (often from insulin resistance or obesity). In a healthy context, this is excellent. If it is very high and you are on therapy, work with a provider to adjust. Watch for signs of excess (acne, mood swings, polycythemia).

**Clinical High (26.5 pg/mL and above):** This is rare unless on testosterone therapy or using anabolic steroids. It can cause acne, mood swings, aggression, polycythemia, infertility, and heart strain. Very high levels may signal a rare tumor. To address: work with a provider. Adjust therapy if needed. Stop any illegal steroid use. Check for tumors if it is persistent. Check hematocrit, estradiol, and PSA.

- Clinical High: 70.01 and above

**Clinical Low (below 14 ng/dL):** This can cause low sex drive, fatigue, weak bones, muscle loss, sad mood, and brain fog. Causes: underactive ovaries, adrenal insufficiency, oral contraceptives, certain medicines, and chronic illness. To raise it: work with a provider. Lifestyle changes (strength training, sleep, whole-food diet with healthy fats) and addressing the underlying cause are foundational.

**Functional Low (14–27.99 ng/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. Many women in this range notice low sex drive, fatigue, or trouble building muscle. Causes: aging, oral contraceptives, chronic stress, poor sleep, underactive thyroid, and certain medicines. To raise it: focus on strength training, sleep 7–9 hours, manage stress, and eat a whole-food diet with healthy fats.

**Optimal (28–56 ng/dL):** This is the goal. It means your body has plenty of usable testosterone. To stay here: keep up strength training, sleep, a whole-food diet with healthy fats, and stress control. Periodically check your hormones (especially during perimenopause and menopause).

**Functional High (56.01–70 ng/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can cause mild signs of too much androgen. Causes: PCOS, insulin resistance, adrenal disorders, and certain medicines. To optimize: work with a provider. Address insulin resistance through diet, exercise, and weight loss if needed. Consider inositol.

**Clinical High (70.01 ng/dL and above):** This can cause significant acne, hirsutism, male-pattern hair loss, deep voice, irregular periods, and infertility. Causes: PCOS (most common), insulin resistance, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, adrenal or ovarian tumors (rare), and certain medicines. To address: work with a provider. Full workup typically includes DHEA-S, 17-hydroxyprogesterone, pelvic ultrasound, and insulin testing.

## PSA, Total (Prostate-Specific Antigen, Total)

**What is this test?** PSA is a protein made by your prostate gland (a small gland in men that helps make semen). Small amounts of PSA can also be made by other tissues. Some PSA leaks into your blood, where it can be measured.

**Why does it matter?** PSA is used to screen for prostate cancer, monitor prostate cancer treatment, and check prostate health. It can also be high with non-cancer conditions like an enlarged prostate (BPH), prostate infection, urinary tract infection, recent ejaculation, or even bike riding.

**Reference ranges (ng/mL, males only):**

- **Optimal: 0–4.0**  
- Functional High: 4.01–8.0  
- Clinical High: 8.01 and above

Note: This test does not have a Clinical Low or Functional Low category in the OHS ranges.

**Optimal (0–4.0 ng/mL):** This is the goal. It means a healthy prostate. To stay here: keep up a prostate-healthy diet — lycopene (found in cooked tomatoes, watermelon), cruciferous vegetables (broccoli, cauliflower, kale), omega-3 fats, green tea, berries, and soy foods. Exercise, maintain a healthy weight, and manage stress. Some men benefit from saw palmetto, beta-sitosterol, or zinc. Periodically monitor, especially after age 50\.

**Functional High (4.01–8.0 ng/mL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It is concerning, especially in younger men or those with a family history. Causes: BPH (common with aging), prostatitis (prostate inflammation), urinary tract infection, recent ejaculation, bike riding, recent prostate exam, or rarely prostate cancer. Even mild rises need follow-up. To address: work with a provider. They may do a digital rectal exam, repeat the PSA, check free PSA, and order imaging or a biopsy. Lifestyle: eat a prostate-healthy diet, exercise, lose extra weight, manage stress. Avoid ejaculation and bike riding for 48 hours before the test.

**Clinical High (8.01 ng/mL and above):** This needs prompt evaluation by a urologist (a doctor who specializes in the urinary tract and male reproductive organs). Causes: BPH, prostatitis, UTI, prostate cancer, recent prostate manipulation, or rarely other conditions. The risk of prostate cancer goes up with higher PSA, faster PSA velocity (how fast the PSA rises over time), and lower free PSA percentage. Workup may include a digital rectal exam, repeat PSA, free PSA, PCA3 or 4Kscore, MRI, and biopsy. To address: follow your provider's recommendations. Eat a prostate-healthy diet, exercise, lose weight, manage stress, and consider supplements (saw palmetto, beta-sitosterol, zinc, lycopene). Quit smoking and limit alcohol.

## Estrogens, Total

**What is this test?** Total estrogens measures all the forms of estrogen in your blood. The main forms are estradiol (the strongest), estrone, and estriol. Estrogens are the main female sex hormones, but men also have small amounts. They are made mostly in the ovaries (women), testicles (men), adrenal glands, and fat tissue.

**Why does it matter?** In women, estrogens are key for periods, pregnancy, bone health, heart health, thinking, and skin. In men, estrogens are needed for bone, brain, and sex drive. Too much or too little can cause problems.

**Reference ranges (pg/mL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–45.49  
- Functional Low: 45.5–94.74  
- **Optimal: 94.75–173.25**  
- Functional High: 173.26–212.5  
- Clinical High: 212.51 and above

**Clinical Low (below 45.5 pg/mL):** In women, very low estrogens happen in menopause, premature ovarian failure (ovaries stop working early), hypothalamic amenorrhea (missing periods from low body fat, over-exercising, or stress), postpartum (after birth), and breastfeeding. Symptoms: hot flashes, vaginal dryness, mood swings, poor sleep, low sex drive, bone loss, and brain fog. In men, low estrogens are rare but can cause low sex drive, weak bones, and metabolic issues. To raise it: address the cause. Women: eat phytoestrogen-rich foods (flax, soy), exercise moderately, gain weight if underweight, manage stress, and consider hormone therapy. Men: check aromatase (the enzyme that turns testosterone into estrogen) and avoid excess aromatase inhibitors.

**Functional Low (45.5–94.74 pg/mL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. In cycling women, this often means the early follicular phase (start of cycle), the luteal phase drop, perimenopause, or lifestyle factors (under-eating, over-exercising, chronic stress). You may have irregular periods, mood swings, hot flashes, low sex drive, and bone loss. To raise it: eat enough calories, healthy fats, and phytoestrogens (flax, soy, lentils). Cut back on intense exercise. Manage stress. A provider can check your adrenal, thyroid, and nutrient status.

**Optimal (94.75–173.25 pg/mL):** This is the goal. The best level depends on sex, age, and cycle phase. In cycling women, estradiol should peak mid-cycle and stay balanced in the luteal phase. In postmenopausal women, lower but not absent estrogens are best. In men, mid-range estradiol is best for bone, brain, and heart health. To stay here: keep up a whole-food, plant-based diet rich in fiber, flax, soy, and cruciferous vegetables. Stay at a healthy weight. Exercise moderately. Manage stress. Support your liver and gut for healthy estrogen breakdown.

**Functional High (173.26–212.5 pg/mL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. In cycling women, this often means estrogen dominance (too much estrogen compared to progesterone). Symptoms: heavy periods, breast tenderness, mood swings, weight gain, fibroids, and endometriosis. In men, it often means too much aromatase activity (turning testosterone into estrogen), seen in obesity, insulin resistance, and aging. To improve: support liver detox (B vitamins, magnesium, cruciferous vegetables). Eat more fiber (helps remove estrogens). Cut back on alcohol. Lose extra body fat. Exercise regularly. A provider can check your estrogen metabolism (DUTCH test).

**Clinical High (212.51 pg/mL and above):** Very high estrogens need evaluation. In women, causes: estrogen-secreting tumors (rare), estrogen therapy, liver disease (poor estrogen breakdown), obesity, and certain medicines. In men, high estradiol raises the risk of breast enlargement, infertility, heart disease, and stroke. Causes: obesity (high aromatase), liver disease, alcohol, certain medicines, and rare tumors. To address: work with a provider. For women, check estrogen metabolism and rule out tumors. For men, address obesity and aromatase activity through weight loss, exercise, and possibly aromatase inhibitors. Increase fiber, cut alcohol, support liver detox, and eat cruciferous vegetables.

## Sex Hormone Binding Globulin (SHBG)

**What is this test?** SHBG is a protein made by your liver. It attaches to sex hormones (testosterone and estrogen) and controls how much of them are "free" and able to work. When SHBG is high, less testosterone and estrogen are free. When SHBG is low, more is free.

**Why does it matter?** SHBG is the gatekeeper of your sex hormones. It is affected by insulin, thyroid, liver, body fat, age, and sex. It helps explain why two people with the same total testosterone can feel very differently.

**Reference ranges (nmol/L, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–21  
- Functional Low: 22–45  
- **Optimal: 46–55**  
- Functional High: 56–77  
- Clinical High: 78 and above

**Clinical Low (below 22 nmol/L):** Low SHBG is often from insulin resistance, obesity, type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, an underactive thyroid, Cushing syndrome (a condition where the body makes too much cortisol), fatty liver, or certain medicines (androgens, steroids). In men, low SHBG can make free testosterone look high for a while, but it also raises the risk of heart disease and diabetes. In women, low SHBG raises free androgens and can cause PCOS, acne, extra hair growth, and hair loss. To raise it: address the cause (insulin resistance, thyroid, liver, weight). Lose weight, exercise, eat a low-glycemic diet (one that does not spike blood sugar), and cut refined carbs. A provider can check fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, thyroid, and liver.

**Functional Low (22–45 nmol/L):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early insulin resistance, mild obesity, a slightly underactive thyroid, or poor liver function. In men, this can raise free testosterone for a while but eventually leads to low androgens and higher heart risk. In women, this often causes signs of too much androgen (acne, extra hair growth, hair loss, irregular cycles). To raise it: improve insulin sensitivity with a whole-food, low-glycemic diet, exercise, and weight loss if needed. Support your thyroid (selenium, iodine, zinc) and liver (cruciferous vegetables, B vitamins, milk thistle). A provider can do further testing.

**Optimal (46–55 nmol/L):** This is the goal. It means balanced hormone regulation, healthy liver, and good insulin sensitivity. In men, this range keeps free testosterone in a healthy range. In women, this range keeps estrogen and testosterone balanced. To stay here: keep up a high-fiber, low-glycemic diet, exercise, and stress control. Limit alcohol (lowers SHBG). Periodically re-check total and free testosterone, estradiol, and fasting insulin.

**Functional High (56–77 nmol/L):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. In men, high SHBG lowers free testosterone, which can cause low energy, low sex drive, and trouble building muscle. Causes: overactive thyroid, aging, liver disease, low body fat, low insulin (from over-exercising or fasting), high estrogen, and certain medicines. In women, high SHBG lowers free estradiol, which can cause low sex drive, vaginal dryness, and bone loss. To lower SHBG: improve insulin sensitivity a bit (eat a bit more carbs, less fasting if overdoing it), treat thyroid, support liver, gain a little body fat if too lean, and consider boron (3–6 mg a day).

**Clinical High (78 nmol/L and above):** This is rare. It can mean significant liver disease, overactive thyroid, severe malnutrition, or rare gene changes. In men, very high SHBG causes severe testosterone deficiency. In women, very high SHBG can cause severe estrogen deficiency. To address: work with a provider. Treat the underlying liver or thyroid disease. Men may need testosterone therapy. Women may need estrogen therapy if deficient. Lifestyle: moderate carb intake, healthy body fat, treat thyroid, and support the liver.

## TSH (Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone)

**What is this test?** TSH is a hormone made by your pituitary gland (a small gland at the base of your brain). It tells your thyroid gland (in your neck) to make thyroid hormones (T4 and T3). Thyroid hormones control your energy, metabolism, body temperature, and many other body functions.

**Why does it matter?** TSH is the best single screening test for thyroid problems. High TSH usually means an underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism). Low TSH usually means an overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism). Many people have symptoms even within the lab range, so functional medicine uses tighter ranges.

**Reference ranges (mIU/L, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–0.4  
- Functional Low: 0.41–0.99  
- **Optimal: 1.0–3.5**  
- Functional High: 3.51–4.5  
- Clinical High: 4.51 and above

**Clinical Low (below 0.41 mIU/L):** Very low TSH means hyperthyroidism (overactive thyroid). It can cause anxiety, fast heart rate, weight loss, feeling hot, shaking, insomnia, diarrhea, and bulging eyes (in Graves disease, an autoimmune thyroid disorder). Causes: Graves disease, toxic multinodular goiter (an enlarged thyroid with hot nodules), toxic adenoma (a single hot nodule), thyroiditis (inflammation of the thyroid, early phase), too much thyroid medicine, too much iodine, or rarely pituitary tumors. To address: work with a provider. Treatment may include antithyroid drugs (methimazole, PTU), radioactive iodine, or surgery. Cut back on iodine and stimulants. Manage stress. Add calming nutrients (magnesium, selenium, L-theanine).

**Functional Low (0.41–0.99 mIU/L):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can be subclinical hyperthyroidism (low TSH with normal thyroid hormones) or early hyperthyroidism. Symptoms: anxiety, insomnia, heart palpitations, and weight loss. Causes: early Graves disease, too much thyroid medicine, too much iodine or selenium, and chronic stress. To optimize: work with a provider. Cut back on iodine and stimulants. Manage stress. Support your HPA axis (the system that manages stress hormones). A provider can check free T3, free T4, thyroid antibodies, and reverse T3.

**Optimal (1.0–3.5 mIU/L):** This is the goal. It means your thyroid is working well. To stay here: make sure you get enough iodine (seaweed, seafood, iodized salt), selenium (Brazil nuts, fish), zinc, iron, vitamin D, and tyrosine (an amino acid your body uses to make thyroid hormones). Manage stress. Sleep 7–9 hours. Avoid endocrine disruptors. A functional medicine provider can also check free T3, free T4, reverse T3, and thyroid antibodies for a fuller picture.

**Functional High (3.51–4.5 mIU/L):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean subclinical hypothyroidism (high TSH with normal thyroid hormones). Symptoms: fatigue, weight gain, brain fog, feeling cold, constipation, and mild sadness. Common causes: Hashimoto's thyroiditis (an autoimmune disease that attacks the thyroid — the most common cause), iodine deficiency, low selenium, low iron, chronic stress, poor T4-to-T3 conversion, and certain medicines. To optimize: address the cause. Get enough iodine, selenium, zinc, iron, and vitamin D. Manage stress. Sleep well. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet. A provider can check free T3, free T4, thyroid antibodies, and reverse T3.

**Clinical High (4.51 mIU/L and above):** High TSH means hypothyroidism (underactive thyroid). It can be subclinical (high TSH, normal T4) or overt (high TSH, low T4). Symptoms: fatigue, weight gain, feeling cold, constipation, brain fog, sadness, hair loss, dry skin, and irregular periods. Causes: Hashimoto's thyroiditis (most common in iodine-sufficient areas), iodine deficiency, thyroid surgery, radioactive iodine, certain medicines (lithium, amiodarone), or rarely pituitary problems. To address: work with a provider. Treatment may include thyroid hormone replacement (levothyroxine, liothyronine, or natural desiccated thyroid). Lifestyle: get enough iodine, selenium, zinc, iron, and vitamin D. Manage stress. Sleep well. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet. A functional medicine provider can check the full thyroid panel, antibodies, and gut health.

## Hemoglobin

**What is this test?** Hemoglobin is the protein in your red blood cells that carries oxygen from your lungs to the rest of your body. It contains iron, which is what makes your blood red.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you how well your blood can carry oxygen. Low hemoglobin (anemia) means your tissues are not getting enough oxygen. You feel tired, weak, short of breath, and foggy. High hemoglobin can be from dehydration, training at altitude, or rare blood disorders.

**Reference ranges (g/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–12.9  
- Functional Low: 13.0–14.5  
- **Optimal: 14.6–16.6**  
- Functional High: 16.7–17.1  
- Clinical High: 17.2 and above

**Clinical Low (below 13.0 g/dL):** This is called anemia. Your body is not getting enough oxygen. You feel tired, weak, short of breath, foggy, and look pale. Causes: iron deficiency (most common), low B12, low folate, chronic blood loss (gut, menstrual), kidney disease, chronic inflammation, bone marrow problems, and underactive thyroid. To address: work with a provider. Treatment depends on the cause — iron (iron bisglycinate or ferrous sulfate), B12 (especially for older adults and vegans), folate, or treatment of the underlying condition. Eat iron-rich foods (grass-fed red meat, organ meats, dark leafy greens, lentils, fortified cereals) with vitamin C for better absorption.

**Functional Low (13.0–14.5 g/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early iron deficiency, low B12 or folate, chronic inflammation, or a slightly underactive thyroid. You may feel tired, have brain fog, hair loss, or trouble exercising. To raise it: find and treat the cause. Eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C. Make sure you get enough B12 (animal products or supplements for vegans) and folate (leafy greens, beans, avocado). A provider can check ferritin, B12, folate, thyroid, and gut health.

**Optimal (14.6–16.6 g/dL):** This is the goal. It means your blood is carrying oxygen well. To stay here: keep up a nutrient-dense diet with iron, B12, folate, and vitamin C. Stay hydrated. Periodically re-check, especially for women with heavy periods.

**Functional High (16.7–17.1 g/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can be from dehydration, living at high altitude, intense endurance training, or too much iron. Very high hemoglobin can make your blood thick and raise the risk of clots. To optimize: drink more water. Cut back on iron supplements if you take them. If you are very high and not athletic or dehydrated, a provider can check for sleep apnea or rare blood disorders.

**Clinical High (17.2 g/dL and above):** Very high hemoglobin needs evaluation. Causes: dehydration, polycythemia vera (a blood cancer where the body makes too many red blood cells), secondary polycythemia (from smoking, COPD, sleep apnea, living at altitude, congenital heart disease), testosterone therapy, or rare tumors that make erythropoietin (a hormone that tells your body to make red blood cells). To address: work with a provider. Stop smoking. Treat sleep apnea. Adjust testosterone therapy if applicable. Phlebotomy (blood removal) may be needed for polycythemia vera. Drink more water.

## Hematocrit

**What is this test?** Hematocrit is the percentage of your blood that is made of red blood cells. The rest is plasma (the liquid part of blood). It is closely tied to hemoglobin.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you the same thing as hemoglobin — how well your blood carries oxygen. Low hematocrit means anemia. High hematocrit means thick blood, which can raise the risk of clots.

**Reference ranges (%, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–35.0  
- Functional Low: 35.01–37.4  
- **Optimal: 37.41–44.0**  
- Functional High: 44.01–44.99  
- Clinical High: 45.0 and above

**Clinical Low (below 35.0%):** This is anemia. It means your blood cannot carry enough oxygen. You feel tired, weak, short of breath, and foggy. Causes: iron deficiency, low B12, low folate, chronic blood loss, kidney disease, chronic inflammation, bone marrow problems, and underactive thyroid. To address: work with a provider. Treatment depends on the cause. Eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C.

**Functional Low (35.01–37.4%):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early iron deficiency, low B12 or folate, chronic inflammation, or mild anemia. You may feel tired or have trouble exercising. To raise it: find and treat the cause. Eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C. Make sure you get enough B12 and folate. A provider can check ferritin, B12, folate, thyroid, and gut health.

**Optimal (37.41–44.0%):** This is the goal. It means your blood is carrying oxygen well and your bone marrow is working properly. To stay here: keep up a nutrient-dense diet with iron, B12, folate, and vitamin C. Stay hydrated. Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (44.01–44.99%):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can be from dehydration, endurance training, high altitude, or too much iron. Very high hematocrit can make blood thick and raise clot risk. To optimize: drink more water. Cut back on iron supplements if needed. If endurance-trained, this is often a normal adaptation.

**Clinical High (45.0% and above):** Very high hematocrit needs evaluation. Causes: polycythemia vera, secondary polycythemia (smoking, COPD, sleep apnea, altitude, congenital heart disease), dehydration, and testosterone therapy. Symptoms: headache, dizziness, blurry vision, and higher clot risk. To address: work with a provider. Stop smoking. Treat sleep apnea. Adjust testosterone therapy. Phlebotomy may be needed. Drink more water.

## White Blood Cell Count (WBC)

**What is this test?** WBC is the total number of white blood cells in your blood. White blood cells are your immune system's soldiers. They fight infection, clean up damaged cells, and remember past invaders. There are 5 main types: neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, and basophils.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you how active your immune system is. Low WBC means your immune system may be weak. High WBC can mean infection, inflammation, or stress. Higher WBC within the normal range is linked to more inflammation and higher heart risk.

**Reference ranges (K/μL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–3.3  
- Functional Low: 3.4–4.4  
- **Optimal: 4.5–8.5**  
- Functional High: 8.6–10.8  
- Clinical High: 10.9 and above

**Clinical Low (below 3.4 K/μL):** This is called leukopenia. Your immune system is weak. You get sick easily. Causes: viral infections, autoimmune disease (lupus, RA), bone marrow disorders, certain medicines (chemotherapy, clozapine, methimazole), severe infections (sepsis, HIV), low B12, folate, copper, or zinc, and an overactive spleen. To address: work with a provider. Support your immune system with a nutrient-dense diet, sleep, stress control, and key nutrients (zinc, vitamin D, vitamin C, B12, folate).

**Functional Low (3.4–4.4 K/μL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can be from chronic viral infections (EBV, CMV, COVID), chronic stress, low nutrients, or mild bone marrow suppression. You may get sick more easily, heal slowly, or feel tired. To optimize: address any chronic viral infections. Support your immune system with a nutrient-dense diet, sleep, and stress control. Make sure you get enough zinc, vitamin D, vitamin C, B12, and folate. A provider can check viral load, gut, and nutrients.

**Optimal (4.5–8.5 K/μL):** This is the goal. It means a balanced immune system with low chronic inflammation and good resilience. To stay here: keep up a colorful plant-based diet rich in antioxidants, exercise, sleep 7–9 hours, manage stress, avoid smoking, limit alcohol, and maintain a healthy gut.

**Functional High (8.6–10.8 K/μL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It often means low-grade chronic inflammation, hidden infection, smoking, belly fat, stress, intense exercise, or chronic allergen exposure. Higher WBC in this range is linked to higher heart risk. To optimize: address any inflammation or infection. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet (vegetables, berries, omega-3 fats). Quit smoking. Lose extra belly fat. Exercise moderately. A provider can check inflammation, gut, teeth, and sinuses.

**Clinical High (10.9 K/μL and above):** This means significant immune activation. Causes: bacterial or viral infection, inflammation (autoimmune disease, IBD, vasculitis — inflammation of blood vessels), tissue damage (surgery, trauma, burns), stress response, smoking, certain medicines (steroids, lithium, beta-agonists — medicines that open the airways), and blood cancers (leukemia, lymphoma). To address: work with a provider. They may do a differential, peripheral smear (looking at blood cells under a microscope), and possibly bone marrow biopsy. Treat the cause.

## Red Blood Cell Count (RBC)

**What is this test?** RBC is the number of red blood cells you have per volume of blood. It is closely tied to hemoglobin and hematocrit.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you how well your blood can carry oxygen. Low RBC is a form of anemia. High RBC can be from dehydration, training, or rare blood disorders.

**Reference ranges (M/μL, both sexes):**

**Clinical Low (below 3.77 M/μL):** This is anemia. Your body is not getting enough oxygen. Causes: iron deficiency, low B12, low folate, chronic blood loss, kidney disease, chronic inflammation, bone marrow disorders, and underactive thyroid. To address: work with a provider. Treatment depends on the cause. Eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C.

**Functional Low (3.77–4.09 M/μL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early iron deficiency, low B12 or folate, chronic inflammation, or mild anemia. You may feel tired or have trouble exercising. To raise it: find and treat the cause. Eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C. A provider can check ferritin, B12, folate, thyroid, and gut health.

To stay here: keep up a nutrient-dense diet with iron, B12, folate, and vitamin C. Stay hydrated. Periodically re-check, especially for menstruating women or men on testosterone therapy.

## MCV (Mean Corpuscular Volume)

**What is this test?** MCV measures the average size of your red blood cells. Small red blood cells (low MCV) and large red blood cells (high MCV) can both be signs of problems.

**Why does it matter?** It helps your provider figure out what kind of anemia (or other problem) you may have. Low MCV usually means iron deficiency. High MCV usually means B12 or folate deficiency, or other issues.

**Reference ranges (fL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–79.9  
- Functional Low: 80.0–83.9  
- **Optimal: 84.0–95.0**  
- Functional High: 95.1–100.0  
- Clinical High: 100.1 and above

**Clinical Low (below 80.0 fL):** This means microcytic anemia — your red blood cells are too small. It impairs oxygen delivery and causes fatigue, brain fog, and trouble exercising. Causes: iron deficiency (most common), thalassemia (a genetic blood disorder), sideroblastic anemia (a rare disorder where the body cannot use iron properly), anemia of chronic disease, lead poisoning, and low copper. To address: work with a provider. Take iron (iron bisglycinate or ferrous sulfate) with vitamin C. Eat iron-rich foods. A provider can check ferritin, TIBC, and gut health.

**Functional Low (80.0–83.9 fL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early iron deficiency, mild thalassemia trait, or low copper. You may feel tired or have trouble exercising. To raise it: eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C. Consider iron supplements if ferritin is low. Make sure you get enough copper (shellfish, nuts, seeds, dark chocolate). A provider can check ferritin, iron studies, and hemoglobin electrophoresis (a test for thalassemia).

**Optimal (84.0–95.0 fL):** This is the goal. It means your red blood cells are the right size, and your iron, B12, folate, and thyroid are good. To stay here: keep up a nutrient-dense diet with iron, B12, folate, and vitamin C. Avoid too much alcohol. Keep a healthy gut. Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (95.1–100.0 fL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean early B12 or folate deficiency, underactive thyroid, alcohol use, certain medicines (methotrexate, hydroxyurea), liver disease, or high red blood cell turnover. You may feel tired, foggy, or have memory issues. To optimize: make sure you get enough B12 (animal products or supplements for vegans), folate (leafy greens, beans, avocado), and thyroid support. Cut back on alcohol. A provider can check B12, folate, methylmalonic acid, homocysteine, and thyroid.

**Clinical High (100.1 fL and above):** This is macrocytic anemia — your red blood cells are too big. Causes: B12 deficiency (most common), folate deficiency, underactive thyroid, liver disease, alcoholism, certain medicines (methotrexate, hydroxyurea, zidovudine, chemo), myelodysplastic syndrome (a bone marrow disorder), and rare genetic conditions. Symptoms: fatigue, brain fog, memory loss, neuropathy (tingling/numbness in B12 deficiency), and glossitis (swollen, smooth tongue). To address: work with a provider. Take B12 (injections for severe deficiency, sublingual or oral for milder). Take folate. Address the cause (thyroid, alcohol, medicines).

## MCH (Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin)

**What is this test?** MCH measures the average amount of hemoglobin in each red blood cell. It is closely tied to MCV.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you how much hemoglobin is in each cell. Low MCH means hypochromic anemia (cells are pale). It usually means iron deficiency.

**Reference ranges (pg, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–27.0  
- Functional Low: 27.1–27.9  
- **Optimal: 28.0–32.0**  
- Functional High: 32.1–33.0  
- Clinical High: 33.1 and above

**Clinical Low (below 27.0 pg):** This means hypochromic anemia — your red blood cells do not have enough hemoglobin. Causes: iron deficiency (most common), thalassemia, sideroblastic anemia, anemia of chronic disease, and lead poisoning. To address: work with a provider. Take iron with vitamin C. Eat iron-rich foods. A provider can check ferritin, iron studies, and gut health.

**Functional Low (27.1–27.9 pg):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early iron deficiency or mild thalassemia trait. To raise it: eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C. Consider iron supplements if ferritin is low. Check for thalassemia if it stays low. A provider can check ferritin, iron studies, and hemoglobin electrophoresis.

**Optimal (28.0–32.0 pg):** This is the goal. It means your red blood cells have the right amount of hemoglobin. To stay here: keep up a nutrient-dense diet with iron, B12, folate, and vitamin C. Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (32.1–33.0 pg):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean high iron, B12 or folate excess, macrocytosis, or high red blood cell turnover. It is usually not a problem on its own. To optimize: if you take iron supplements, watch your dose. Make sure your nutrition is balanced. If it stays high, check B12, folate, and reticulocyte count (a measure of new red blood cells).

**Clinical High (33.1 pg and above):** This is uncommon and usually happens with macrocytic anemia from B12 or folate deficiency, underactive thyroid, liver disease, or alcoholism. To address: work with a provider. Treat the cause (B12, folate, thyroid, alcohol, liver).

## MCHC (Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin Concentration)

**What is this test?** MCHC measures how concentrated hemoglobin is in your red blood cells. It is closely tied to MCH and MCV.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you how "packed" with hemoglobin each cell is. Low MCHC means hypochromic anemia (cells are pale). High MCHC can mean hereditary spherocytosis (a genetic condition where red blood cells are round instead of disc-shaped).

**Reference ranges (g/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–31.9  
- Functional Low: 32.0–32.5  
- **Optimal: 32.6–35.0**  
- Functional High: 35.1–36.0  
- Clinical High: 36.1 and above

**Clinical Low (below 32.0 g/dL):** This means hypochromic anemia. Causes: iron deficiency (most common), thalassemia, sideroblastic anemia, anemia of chronic disease, and lead poisoning. To address: work with a provider. Take iron with vitamin C. Eat iron-rich foods. A provider can check ferritin, iron studies, and gut health.

**Functional Low (32.0–32.5 g/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early iron deficiency or mild thalassemia trait. To raise it: eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C. Consider iron supplements if ferritin is low. A provider can check ferritin and iron studies.

**Optimal (32.6–35.0 g/dL):** This is the goal. It means your red blood cells have the right amount of hemoglobin. To stay here: keep up a nutrient-dense diet with iron, B12, folate, and vitamin C. Avoid too much alcohol. Keep a healthy gut. Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (35.1–36.0 g/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It is usually harmless. It can mean dehydration or high iron. To optimize: drink more water. Watch your iron supplement dose if you take them. If it stays very high, check for hereditary spherocytosis (a benign genetic condition).

**Clinical High (36.1 g/dL and above):** This is rare. It can mean hereditary spherocytosis, severe dehydration, or very high iron. To address: work with a provider. Treat the cause. For hereditary spherocytosis, the spleen may need to be removed in severe cases. For dehydration, drink more water.

## RDW (Red Cell Distribution Width)

**What is this test?** RDW measures how much your red blood cells vary in size. A high RDW means your red blood cells are different sizes (some big, some small). A low RDW means they are all about the same size.

**Why does it matter?** A high RDW is a sign of mixed anemia, recent blood loss, or nutrient problems. It is also an independent predictor of heart disease and death.

**Reference ranges (%, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–7.99  
- Functional Low: 8.0–10.0  
- **Optimal: 10.01–14.0**  
- Functional High: 14.01–15.0  
- Clinical High: 15.01 and above

**Clinical Low (below 8.0%):** A low RDW means your red blood cells are all about the same size. This is usually a good sign. No changes needed. To maintain: keep up a nutrient-dense diet with iron, B12, and folate.

**Functional Low (8.0–10.0%):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It is usually a sign of healthy red blood cell production. To maintain: keep up a nutrient-dense diet with iron, B12, and folate.

**Optimal (10.01–14.0%):** This is the goal. It means your red blood cells are uniform in size and well-made. It is also linked to lower heart risk and longer life. To stay here: keep up a nutrient-dense diet with iron, B12, folate, and vitamin C. Exercise, sleep, and manage stress. Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (14.01–15.0%):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It means your red blood cells vary in size. Common causes: early iron deficiency, low B12 or folate, mixed anemia, recent blood loss, or chronic inflammation. You may feel tired or have higher long-term heart risk. To optimize: find and treat the cause. Eat more iron, B12, and folate. A provider can check ferritin, B12, folate, and inflammation.

**Clinical High (15.01% and above):** A high RDW raises your risk of heart disease, death, and chronic illness. Common causes: iron deficiency anemia (most common), B12 or folate deficiency, mixed deficiencies, chronic blood loss, bone marrow disorders, chronic inflammation, liver disease, kidney disease, and recent blood transfusion. To address: work with a provider. Find and treat the cause. You may need iron, B12, or folate. Eat a nutrient-dense, anti-inflammatory diet.

## Platelet Count

**What is this test?** Platelets are small blood cells that help your blood clot. They also play a role in wound healing and inflammation.

**Why does it matter?** Platelets are needed to stop bleeding. Low platelets (thrombocytopenia) raise your risk of bleeding. High platelets (thrombocytosis) raise your risk of clots and can signal inflammation.

**Reference ranges (K/μL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–74  
- Functional Low: 75–149  
- **Optimal: 150–400**  
- Functional High: 401–454  
- Clinical High: 455 and above

**Clinical Low (below 75 K/μL):** This is called thrombocytopenia. Your blood does not clot well, so you bleed or bruise easily. Causes: bone marrow disorders (leukemia, aplastic anemia, myelodysplastic syndrome), autoimmune destruction (ITP — immune thrombocytopenia, lupus), viral infections (HIV, hepatitis C, EBV, COVID), medicines (heparin, chemo, some antibiotics), severe infections (sepsis), low B12, folate, or copper, liver disease, and rare genetic conditions. To address: work with a provider. Treatment depends on the cause — treat the underlying condition, corticosteroids for ITP, platelet transfusions for severe bleeding risk. Support your bone marrow with B12, folate, iron, and zinc.

**Functional Low (75–149 K/μL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can mean mild bone marrow suppression, chronic viral infections (EBV, COVID), low nutrients, or mild autoimmune activity. You may bruise easily, heal slowly, or feel tired. To raise it: find and treat the cause. Support your bone marrow with a nutrient-dense diet with B12, folate, iron, zinc, and vitamin C. A provider can check viral load, gut, and autoimmunity.

**Optimal (150–400 K/μL):** This is the goal. It means balanced clotting and low chronic inflammation. To stay here: keep up a colorful plant-based diet rich in antioxidants, exercise, sleep 7–9 hours, manage stress, avoid smoking, limit alcohol, and maintain a healthy gut.

**Functional High (401–454 K/μL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It often means low-grade chronic inflammation, iron deficiency, infection, smoking, belly fat, or chronic stress. Higher platelets in this range are linked to higher heart and clot risk. To optimize: address any inflammation or iron deficiency. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet (vegetables, berries, omega-3 fats). Quit smoking. Lose extra belly fat. Exercise regularly. A provider can check inflammation, ferritin, and gut health.

**Clinical High (455 K/μL and above):** This is called thrombocytosis. It raises your risk of clots. Causes: reactive (iron deficiency, infection, inflammation, surgery, cancer, splenectomy), primary (essential thrombocythemia — a bone marrow disorder), and certain medicines. To address: work with a provider. Find and treat the cause. Treatment may include low-dose aspirin, hydroxyurea (a medicine that lowers platelet count), or other medicines. Address iron deficiency if present.

## MPV (Mean Platelet Volume)

**What is this test?** MPV measures the average size of your platelets. Larger platelets are younger and more active, which means higher clot risk. Smaller platelets are older and less active.

**Why does it matter?** Larger platelets are more reactive and raise your risk of clots and heart problems. MPV gives you extra information beyond just the platelet count.

**Reference ranges (fL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–9.3  
- Functional Low: 9.4–10.5  
- **Optimal: 10.6–11.9**  
- Functional High: 12.0–12.2  
- Clinical High: 12.3 and above

**Clinical Low (below 9.3 fL):** This means smaller, older platelets. It is usually a sign of lower clot risk. There is usually no concern. To maintain: keep up a healthy lifestyle.

**Functional Low (9.4–10.5 fL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It is usually harmless. To maintain: keep up a healthy lifestyle.

**Optimal (10.6–11.9 fL):** This is the goal. It means balanced platelet size with low clot risk. To stay here: keep up a whole-food, anti-inflammatory diet, exercise, sleep 7–9 hours, manage stress, avoid smoking, and limit alcohol.

**Functional High (12.0–12.2 fL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It means larger, more reactive platelets, which raises your clot and heart risk, especially if your platelet count is also high or you have diabetes, high blood pressure, or you smoke. Common causes: low-grade chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, smoking, and recent heart attack or stroke. To optimize: address inflammation, insulin resistance, and heart risk factors. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet, exercise, lose extra weight, and manage stress. A provider can check inflammation, fasting insulin, and heart risk.

**Clinical High (12.3 fL and above):** This means very large, hyperactive platelets. It is linked to a much higher risk of clots. Causes: essential thrombocythemia, other bone marrow disorders, recent heart attack or stroke, uncontrolled diabetes, severe inflammation, and certain cancers. To address: work with a provider. They may order a workup and consider low-dose aspirin, heart risk reduction, and treatment of the underlying cause. Eat a heart-healthy, anti-inflammatory diet.

## Absolute Neutrophils

**What is this test?** Neutrophils are the most common white blood cells. They are your body's first responders to bacterial and fungal infections. The absolute count is calculated from the WBC and the neutrophil percentage.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you how strong your body's first-line defense is. Low neutrophils raise your risk of bacterial and fungal infections. High neutrophils often mean bacterial infection, inflammation, or stress.

**Reference ranges (cells/μL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–1,499  
- Functional Low: 1,500–1,999  
- **Optimal: 2,000–5,000**  
- Functional High: 5,001–7,800  
- Clinical High: 7,801 and above

**Clinical Low (below 1,500/μL):** This is called neutropenia. You are at high risk of bacterial and fungal infections. Causes: chemo, radiation, bone marrow disorders, severe viral infections, autoimmune disease (lupus, RA), certain medicines (clozapine, methimazole, sulfa drugs), low B12, folate, or copper, and an overactive spleen. To address: work with a provider. They may give growth factors (G-CSF — a medicine that helps your bone marrow make more neutrophils), antibiotics for protection, and treat the cause. Support your bone marrow with B12, folate, zinc, and vitamin D.

**Functional Low (1,500–1,999/μL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can be from chronic viral infections (EBV, COVID), chronic stress, mild bone marrow suppression, or low nutrients. You may get sick more easily, heal slowly, or feel tired. To raise it: address any chronic viral infections. Support your immune system with a nutrient-dense diet, sleep, and stress control. Make sure you get enough zinc, vitamin D, vitamin C, B12, and folate.

**Optimal (2,000–5,000/μL):** This is the goal. It means balanced innate immune function with appropriate response to infection and low chronic inflammation. To stay here: keep up a whole-food diet, exercise, sleep 7–9 hours, manage stress, avoid smoking, limit alcohol, and maintain a healthy gut.

**Functional High (5,001–7,800/μL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It often means low-grade chronic inflammation, hidden infection, smoking, belly fat, chronic stress, or intense exercise. Higher neutrophils in this range are linked to higher heart risk. To optimize: address inflammation and any hidden infection. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet, quit smoking, lose extra belly fat, and exercise moderately. A provider can check inflammation, gut, teeth, and sinuses.

**Clinical High (7,801/μL and above):** This means significant immune activation. Most often, it is from a bacterial infection. Other causes: severe inflammation (autoimmune disease, vasculitis), tissue damage (surgery, trauma, burns), certain medicines (steroids, lithium, beta-agonists), and bone marrow disorders (chronic myeloid leukemia, polycythemia vera). To address: work with a provider. Treat the underlying infection or inflammation. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet.

**Functional High (3.0–5.9%):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. Common causes: allergies (food, environmental, seasonal), asthma, eczema, mild parasite infection, and certain autoimmune conditions. You may have allergic symptoms, gut issues, or skin problems. To optimize: find and address allergens (food sensitivity testing, elimination diet). Support gut health. Cut down on allergen exposure. A provider can check gut and immune function.

**Clinical High (6.0% and above):** This is called eosinophilia. It means significant allergic, parasitic, or autoimmune activity. Causes: severe allergies, asthma, eczema, parasite infections, certain autoimmune diseases, certain cancers, and certain medicines. To address: work with a provider. Find and treat the cause.

## Basophils (%)

**What is this test?** This is the percentage of basophils out of your total white blood cells. It is the relative amount. It is reported with the absolute count.

**Why does it matter?** It gives you extra information about allergies, inflammation, and certain bone marrow disorders. High basophil percentage can be a sign of chronic inflammation or rare bone marrow problems.

- Functional High: 2.6–3.9  
- Clinical High: 4.0 and above

You may have allergic symptoms, fatigue, or mild inflammation. To optimize: find and address allergens, support gut health, and treat any inflammation. A provider can check allergies, gut, thyroid, and inflammation.

**Clinical High (4.0% and above):** This is called basophilia. It needs evaluation. Causes: chronic myeloid leukemia and other bone marrow disorders, chronic inflammation, allergies, underactive thyroid, and iron deficiency. To address: work with a provider. They may order a bone marrow biopsy, JAK2 testing, and an evaluation for the underlying cause.

## Monocytes (%)

**What is this test?** This is the percentage of monocytes out of your total white blood cells. It is the relative amount. It is reported with the absolute count.

**Why does it matter?** It gives you another way to look at chronic inflammation, infections, and tissue repair. High monocyte percentage can mean chronic inflammation, chronic infection, or autoimmune disease.

**Reference ranges (%, both sexes):**

- Clinical High: 10.0 and above

It often means chronic inflammation, chronic infections (viral, dental, gut), autoimmune disease, smoking, belly fat, or recovery from an acute infection. ## Calcium

**What is this test?** Calcium is the most common mineral in your body. About 99% of it is in your bones and teeth. The other 1% is in your blood, where it helps your muscles squeeze, your nerves send signals, your blood clot, and your cells talk to each other.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you about your bone health, parathyroid gland function, vitamin D status, and how well your body manages minerals. Blood calcium is tightly controlled, so even small changes can be meaningful. Bone density scans (DEXA) are the best way to know your total body calcium.

**Reference ranges (mg/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–8.05  
- Functional Low: 8.06–9.01  
- **Optimal: 9.02–10.5**  
- Functional High: 10.51–11.03  
- Clinical High: 11.04 and above

**Clinical Low (below 8.05 mg/dL):** This is called hypocalcemia. It can cause muscle cramps, tingling, twitching, seizures, irregular heart beats, and brittle nails. Causes: underactive parathyroid glands, low vitamin D, low magnesium, severe malnutrition, malabsorption (celiac, IBD), kidney disease, certain medicines (loop diuretics, bisphosphonates, calcitonin), and acute pancreatitis. To address: work with a provider. Treatment may include calcium and vitamin D supplements, magnesium, and treatment of the cause. Eat calcium-rich foods (leafy greens, fortified plant milks, sardines, almonds, tofu). Make sure you have enough vitamin D and magnesium to absorb calcium.

**Functional Low (8.06–9.01 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means low vitamin D, mild parathyroid problems, not enough calcium in your diet, or malabsorption. You may have subtle muscle cramps, brittle nails, dental issues, and a higher long-term risk of osteoporosis. To raise it: get 1000–1200 mg of calcium a day from food first (leafy greens, fortified plant milks, sardines, almonds, tofu). Make sure you have enough vitamin D (target 40–60 ng/mL), magnesium (400–500 mg a day), and vitamin K2 (helps put calcium in bones). Do weight-bearing exercise. A provider can check vitamin D, parathyroid hormone, and gut health.

**Optimal (9.02–10.5 mg/dL):** This is the goal. It means balanced calcium regulation, healthy parathyroid function, and enough vitamin D. To stay here: keep up a diet rich in leafy greens (kale, bok choy, broccoli, collards), fortified plant milks, sardines, almonds, tofu, and tahini. Make sure you have enough vitamin D (sunlight, supplements), magnesium, and K2. Do weight-bearing exercise. Avoid too much sodium and caffeine (both increase calcium loss). Periodically re-check, especially for postmenopausal women and older adults.

**Functional High (10.51–11.03 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean overactive parathyroid glands (most common cause), too much vitamin D or calcium, certain cancers, dehydration, or long-term immobility. You may feel tired, constipated, have kidney stones, bone pain, or brain fog. To optimize: check for primary hyperparathyroidism (test PTH, ionized calcium, 24-hour urine calcium). Cut back on calcium and vitamin D supplements if you take too much. Drink more water. A provider can check parathyroid function.

**Clinical High (11.04 mg/dL and above):** This is called hypercalcemia. It can cause "stones, bones, groans, and psychiatric overtones" — kidney stones, bone pain, belly pain, sadness, confusion, and irregular heart beats. Causes: primary hyperparathyroidism (most common), certain cancers (lung, breast, multiple myeloma, lymphoma), too much vitamin D or calcium, granulomatous diseases (sarcoidosis, TB), long-term immobility, and certain medicines (thiazide diuretics, lithium). To address: work with a provider urgently. Treatment depends on the cause and how severe it is — fluids, bisphosphonates, calcitonin, parathyroid surgery, or cancer treatment. Cut back on calcium and vitamin D supplements.

## Iron, Total (Serum Iron)

**What is this test?** Serum iron measures how much iron is in your blood, mostly attached to a protein called transferrin. It changes a lot during the day and with meals, so it is best looked at with other iron tests (TIBC, transferrin saturation, ferritin).

**Why does it matter?** It tells you about your iron status. Low iron causes anemia, fatigue, brain fog, and hair loss. High iron can damage your liver, heart, and pancreas (see hemochromatosis).

**Reference ranges (μg/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–49  
- Functional Low: 50–99  
- **Optimal: 100–150**  
- Functional High: 151–180  
- Clinical High: 181 and above

**Clinical Low (below 50 μg/dL):** This means iron deficiency — the most common nutritional deficiency in the world. Causes: not enough iron in your diet, malabsorption (celiac, IBD, SIBO, H. pylori), chronic blood loss (gut, menstrual, ulcers), pregnancy, growth spurts, and chronic inflammation. You may feel tired, foggy, lose hair, have brittle nails, restless legs, crave non-food items (pica), and have trouble exercising. To address: work with a provider. Eat iron-rich foods (grass-fed red meat, organ meats, dark leafy greens, lentils, fortified cereals) with vitamin C for better absorption. Take iron supplements (iron bisglycinate is gentle on the gut).

**Functional Low (50–99 μg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early iron deficiency, not enough iron in your diet, mild malabsorption, or chronic inflammation. You may feel tired, foggy, lose hair, or have trouble exercising. To raise it: eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C. Cut back on coffee and tea with meals (tannins block iron absorption). Consider iron supplements if ferritin is also low. A provider can check ferritin, TIBC, transferrin saturation, and gut health.

**Optimal (100–150 μg/dL):** This is the goal. It means you are getting enough iron, absorbing it well, and have no chronic blood loss. To stay here: keep up a whole-food diet with heme iron (iron from animal sources: grass-fed red meat, organ meats, sardines) and plant iron (dark leafy greens, lentils, fortified cereals), with vitamin C. Avoid coffee and tea with meals. Periodically re-check, especially for menstruating women, pregnant women, and those with gut problems.

**Functional High (151–180 μg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean a lot of iron in your diet, iron supplements, hemochromatosis (a genetic disorder where your body absorbs too much iron), liver disease, hemolysis (when red blood cells are destroyed), or low B6. You may have more oxidative stress over time (oxidative stress is cell damage from unstable molecules called free radicals). To optimize: cut back on iron supplements if you take too many. Limit heme iron. Eat more iron-absorption blockers (coffee, tea, calcium with meals). Check ferritin and transferrin saturation. A provider can check ferritin, transferrin saturation, liver, and the HFE gene (for hemochromatosis).

**Clinical High (181 μg/dL and above):** This needs evaluation. Causes: iron supplements, hemochromatosis (HFE gene mutations), liver disease, hemolytic anemia, sideroblastic anemia (a rare disorder where the body cannot use iron to make hemoglobin), lead poisoning, and acute iron poisoning. Iron overload damages the liver, heart, pancreas, and joints. To address: work with a provider. Treatment may include phlebotomy (blood removal) for hemochromatosis, iron chelation (medicines that bind and remove iron), and dietary changes. Avoid vitamin C with meals if you have iron overload.

## Iron Binding Capacity (TIBC)

**What is this test?** TIBC (Total Iron Binding Capacity) measures how well your blood can carry iron. It mainly reflects transferrin, the main protein that carries iron in your blood. High TIBC usually means your body is trying to grab more iron (iron deficiency). Low TIBC usually means your body has plenty of iron or has inflammation, liver disease, or malnutrition.

**Why does it matter?** It helps figure out if your iron is low because of deficiency, inflammation, or overload. It is most useful when looked at with serum iron and transferrin saturation.

**Reference ranges (μg/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–124  
- Functional Low: 125–200  
- **Optimal: 201–400**  
- Functional High: 401–450  
- Clinical High: 451 and above

**Clinical Low (below 125 μg/dL):** This means iron overload, chronic inflammation, liver disease, malnutrition, or protein loss. Common causes: hemochromatosis, chronic inflammation, liver disease, nephrotic syndrome (a kidney disorder that causes protein loss in urine), and severe malnutrition. You may have iron overload, fatigue, and organ damage. To address: work with a provider. Treat the cause. For hemochromatosis, phlebotomy is the standard treatment. For inflammation, address the underlying cause. For liver disease, support your liver.

**Functional Low (125–200 μg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early iron overload, low-grade chronic inflammation, or mild liver problems. To optimize: address any inflammation or iron overload. Cut back on iron supplements if you take too many. Limit heme iron. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet. A provider can check ferritin, transferrin saturation, liver, and the HFE gene.

**Optimal (201–400 μg/dL):** This is the goal. It means balanced iron metabolism with enough transferrin and no iron overload or deficiency. To stay here: keep up a whole-food diet, exercise, sleep 7–9 hours, manage stress, avoid smoking, limit alcohol, and maintain a healthy gut. Periodically re-check, especially if you have risk factors for iron problems.

**Functional High (401–450 μg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It often means early iron deficiency (your body is making more transferrin to grab scarce iron), not enough iron in your diet, or mild malabsorption. You may feel tired or have trouble exercising. To optimize: eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C. Cut back on coffee and tea with meals. A provider can check ferritin, transferrin saturation, and gut health.

**Clinical High (451 μg/dL and above):** This is a classic finding in iron deficiency anemia. Causes: iron deficiency (most common), pregnancy, birth control pills, and acute hepatitis. You may feel tired, foggy, lose hair, and have trouble exercising. To address: work with a provider. Take iron supplements (iron bisglycinate or ferrous sulfate) with vitamin C. Eat iron-rich foods. Check for chronic blood loss (gut, menstrual).

## % Saturation (Transferrin Saturation)

**What is this test?** Transferrin saturation is your serum iron divided by your TIBC. It shows what percentage of your transferrin is full of iron. It is the main screening test for iron overload (hemochromatosis).

**Why does it matter?** It tells you how full your iron taxis are. Low saturation means your body is short on iron. High saturation means your body has too much iron.

**Reference ranges (%, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–14  
- Functional Low: 15–19  
- **Optimal: 20–45**  
- Functional High: 46–54  
- Clinical High: 55 and above

**Clinical Low (below 15%):** This means iron deficiency. It is one of the earliest and most specific signs of iron deficiency, often showing up before anemia. Causes: not enough iron in your diet, malabsorption, chronic blood loss, pregnancy, and growth spurts. To address: work with a provider. Take iron supplements with vitamin C. Eat iron-rich foods. A provider can check ferritin, gut health, and sources of blood loss.

**Functional Low (15–19%):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early iron deficiency or low iron stores before anemia develops. You may feel tired, foggy, lose hair, have restless legs, or have trouble exercising. To optimize: eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C. Cut back on coffee and tea with meals. Consider iron supplements if ferritin is also low. A provider can check ferritin, gut, and causes of iron loss.

**Optimal (20–45%):** This is the goal. It means balanced iron metabolism with enough iron stores and no overload. To stay here: keep up a whole-food diet with heme and plant iron, with vitamin C. Avoid coffee and tea with meals. Periodically re-check, especially for menstruating women, pregnant women, and those with gut problems.

**Functional High (46–54%):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean a lot of iron in your diet, iron supplements, hemochromatosis, liver disease, or hemolysis. You may have more oxidative stress over time. To optimize: cut back on iron supplements if you take too many. Limit heme iron. Eat more iron-absorption blockers. A provider can check ferritin, liver, and the HFE gene.

**Clinical High (55% and above):** This is the screening cutoff for hemochromatosis (HFE gene mutations — C282Y, H63D). Sustained saturation above 45% needs genetic testing. Causes: hereditary hemochromatosis (most common in people of Northern European descent), iron supplements, liver disease, hemolytic anemia, and sideroblastic anemia. Iron overload damages the liver, heart, pancreas, and joints. To address: work with a provider. They will order genetic testing for HFE, ferritin, and liver tests. Treatment is phlebotomy (regular blood removal) for hemochromatosis. Cut back on iron intake. Avoid vitamin C with meals.

## Ferritin, Male

**What is this test?** Ferritin is a protein that stores iron in your body. It reflects your total iron stores. It is the best single marker of iron status, especially for iron deficiency. Ferritin is also an acute phase reactant, meaning it goes up with inflammation, infection, and liver disease — so a "normal" ferritin can hide iron deficiency when there is inflammation.

**Why does it matter?** Low ferritin is the earliest sign of iron deficiency, even before anemia shows up. It causes fatigue, brain fog, hair loss, brittle nails, and restless legs. High ferritin can mean iron overload (hemochromatosis), inflammation, or liver disease.

**Reference ranges (ng/mL, males only):**

- Clinical Low: 0–12.99  
- Functional Low: 13.0–50.99  
- **Optimal: 51.0–100.99**  
- Functional High: 101.0–300.99  
- Clinical High: 301.0 and above

**Clinical Low (below 13.0 ng/mL):** This is diagnostic for iron deficiency, even before anemia develops. It is the earliest and most sensitive sign of iron depletion. Causes: not enough iron in your diet, malabsorption (celiac, IBD, SIBO, H. pylori, gastric bypass), chronic blood loss (gut, ulcers), and chronic inflammation. You may feel tired, foggy, lose hair, have brittle nails, restless legs, crave non-food items (pica), and have trouble exercising. To address: work with a provider. Take iron supplements (iron bisglycinate is gentle on the gut, 25–50 mg a day) with vitamin C. Eat heme iron (iron from animal sources: grass-fed red meat, organ meats, sardines) and plant iron (dark leafy greens, lentils, fortified cereals) with vitamin C. Check for chronic blood loss (gut).

**Functional Low (13.0–50.99 ng/mL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It means your iron stores are too low for your body to work at its best, even before anemia develops. It is linked to fatigue, brain fog, hair loss, brittle nails, restless legs, exercise intolerance, and reduced thyroid function (your body needs iron to turn T4 into T3). To raise it: eat iron-rich foods (heme and plant sources) with vitamin C. Consider iron supplements (iron bisglycinate, 18–36 mg a day). A provider can check ferritin, iron studies, thyroid, and gut health.

**Optimal (51.0–100.99 ng/mL):** This is the goal. It means you have enough iron for your body to work at its best — energy, thinking, thyroid function, and exercise tolerance. To stay here: keep up a whole-food diet rich in heme iron (grass-fed red meat, organ meats, sardines) and plant iron (dark leafy greens, lentils, fortified cereals) with vitamin C. Avoid coffee and tea with meals. Periodic re-testing is a good idea.

**Functional High (101.0–300.99 ng/mL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. Causes: iron supplements, high dietary iron, inflammation (acute phase reactant), liver disease, metabolic syndrome, alcohol use, and hemochromatosis (HFE gene mutations). Ferritin above 100 with normal iron studies and no inflammation is usually harmless. With inflammation, high ferritin can hide iron deficiency. To optimize: look for the cause. Cut back on iron supplements if needed. Limit alcohol. A provider can check fasting insulin, liver, transferrin saturation, and the HFE gene.

**Clinical High (301.0 ng/mL and above):** A high ferritin in men needs evaluation. Causes: hemochromatosis (most common in men of Northern European descent — HFE gene mutations C282Y, H63D), liver disease, metabolic syndrome, inflammation, cancer, and iron supplements. Hemochromatosis causes iron overload with damage to the liver, heart, pancreas, and joints. Symptoms: fatigue, joint pain, belly pain, bronze skin, diabetes, and heart problems. To address: work with a provider. They will order genetic testing for HFE, transferrin saturation, liver tests, and iron studies. Treatment for hemochromatosis is phlebotomy (regular blood removal). Cut back on iron intake. Avoid vitamin C with meals. Avoid alcohol.

## Magnesium

**What is this test?** Magnesium is a mineral involved in over 300 chemical reactions in your body. It helps make energy (ATP — the energy currency of your cells), relaxes muscles, calms nerves, controls blood sugar, regulates blood pressure, and helps you sleep. Most magnesium in your body is inside your cells. Red blood cell (RBC) magnesium is a more accurate test than serum magnesium.

**Why does it matter?** Magnesium is critical for energy, sleep, blood sugar, blood pressure, and muscle function. About half of Americans do not get enough magnesium. Low magnesium is linked to many health problems.

**Reference ranges (mg/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–1.5  
- Functional Low: 1.6–2.0  
- **Optimal: 2.1–3.0**  
- Functional High: 3.1–3.2  
- Clinical High: 3.3 and above

**Clinical Low (below 1.5 mg/dL):** This is called hypomagnesemia. It can cause muscle cramps, tremors, seizures, irregular heart beats, fatigue, anxiety, insomnia, and crankiness. Causes: not enough magnesium in your diet, malabsorption, chronic alcohol use, certain medicines (PPIs — proton pump inhibitors used for acid reflux, diuretics, some antibiotics), uncontrolled diabetes, overactive parathyroid glands, chronic diarrhea, and rare genetic disorders. To address: work with a provider. Take magnesium supplements (magnesium glycinate, malate, or threonate). Eat magnesium-rich foods (pumpkin seeds, almonds, spinach, dark chocolate, avocados, black beans). Treat the cause. RBC magnesium is the better test.

**Functional Low (1.6–2.0 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It is very common — about half of Americans are magnesium deficient. Subtle symptoms: muscle cramps, insomnia, anxiety, fatigue, constipation, and heart palpitations. To raise it: eat magnesium-rich foods (pumpkin seeds, almonds, spinach, dark chocolate, avocados, black beans, salmon). Consider magnesium supplements (300–600 mg a day of magnesium glycinate or malate). Treat the cause. RBC magnesium is the better test.

**Optimal (2.1–3.0 mg/dL):** This is the goal. It means your magnesium stores are good, your muscles and nerves work well, and your stress response is balanced. To stay here: keep up magnesium-rich foods. Consider ongoing magnesium supplements (200–400 mg a day) since deficiency is so common. Manage stress (stress uses up magnesium). Avoid too much alcohol. Keep blood sugar well-controlled. Periodically re-check RBC magnesium.

**Functional High (3.1–3.2 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It usually means a lot of supplementation, healthy kidneys that are keeping magnesium in, or dehydration. To optimize: if supplementing, make sure your dose is not too high. Drink plenty of water. If not supplementing, this is usually a great sign of strong magnesium status.

**Clinical High (3.3 mg/dL and above):** Very high serum magnesium is rare and usually from too much supplementation, especially in people with kidney disease. It can cause tiredness, confusion, low blood pressure, and irregular heart beats. To address: work with a provider. Cut back or stop magnesium supplements. In severe cases, calcium gluconate IV (through a vein) may be needed. If you have kidney disease, your provider will check your kidneys. Avoid magnesium laxatives and antacids.

## Carbon Dioxide (CO2 / Bicarbonate)

**What is this test?** CO2 (also called bicarbonate or HCO3-) measures bicarbonate in your blood. Bicarbonate is your body's main buffer — it keeps your blood pH (acid-base balance) in the right range.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you about your acid-base balance, kidney function, and lung function. Your body needs to keep pH in a tight range to work well.

**Reference ranges (mmol/L, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–19  
- Functional Low: 19.1–21.9  
- **Optimal: 22–29**  
- Functional High: 29.1–32  
- Clinical High: 32.1 and above

**Clinical Low (below 19 mmol/L):** This is called metabolic acidosis. It can cause fast breathing, confusion, tiredness, and shock in severe cases. Causes: diabetic ketoacidosis, lactic acidosis, kidney disease, severe diarrhea, certain poisonings (methanol, ethylene glycol — found in antifreeze, salicylates like aspirin), prolonged fasting, ketogenic diet (mild), and certain medicines. To address: work with a provider urgently for severe acidosis. Treatment depends on the cause — insulin for DKA, IV fluids, sodium bicarbonate in some cases, treatment of the underlying cause.

**Functional Low (19.1–21.9 mmol/L):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can mean subclinical metabolic acidosis from a high-acid diet (lots of animal protein, processed foods), mild kidney problems, chronic stress, or chronic inflammation. You may feel tired, foggy, and lose bone over time. To optimize: shift to a plant-centered, alkaline-promoting diet (vegetables, fruits, leafy greens). Cut back on processed foods. Drink plenty of water. Support your kidneys. A provider can check kidney function and dietary acid load.

**Optimal (22–29 mmol/L):** This is the goal. It means balanced acid-base status, healthy kidneys, and a balanced diet. To stay here: keep up a colorful plant-based diet rich in vegetables and fruits. Limit processed foods. Stay well hydrated. Exercise most days. Periodically re-check, especially with diet changes or illness.

**Functional High (29.1–32 mmol/L):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean subclinical metabolic alkalosis from long-term vomiting, diuretics, too much bicarbonate, or your body compensating for chronic respiratory acidosis. Most often, it is harmless. To optimize: make sure your diet and fluids are balanced. If you take diuretics or bicarbonate supplements, work with a provider to adjust.

**Clinical High (32.1 mmol/L and above):** This is called metabolic alkalosis. It can cause muscle twitching, hand tremor, confusion, and irregular heart beats in severe cases. Causes: long-term vomiting, diuretics, too much bicarbonate/antacid, hyperaldosteronism (a condition where the adrenal glands make too much aldosterone), Cushing syndrome, Bartter syndrome (a rare genetic kidney disorder), and chronic respiratory acidosis. To address: work with a provider. Treatment depends on the cause — IV fluids, electrolytes, treating the underlying cause, or in some cases, ammonium chloride or acetazolamide.

## Sodium

**What is this test?** Sodium is a mineral that is mostly outside your cells. It helps control fluid balance, nerve signals, and muscle contractions. Your kidneys, adrenal glands, and thirst mechanism keep it in a tight range.

**Why does it matter?** Sodium is critical for hydration, nerve function, and muscle function. Both too little and too much sodium can be dangerous.

**Reference ranges (mmol/L, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–133  
- Functional Low: 134–135  
- **Optimal: 136–143**  
- Functional High: 144–146  
- Clinical High: 147 and above

**Clinical Low (below 134 mmol/L):** This is called hyponatremia. It can cause nausea, headache, confusion, tiredness, seizures, and coma in severe cases. Causes: drinking too much water, SIADH (a condition where the body makes too much antidiuretic hormone), underactive adrenal glands, underactive thyroid, heart failure, kidney disease, liver cirrhosis, certain medicines (diuretics, SSRIs — a common type of antidepressant, anticonvulsants), and severe vomiting or diarrhea. To address: work with a provider. Treatment depends on the cause and how severe it is — fluid restriction, sodium replacement, treating the underlying cause, or hypertonic saline (a salty IV fluid) in severe cases.

**Functional Low (134–135 mmol/L):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means over-hydration, mild adrenal insufficiency, low dietary sodium, certain medicines (thiazide diuretics), or mild SIADH. You may feel tired, foggy, or have headaches. To optimize: drink to thirst (usually 2–3 liters a day, not more). Make sure you get enough dietary sodium (most people need 1.5–3 grams a day from whole-food sources like sea salt, olives, fermented foods, celery). Support your adrenals. A provider can check adrenal function and fluid balance.

**Optimal (136–143 mmol/L):** This is the goal. It means balanced fluid regulation, healthy adrenal and kidney function, and good hydration. To stay here: drink to thirst (usually 2–3 liters a day). Eat a whole-food diet with moderate sodium from natural sources. Avoid both too much and too little water. Avoid too much processed food (high in sodium) and too much plain water without electrolytes. Periodically re-check, especially with new symptoms.

**Functional High (144–146 mmol/L):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It often means mild dehydration, too much dietary sodium (processed foods), or mild diabetes insipidus (a condition where the kidneys cannot concentrate urine). You may feel thirsty, tired, and have higher heart risk over time. To optimize: drink more water. Cut back on processed foods. Eat whole-food sources of sodium. A provider can check hydration, kidney, and adrenal function.

**Clinical High (147 mmol/L and above):** This is called hypernatremia. It means significant dehydration or too much sodium. Symptoms: thirst, confusion, muscle twitching, seizures, and coma in severe cases. Causes: not drinking enough water, too much sweating, diabetes insipidus, kidney disease, certain medicines, and salt poisoning. To address: work with a provider. Treatment depends on the cause and how severe it is — slow rehydration with electrolyte-balanced fluids, treating the cause, and avoiding fast correction (which can cause brain swelling).

## Potassium

**What is this test?** Potassium is a mineral that is mostly inside your cells. It is critical for muscle contractions, nerve signals, blood pressure, and fluid balance. Most potassium in your body is inside cells, so serum potassium is a small fraction of total.

**Why does it matter?** It is critical for your heart, muscles, and nerves. Both too little and too much potassium can cause irregular heart beats. Higher potassium within the normal range is linked to better blood pressure and heart health.

**Reference ranges (mmol/L, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–3.0  
- Functional Low: 3.1–3.9  
- **Optimal: 4.0–4.9**  
- Functional High: 5.0–5.2  
- Clinical High: 5.3 and above

**Clinical Low (below 3.0 mmol/L):** This is called hypokalemia. It can cause muscle weakness, cramps, fatigue, constipation, heart palpitations, irregular heart beats, and paralysis in severe cases. Causes: not enough potassium in your diet, certain medicines (loop and thiazide diuretics, laxatives, steroids), chronic diarrhea, vomiting, too much sweating, kidney disease, hyperaldosteronism, and low magnesium. To address: work with a provider. Take potassium supplements (potassium citrate or chloride). Eat potassium-rich foods (avocados, bananas, sweet potatoes, spinach, beans, salmon, coconut water).

**Functional Low (3.1–3.9 mmol/L):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means not enough potassium in your diet, certain medicines, chronic stress (cortisol drives potassium out of cells), or mild kidney loss. You may feel tired, have muscle cramps, constipation, or slightly higher blood pressure. To raise it: eat potassium-rich foods (avocados, bananas, sweet potatoes, spinach, beans, salmon, coconut water). Make sure you have enough magnesium. A provider can check adrenal function, medicines, and kidney losses.

**Optimal (4.0–4.9 mmol/L):** This is the goal. It means you are getting enough potassium, your kidneys work well, and your adrenals and cells are balanced. To stay here: keep up a diet rich in potassium-rich foods (avocados, bananas, sweet potatoes, spinach, beans, salmon, coconut water, tomatoes, oranges). Limit processed foods (low in potassium). Make sure you have enough magnesium. Periodically re-check, especially with new medicines or illness.

**Functional High (5.0–5.2 mmol/L):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean a lot of potassium in your diet, certain medicines (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics — common blood pressure medicines), kidney problems, or hard exercise (briefly). In most cases, this is a great sign of strong potassium status. If you take medicines that raise potassium, work with a provider to monitor. Drink plenty of water.

**Clinical High (5.3 mmol/L and above):** This is called hyperkalemia. It can cause muscle weakness, heart palpitations, irregular heart beats, and cardiac arrest in severe cases. Causes: kidney disease, adrenal insufficiency, certain medicines (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs — common pain medicines like ibuprofen), too much potassium, tissue breakdown (rhabdomyolysis — muscle breakdown, hemolysis), and metabolic acidosis. To address: work with a provider urgently. Treatment depends on how severe it is — calcium gluconate (protects the heart), insulin and glucose, sodium bicarbonate, diuretics, or dialysis in severe cases. Review medicines with a provider.

## Chloride

**What is this test?** Chloride is a mineral that is mostly outside your cells. It works with sodium to control fluid balance, blood volume, and acid-base balance. It is also a main part of stomach acid (HCl).

**Why does it matter?** It helps you understand your fluid, acid-base, and stomach acid status. Low chloride can mean long-term vomiting or metabolic alkalosis. High chloride can mean metabolic acidosis.

**Reference ranges (mmol/L, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–97  
- Functional Low: 98–99  
- **Optimal: 100–105**  
- Functional High: 106–110  
- Clinical High: 111 and above

**Clinical Low (below 98 mmol/L):** This is called hypochloremia. It can cause muscle twitching, tiredness, and trouble breathing. Causes: long-term vomiting, chronic respiratory acidosis, metabolic alkalosis, certain medicines (diuretics), adrenal insufficiency, and rare genetic conditions (Bartter syndrome, Gitelman syndrome). To address: work with a provider. Treatment depends on the cause — IV saline in some cases, treating the underlying cause, and adjusting medicines.

**Functional Low (98–99 mmol/L):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means mild metabolic alkalosis, long-term vomiting, certain medicines (diuretics), or low stomach acid. You may feel tired, have poor digestion, and electrolyte imbalances. To optimize: drink enough water and electrolytes. If your stomach acid is low, consider betaine HCl with meals (under provider guidance). A provider can check stomach acid, adrenal function, and acid-base balance.

**Optimal (100–105 mmol/L):** This is the goal. It means balanced fluid and acid-base status, healthy kidneys, and good stomach acid. To stay here: keep up a whole-food diet with adequate sea salt (1.5–3 grams of sodium a day, which provides chloride), drink enough water, and keep your digestion healthy. Avoid too much processed food (high in chloride) and too much plain water without electrolytes. Periodically re-check, especially with new symptoms.

**Functional High (106–110 mmol/L):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean mild metabolic acidosis, too much salt, certain medicines, or dehydration. To optimize: drink plenty of water, cut back on processed food, and eat a whole-food diet. A provider can check kidney function, acid-base balance, and dietary habits.

**Clinical High (111 mmol/L and above):** This is called hyperchloremia. It is usually linked to metabolic acidosis. Causes: kidney disease, severe diarrhea, certain medicines (carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, saline infusions), diabetes insipidus, and renal tubular acidosis (a condition where the kidneys cannot remove acid properly). To address: work with a provider. Treatment depends on the cause — IV fluids, sodium bicarbonate in some cases, treating the underlying condition. Cut back on excess salt.

## Vitamin B12

**What is this test?** Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is a vitamin that helps your body make red blood cells, DNA, and nerve coverings. It is also key for energy and methylation (a process that helps your body turn on and off genes, make neurotransmitters, and process toxins). Methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine are more sensitive tests for B12 function.

**Why does it matter?** Low B12 causes anemia, nerve damage, brain fog, sad mood, and high homocysteine (which raises heart risk). Vegans and vegetarians are at high risk. Stomach acid declines with age, so older adults are at risk too.

**Reference ranges (pg/mL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–200  
- Functional Low: 201–399  
- **Optimal: 400–950**  
- Functional High: 951–1100  
- Clinical High: 1101 and above

**Clinical Low (below 201 pg/mL):** This causes megaloblastic anemia (a type of anemia with large red blood cells), nerve damage in the hands and feet, memory loss, depression, fatigue, swollen tongue, and high homocysteine. Causes: not enough B12 in your diet (vegans, vegetarians), malabsorption (pernicious anemia — a condition where the body cannot absorb B12, celiac, IBD, SIBO, H. pylori, gastric bypass), certain medicines (PPIs, metformin, H2 blockers), and rare genetic disorders. To address: work with a provider. B12 supplements are key — injections for severe deficiency or malabsorption, sublingual (under the tongue) or oral for milder cases. Eat B12-rich foods (grass-fed red meat, fish, eggs, dairy, fortified nutritional yeast). Treat the cause. MMA and homocysteine are better tests.

**Functional Low (201–399 pg/mL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It is linked to subtle fatigue, brain fog, mood changes, nerve symptoms, and high homocysteine. Many people in this range have high MMA (a more sensitive marker) and would benefit from supplements. Causes: not enough B12 in your diet, mild malabsorption, certain medicines (PPIs, metformin), and aging (less stomach acid). To raise it: eat B12-rich foods (animal products or fortified nutritional yeast for vegans). Consider B12 supplements (methylcobalamin or hydroxocobalamin, 500–1000 mcg a day sublingual). A provider can check MMA, homocysteine, and gut health.

**Optimal (400–950 pg/mL):** This is the goal. It means you are getting enough B12, absorbing it well, and your methylation is working well. To stay here: keep up a whole-food diet with B12 (animal products or fortified foods for vegans). For vegans and those with malabsorption, consider ongoing B12 supplements (500–1000 mcg a day methylcobalamin). Periodically re-check along with MMA and homocysteine.

**Functional High (951–1100 pg/mL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It usually means you are taking supplements, eat a lot of B12-rich foods, or rarely it can mean liver disease, certain cancers, or kidney disease. In most cases of supplementation, this is harmless. If very high without supplements, a provider can check for liver disease or certain blood disorders. Continue your current B12 plan.

**Clinical High (1101 pg/mL and above):** Very high B12 is rare and usually from high-dose supplements. Without supplements, it can be linked to liver disease, kidney disease, certain cancers (especially blood cancers), and rarely other conditions. To address: work with a provider to check for underlying conditions if B12 is very high without supplements. Cut back on supplement dose if it is too high.

## Vitamin D (25-OH Vitamin D)

**What is this test?** Vitamin D is a vitamin and a prohormone (your body turns it into a hormone). It is critical for bone health, immune function, mood, hormone regulation, and cell health. Your skin makes vitamin D from sunlight. You can also get it from food (fatty fish, fortified dairy, egg yolks) and supplements. The 25-OH form is the best marker of your vitamin D status.

**Why does it matter?** Vitamin D is one of the most common nutritional deficiencies. Low vitamin D is linked to weak bones, frequent infections, autoimmune disease, depression, fatigue, and higher risk of many chronic diseases (cancer, heart disease, diabetes).

**Reference ranges (ng/mL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–40  
- Functional Low: 41–59  
- **Optimal: 60–90**  
- Functional High: 91–109  
- Clinical High: 110 and above

**Clinical Low (below 41 ng/mL):** This is vitamin D deficiency or insufficiency. It causes rickets in children, osteomalacia in adults (soft bones), osteoporosis, higher fracture risk, frequent infections, autoimmune disease, depression, fatigue, and higher risk of chronic disease. Causes: not enough sun exposure, dark skin, malabsorption (celiac, IBD, gastric bypass), obesity (vitamin D gets trapped in fat), kidney or liver disease, certain medicines (steroids, anticonvulsants), and aging. To address: work with a provider. Take vitamin D3 supplements (2000–5000 IU a day, sometimes more). Eat vitamin D-rich foods (fatty fish, fortified dairy, egg yolks). Treat malabsorption and obesity.

**Functional Low (41–59 ng/mL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It is linked to subtle fatigue, mood changes, frequent infections, bone loss, and higher long-term disease risk. The OHS functional range is tighter than the conventional lab range — this level may not yet be a clinical concern but is not yet optimal. To optimize: take vitamin D3 supplements (2000–4000 IU a day) with a fat-containing meal for better absorption. Eat vitamin D-rich foods (fatty fish, fortified dairy, egg yolks). Get safe sun exposure (15–30 minutes of midday sun on bare skin). A provider can check gut, liver, and kidney if your levels stay low.

**Optimal (60–90 ng/mL):** This is the goal. It means your bones, immune system, mood, and long-term health are well-supported. To stay here: keep up a whole-food diet rich in vitamin D (fatty fish, fortified dairy, egg yolks), safe sun exposure, and supplements if needed (1000–2000 IU a day for maintenance). Co-factors for vitamin D are magnesium, vitamin K2, and zinc — make sure you have enough. Periodically re-check, especially in winter or with lifestyle changes.

**Functional High (91–109 ng/mL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It usually means high-dose supplements and is generally safe. To optimize: watch for signs of too much (nausea, vomiting, constipation, weakness, kidney stones). Check calcium and PTH (parathyroid hormone). Cut back on dose if needed. Keep taking co-factors (magnesium, K2, zinc) for healthy calcium balance.

**Clinical High (110 ng/mL and above):** Very high vitamin D (usually from too much supplementation) can cause hypercalcemia (high blood calcium). Symptoms: nausea, vomiting, constipation, weakness, confusion, kidney stones, and irregular heart beats. To address: stop vitamin D supplements and work with a provider. Stop calcium supplements. Drink plenty of water. Time usually clears mild-moderate elevations. Severe toxicity may need hospital care. Re-check vitamin D and calcium.

## AST (Aspartate Aminotransferase)

**What is this test?** AST is an enzyme (a protein that speeds up chemical reactions) found in your liver, heart, muscles, kidneys, brain, and red blood cells. It leaks into your blood when these tissues are damaged.

**Why does it matter?** It is one of the main markers for liver injury, but it can also be high with muscle injury or heart attack. ALT is a more liver-specific marker, so AST is usually looked at with ALT.

**Reference ranges (U/L, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–9  
- Functional Low: 10–12  
- **Optimal: 13–24**  
- Functional High: 25–35  
- Clinical High: 36 and above

**Clinical Low (below 10 U/L):** This is on the low end. It can mean low B6 (pyridoxine), because AST needs B6 to work. It can also happen in advanced liver disease with reduced liver function, and in kidney failure. You may feel tired and have other B6 deficiency symptoms (tingling in hands and feet, anemia, mood changes). To optimize: make sure you get enough B6 (chickpeas, tuna, salmon, chicken, bananas, potatoes, sunflower seeds). A provider can check B6, liver, and kidney.

**Functional Low (10–12 U/L):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means subclinical B6 deficiency, which is very common. Symptoms can be subtle fatigue, mood changes, anemia, and tingling in hands and feet. To optimize: eat B6-rich foods (chickpeas, tuna, salmon, chicken, bananas, potatoes, sunflower seeds). Consider B6 supplements (10–50 mg a day of P-5-P, the active form). A provider can check B6.

**Optimal (13–24 U/L):** This is the goal. It means your liver and muscles are healthy, you have enough B6, and there is little tissue damage. To stay here: keep up a liver-friendly, plant-centered diet (vegetables, fruits, leafy greens, beets, cruciferous vegetables). Limit alcohol. Avoid liver-toxic substances. Exercise most days. Periodically re-check, especially with new medicines or symptoms.

**Functional High (25–35 U/L):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It often means low-grade liver stress, mild fatty liver, muscle injury (recent hard exercise), alcohol use, certain medicines (statins for cholesterol, acetaminophen/Tylenol), or metabolic dysfunction. You may feel tired, foggy, or have right upper belly discomfort. To optimize: find and address the cause. Cut back on alcohol, refined sugar, and processed foods. Eat a liver-friendly, plant-centered diet. Exercise regularly. A provider can check liver, fasting insulin, and muscles.

**Clinical High (36 U/L and above):** High AST means liver, muscle, or other tissue injury. Causes: liver disease (hepatitis, fatty liver, cirrhosis, drug-induced liver injury, liver cancer), muscle injury (rhabdomyolysis, myositis, recent hard exercise, statins), heart attack, hemolysis, and certain genetic conditions. AST higher than ALT suggests alcoholic liver disease or muscle injury. ALT higher than AST suggests non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). To address: work with a provider. Find and treat the cause. Cut back on alcohol, processed foods, and liver-toxic medicines. Eat a liver-friendly, plant-centered diet and exercise.

## ALT (Alanine Aminotransferase)

**What is this test?** ALT is an enzyme found mostly in your liver. It is the most specific and sensitive marker for liver injury.

**Why does it matter?** It is the main marker for liver damage. High ALT is one of the most common abnormal lab findings and often signals fatty liver disease. It is usually looked at with AST.

**Reference ranges (U/L, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–5  
- Functional Low: 6–9  
- **Optimal: 10–23**  
- Functional High: 24–27  
- Clinical High: 28 and above

**Clinical Low (below 6 U/L):** This is on the low end. It can mean low B6 (pyridoxine), because ALT needs B6 to work. It can also happen in advanced liver disease with reduced liver function. To optimize: make sure you get enough B6 (chickpeas, tuna, salmon, chicken, bananas, potatoes, sunflower seeds). A provider can check B6 and liver.

**Functional Low (6–9 U/L):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means subclinical B6 deficiency, which is very common. To optimize: eat B6-rich foods. Consider B6 supplements (10–50 mg a day of P-5-P). A provider can check B6.

**Optimal (10–23 U/L):** This is the goal. It means your liver is healthy, your metabolic function is good, and there is little cell turnover. To stay here: keep up a liver-friendly, plant-centered diet (vegetables, fruits, leafy greens, beets, cruciferous vegetables, coffee). Limit alcohol. Avoid liver-toxic substances. Exercise most days. Maintain a healthy weight (belly fat drives fatty liver). Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (24–27 U/L):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It is the most sensitive early marker for non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), which affects up to 30% of adults. Causes: insulin resistance, obesity, metabolic syndrome, alcohol, certain medicines, viral hepatitis, and autoimmune hepatitis. You may feel tired, foggy, or have right upper belly discomfort. To optimize: find and address the cause. Cut back on refined sugar, processed foods, and alcohol. Eat a liver-friendly, plant-centered diet. Exercise regularly. Lose extra body fat. A provider can check fasting insulin, HOMA-IR (a calculation that estimates insulin resistance), liver imaging, and viral hepatitis.

**Clinical High (28 U/L and above):** High ALT means significant liver injury. Causes: NAFLD and NASH (non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, a more serious form of fatty liver — most common), alcoholic liver disease, viral hepatitis (B, C), drug-induced liver injury (acetaminophen, statins, certain herbs), autoimmune hepatitis, hemochromatosis, Wilson disease (a genetic disorder where copper builds up in the liver), alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (a genetic disorder that can cause liver and lung disease), and liver cancer. To address: work with a provider. Full workup usually includes viral hepatitis panel, autoimmune markers, iron studies, liver imaging, and possibly biopsy. Treatment depends on the cause. Lifestyle changes (diet, exercise, weight loss, alcohol cessation) are foundational. For NAFLD/NASH, losing 7–10% of your body weight can reverse the disease.

## Bilirubin (Direct / Conjugated)

**What is this test?** Bilirubin is a yellow pigment made when your body breaks down old red blood cells. Your liver processes bilirubin, attaches (conjugates) it to a sugar to make it water-soluble, and sends it out in bile (a fluid your liver makes to help digest fat). Direct (conjugated) bilirubin is the water-soluble form that has been processed by your liver.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you how well your liver and bile ducts are working. High direct bilirubin often means liver or bile duct disease.

**Reference ranges (mg/dL, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively as negative or positive. A negative result is the goal. A positive result (any detectable direct bilirubin in the blood) is a Clinical High that needs evaluation.

**Optimal (Negative):** A negative direct bilirubin is the goal. It means your liver is processing bilirubin well and your antioxidant capacity is good. To stay here: keep up a liver-friendly, plant-centered diet. Limit alcohol. Exercise most days. Periodically re-check.

**Clinical High (Positive):** A positive direct bilirubin means significant liver or bile duct disease. Causes: cholestasis (bile duct blockage, primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis), hepatitis (viral, alcoholic, autoimmune, drug-induced), cirrhosis, and inherited disorders (Dubin-Johnson, Rotor syndromes). To address: work with a provider. They will order imaging (ultrasound, MRCP — a special MRI that looks at bile ducts) and a full liver workup. Treat the cause. Limit alcohol and liver-toxic substances.

## Bilirubin, Total

**What is this test?** Total bilirubin is your direct (conjugated) plus indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin. Indirect bilirubin is the fat-soluble form that has not been processed by your liver yet.

**Why does it matter?** It is a useful marker of liver function, hemolysis, and bile duct health. Mildly high bilirubin is often benign (Gilbert syndrome), but it can also signal liver disease or hemolysis.

**Reference ranges (mg/dL, both sexes):**

- **Optimal: 0–1.5**  
- Functional High: 1.6–2.5  
- Clinical High: 2.6 and above

Note: This test does not have a Clinical Low or Functional Low category in the OHS ranges.

**Optimal (0–1.5 mg/dL):** This is the goal. It means your liver is working well, you have normal red blood cell turnover, and good antioxidant capacity. To stay here: keep up a liver-friendly, plant-centered diet. Limit alcohol. Exercise most days. Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (1.6–2.5 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean Gilbert syndrome (a common, harmless genetic condition affecting 5–10% of people, with mildly high unconjugated bilirubin that rises with stress, fasting, or illness), mild hemolysis, or early liver disease. Gilbert syndrome is benign and does not need treatment. To optimize: support your liver with a plant-centered diet. Limit alcohol. Drink plenty of water. A provider can check liver, gallbladder, and hemolysis markers.

**Clinical High (2.6 mg/dL and above):** High total bilirubin means liver, bile duct, or hemolytic disease. Causes: hemolytic anemia, viral hepatitis, alcoholic liver disease, drug-induced liver injury, cirrhosis, cholestasis, primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, and inherited disorders (Gilbert, Dubin-Johnson, Rotor — usually benign with mild elevation). To address: work with a provider. Full workup includes liver enzymes, viral hepatitis panel, autoimmune markers, hemolysis markers, and imaging. Treat the cause. Limit alcohol and liver-toxic substances.

## eGFR (Estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate)

**What is this test?** eGFR estimates how much blood your kidneys filter per minute. It is the best single marker of kidney function. eGFR is calculated from creatinine, age, sex, and sometimes race. Cystatin C-based eGFR is more accurate than creatinine-based eGFR, especially in muscular or older people.

**Why does it matter?** It is used to diagnose and stage chronic kidney disease (CKD). Kidney disease is often silent until it is advanced. Early detection is key.

**Reference ranges (mL/min/1.73m², both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–59  
- Functional Low: 60–64  
- **Optimal: 65–140**  
- Functional High: 141–150  
- Clinical High: above 150

**Clinical Low (below 60 mL/min/1.73m²):** This means chronic kidney disease (CKD). It is staged from 3a (45–59) to 5 (below 15, kidney failure). Causes: diabetes (most common), high blood pressure, glomerulonephritis (inflammation of the kidney's filtering units), polycystic kidney disease, long-term NSAID use, autoimmune disease (lupus), repeated kidney infections, and blockage. Symptoms are often subtle until advanced disease (fatigue, swelling, urination changes, itching, confusion). To address: work with a provider. Strict blood pressure and blood sugar control are foundational. Limit sodium, moderate protein, and limit phosphorus. Avoid kidney-toxic substances (NSAIDs, contrast dye for imaging, certain antibiotics). A kidney specialist (nephrologist) is usually needed for stage 3 and above.

**Functional Low (60–64 mL/min/1.73m²):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early kidney dysfunction from diabetes, high blood pressure, insulin resistance, NSAID use, dehydration, or aging. You are at higher risk of progressive kidney disease. To optimize: find and address the cause. Strict blood pressure and blood sugar control are foundational. Limit sodium and processed foods. Stay well hydrated. Avoid kidney-toxic substances. Eat a plant-centered diet rich in vegetables, fruits, and beans (linked to slower CKD progression). A provider can check fasting insulin, blood pressure, and cystatin C.

**Optimal (65–140 mL/min/1.73m²):** This is the goal. It means your kidneys are filtering blood well. To stay here: keep up a whole-food, plant-centered diet. Stay well hydrated. Limit sodium and processed foods. Keep blood pressure and blood sugar in a healthy range. Avoid too much NSAID use. Exercise most days. Periodically re-check, especially with diabetes, high blood pressure, or family history of kidney disease.

**Functional High (141–150 mL/min/1.73m²):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It usually means your kidneys are working great. It can also happen in pregnancy and with very high muscle mass (which can make eGFR inaccurate). To maintain: keep up a whole-food diet, hydration, and healthy lifestyle. Periodic monitoring is enough.

**Clinical High (above 150 mL/min/1.73m²):** This is rare. It usually means hyperfiltration (the kidneys are filtering too much), which is an early sign of diabetic kidney disease. It can also be from inaccuracy due to very low creatinine (muscular dystrophy, severe liver disease). To address: work with a provider. Optimize blood sugar and blood pressure. A provider can check fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, and cystatin C.

## Creatinine

**What is this test?** Creatinine is a waste product of muscle metabolism. Your kidneys filter it out of your blood and put it in your urine. Serum creatinine is the most common marker of kidney function.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you how well your kidneys are working. It is heavily affected by muscle mass — muscular people have higher levels, and elderly or sedentary people have lower levels. Cystatin C is a more accurate marker of kidney function, especially in muscular or older people.

**Reference ranges (mg/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–0.56  
- Functional Low: 0.57–0.74  
- **Optimal: 0.75–1.2**  
- Functional High: 1.21–1.35  
- Clinical High: above 1.35

**Clinical Low (below 0.57 mg/dL):** Low creatinine is usually harmless and means low muscle mass (elderly, sedentary, malnutrition) or hyperfiltration. It can also happen in severe liver disease. You may have sarcopenia (muscle loss), frailty, and reduced functional capacity. To optimize: do strength training to build muscle. Eat 1.0–1.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight. Treat any underlying liver disease or malnutrition.

**Functional Low (0.57–0.74 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means low muscle mass, sedentary lifestyle, or mild malnutrition. You may have subtle sarcopenia and reduced metabolic capacity. To optimize: do strength training. Eat enough protein. Stay active. A provider can check body composition and nutrition.

**Optimal (0.75–1.2 mg/dL):** This is the goal. It means your kidneys are filtering well and you have enough muscle mass. To stay here: keep up a whole-food diet, regular strength training, enough protein, and good hydration. Avoid kidney-toxic substances. Periodically re-check, especially with diabetes, high blood pressure, or family history of kidney disease.

**Functional High (1.21–1.35 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean high muscle mass (athletic), high protein intake, mild dehydration, or early kidney dysfunction. You may have slightly higher kidney risk if it is from a kidney cause. To optimize: drink plenty of water, moderate your protein intake, and check kidney function with cystatin C and eGFR. A provider can check kidney function, hydration, and body composition.

**Clinical High (above 1.35 mg/dL):** High creatinine means reduced kidney filtration and possible kidney disease. Causes: chronic kidney disease (most common), acute kidney injury, dehydration, high muscle mass (rarely clinically significant), rhabdomyolysis (muscle breakdown), certain medicines (NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, diuretics), urinary blockage, and high-protein diets. To address: work with a provider. Find and treat the cause. Drink plenty of water. Avoid kidney-toxic substances. For chronic kidney disease, manage blood pressure and blood sugar strictly. A kidney specialist may be needed.

## Urea Nitrogen (BUN)

**What is this test?** BUN (Blood Urea Nitrogen) measures the amount of urea nitrogen in your blood. Urea is a waste product of protein metabolism. Your liver makes it, and your kidneys get rid of it. BUN reflects both kidney function and how much protein you eat.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you about kidney function and protein metabolism. High BUN can mean kidney problems, dehydration, or a high-protein diet. Low BUN can mean malnutrition or liver problems.

**Reference ranges (mg/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–5  
- Functional Low: 6–9  
- **Optimal: 10–22**  
- Functional High: 23–25  
- Clinical High: 26 and above

**Clinical Low (below 6 mg/dL):** Low BUN is rare. It can mean not enough protein in your diet, malnutrition, severe liver disease (your liver makes less urea), or over-hydration. It can also happen in pregnancy and with certain medicines (growth hormone, anabolic steroids). You may have malnutrition, muscle loss, and reduced detox capacity. To optimize: eat enough protein (0.8–1.2 grams per kilogram of body weight). Treat any underlying liver disease, malnutrition, or over-hydration.

**Functional Low (6–9 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means not enough protein, low muscle mass, mild liver problems, or over-hydration. You may have subtle malnutrition, muscle loss, and reduced metabolic capacity. To optimize: eat enough protein from whole-food sources (beans, fish, eggs, poultry, Greek yogurt). Do strength training. Treat any underlying liver issues. A provider can check nutrition, muscle mass, and liver.

**Optimal (10–22 mg/dL):** This is the goal. It means balanced protein metabolism, healthy liver urea production, and well-functioning kidney excretion. To stay here: keep up a whole-food diet with enough protein (0.8–1.2 grams per kilogram of body weight). Stay well hydrated. Avoid too much alcohol. Periodically re-check, especially with new symptoms.

**Functional High (23–25 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean high protein intake, mild dehydration, mild kidney problems, certain medicines (steroids, diuretics), or heart failure. You may have slightly more kidney stress. To optimize: drink plenty of water, moderate your protein intake, and check kidney function. A provider can check kidney function, hydration, and dietary protein.

**Clinical High (26 mg/dL and above):** High BUN means increased urea production or reduced kidney excretion. Causes: kidney disease, dehydration, high protein intake, GI bleeding, heart failure, certain medicines, and rarely urea cycle disorders (rare genetic disorders where the body cannot get rid of ammonia). The BUN/creatinine ratio helps tell pre-renal (dehydration, GI bleeding — ratio above 20\) from renal (kidney disease — ratio 10–20) causes. To address: work with a provider. Find and treat the cause. For dehydration, drink more fluids. For kidney disease, manage blood pressure and blood sugar. For GI bleeding, urgent evaluation. For high protein intake, moderate the amount of protein you eat

## BUN/Creatinine Ratio

**What is this test?** The BUN/creatinine ratio is your BUN divided by creatinine. It helps tell pre-renal causes of high BUN (dehydration, GI bleeding, heart failure — ratio above 20\) from renal causes (kidney disease — ratio 10–20) or post-renal causes (blockage).

**Why does it matter?** It is a simple way to help figure out the cause of an elevated BUN. A high ratio points to dehydration, GI bleeding, or heart failure. A normal ratio with elevated BUN points to kidney disease.

**Reference ranges (ratio, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–5.99  
- Functional Low: 6.0–9.99  
- **Optimal: 10.0–20.99**  
- Functional High: 21.0–23.99  
- Clinical High: 24.0 and above

**Clinical Low (below 6.0):** This is rare. It usually means not enough protein, malnutrition, severe liver disease (low urea production), over-hydration, or rarely SIADH. You may have malnutrition, muscle loss, and reduced detox capacity. To optimize: eat enough protein, treat any underlying liver disease, and check fluid balance.

**Functional Low (6.0–9.99):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means mild malnutrition, low protein intake, mild liver problems, or over-hydration. You may have subtle malnutrition and reduced metabolic capacity. To optimize: eat enough protein, do strength training, and treat any underlying liver issues.

**Optimal (10.0–20.99):** This is the goal. It means balanced protein metabolism, healthy liver and kidney function, and good hydration. To stay here: keep up a whole-food diet with enough protein, stay well hydrated, and avoid too much alcohol. Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (21.0–23.99):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean mild dehydration, high protein intake, mild heart failure, certain medicines (steroids, diuretics), or early kidney problems. To optimize: drink plenty of water, moderate your protein intake, and check kidney and heart function. A provider can check kidney function, hydration, and heart health.

**Clinical High (24.0 and above):** This means pre-renal azotemia — increased BUN from dehydration, GI bleeding, heart failure, or catabolic states (steroids, infection). Causes of GI bleeding include ulcers, gastritis, varices, and cancer. To address: work with a provider urgently if GI bleeding is suspected. For dehydration, IV or oral fluids. For heart failure, optimize heart failure treatment. For catabolic states, treat the underlying cause.

## Color (Urine)

**What is this test?** Urine color shows how concentrated your urine is and can give you clues about your hydration, diet, and health. Normal urine is yellow. The yellow color comes from urochromes, which are made when your body breaks down hemoglobin.

**Why does it matter?** It is a quick, free way to check your hydration. Unusual colors (red, brown, black, green, milky) can be a sign of specific conditions, foods, or medicines.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test. This test is reported qualitatively. A normal yellow color is the goal. A "Functional High" or "Clinical High" usually refers to unusual or abnormal colors that need follow-up.

**Optimal (Yellow):** An optimal urine color is pale to straw yellow, which means you are well hydrated and your kidneys are working well. To stay here: drink to thirst (usually 2–3 liters a day, more with exercise and heat). Make sure you have enough electrolytes. Eat a colorful plant-based diet. Check your urine color periodically as a quick hydration check.

**Clinical High (Unusual Colors):** Unusual urine colors need evaluation. Red/brown urine can mean blood (hematuria), myoglobin (from rhabdomyolysis), hemoglobin (from hemolysis), porphyria (a group of rare genetic disorders), beets, or rifampin (an antibiotic). Dark brown/black can mean liver disease, melanoma, or certain medicines. Green/blue can mean a Pseudomonas infection (a type of bacteria), certain medicines, or food dyes. Milky/white can mean chyluria (lymph fluid in urine, from lymphatic blockage) or pyuria (white blood cells in urine, from UTI). To address: work with a provider. Find and treat the cause. Drink more water.

## Appearance (Urine)

**What is this test?** Urine appearance describes how clear your urine is — clear, slightly cloudy, cloudy, or turbid.

**Why does it matter?** It can be a quick sign of infection, crystals, mucus, or other things in the urine.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test. This test is reported qualitatively. A clear appearance is the goal. A "Functional High" or "Clinical High" usually refers to cloudy or turbid urine that may need follow-up.

**Optimal (Clear):** Optimal urine is clear with no visible cloudiness, sediment, or particles. It means your kidneys are working well, there is no infection, and you are well hydrated. To stay here: keep drinking to thirst. Eat a whole-food diet. Support your kidney and bladder health. Periodic checks are enough.

**Clinical High (Cloudy/Turbid):** Cloudy or turbid urine can mean a urinary tract infection (most common), pyuria (white blood cells), hematuria (red blood cells), crystals (uric acid, phosphate, oxalate), chyluria (lymph fluid), mucus, or heavy sediment. To address: work with a provider. They will order a urinalysis and urine culture. Treat the underlying infection. Drink more water. If recurrent, check for chronic UTI, interstitial cystitis (a chronic bladder pain condition), or other conditions.

## Specific Gravity (Urine)

**What is this test?** Urine specific gravity measures how concentrated your urine is compared to water. It shows how well your kidneys can concentrate and dilute urine.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you about your hydration and kidney function. High specific gravity means concentrated urine (often from dehydration). Low specific gravity means dilute urine (often from over-hydration or kidney problems).

**Reference ranges (ratio, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–0.93  
- Functional Low: 0.93–1.0  
- **Optimal: 1.0–1.03**  
- Functional High: 1.03–1.06  
- Clinical High: above 1.06

**Clinical Low (below 0.93):** Very dilute urine can mean over-hydration, diabetes insipidus (kidneys cannot concentrate urine), kidney disease (impaired concentrating ability), or diuretics. Persistent very dilute urine can cause electrolyte imbalances and raise UTI risk. To address: work with a provider. Find and treat the cause. Moderate your fluid intake.

**Functional Low (0.93–1.0):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means over-hydration or mild concentrating problems. You may be drinking too much water and diluting important electrolytes. To optimize: moderate your fluid intake (drink to thirst, usually 2–3 liters a day) and make sure you have enough electrolytes. A provider can check kidney concentrating ability if it stays low.

**Optimal (1.0–1.03):** This is the goal. It means balanced hydration, normal kidney concentrating ability, and well-regulated fluid balance. To stay here: drink to thirst (usually 2–3 liters a day, more with exercise and heat). Make sure you have enough electrolytes (sea salt, electrolytes in water if you drink a lot). Eat a whole-food diet. Periodic checks are a useful hydration marker.

**Functional High (1.03–1.06):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It often means mild dehydration, high protein intake, or concentrated morning urine. You may be slightly dehydrated. To optimize: drink more water and make sure you have enough electrolytes. If it stays high with normal fluid intake, check kidney concentrating ability.

**Clinical High (above 1.06):** Very concentrated urine usually means dehydration. Other causes: high protein intake, certain medicines, heart failure, liver disease, SIADH, and adrenal insufficiency. Very concentrated urine can raise the risk of kidney stones and UTIs. To address: work with a provider. Drink more water. Find and treat the cause. For kidney stone prevention, aim for specific gravity below 1.020 and urine output above 2 liters a day.

## pH (Urine)

**What is this test?** Urine pH measures how acidic or alkaline your urine is.

**Why does it matter?** It reflects your diet (acidic urine with animal protein, alkaline urine with plant foods), acid-base balance, kidney function, and certain infections or medicines. Urine pH affects kidney stone risk — uric acid stones form in acidic urine, and struvite stones form in alkaline urine.

**Reference ranges (pH, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–2.99  
- Functional Low: 3.0–4.99  
- **Optimal: 5.0–8.0**  
- Functional High: 8.01–8.99  
- Clinical High: 9.0 and above

**Clinical Low (below 3.0):** Very acidic urine is unusual. It can mean severe metabolic acidosis, uncontrolled diabetes with ketoacidosis, certain poisonings, prolonged fasting, and certain medicines. Persistent very acidic urine raises the risk of uric acid kidney stones. To optimize: work with a provider to find and treat the cause. Increase plant foods (vegetables, fruits, beans). Cut back on animal protein and processed foods. Consider alkaline water or potassium citrate.

**Functional Low (3.0–4.99):** This is in the lab range but on the acidic side. It often means a high-protein, low-vegetable diet or mild metabolic acidosis. You may have slightly higher risk of uric acid stones and bone loss over time. To optimize: eat more plant foods (vegetables, fruits, beans). Cut back on animal protein. Eat an alkaline-promoting diet.

**Optimal (5.0–8.0):** This is the goal. It means balanced acid-base status, healthy kidney function, and a plant-prominent diet. To stay here: keep up a plant-centered diet rich in vegetables, fruits, leafy greens, and beans. Limit processed foods and excess animal protein. Drink plenty of water. Periodically re-check, especially with diet changes.

**Functional High (8.01–8.99):** This is in the lab range but on the alkaline side. It often means a plant-heavy diet (usually healthy), a recent meal, or certain medicines (acetazolamide, potassium citrate). It can also mean a UTI with urea-splitting bacteria. To optimize: keep a balanced diet. If it stays very alkaline and you have symptoms, check for UTI.

**Clinical High (9.0 and above):** Very alkaline urine can mean a UTI with urea-splitting bacteria (Proteus, Klebsiella), renal tubular acidosis type 1, milk-alkali syndrome, certain medicines, or long-term vomiting. Alkaline urine raises the risk of struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate) kidney stones. To address: work with a provider. Treat any UTI. Acidify urine with vitamin C, cranberry, or betaine HCl in some cases. Cut back on excess plant intake if it is very alkaline.

## Reducing Substances (Urine)

**What is this test?** This test looks for reducing agents in your urine. The most common is glucose (sugar), which suggests diabetes. Other reducing agents include fructose, galactose, lactose, certain medicines, and ascorbic acid (vitamin C).

**Why does it matter?** Positive reducing substances in urine can mean diabetes, pregnancy-related sugar in urine, or rare metabolic disorders.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative result is the goal. A positive result needs evaluation.

**Optimal (Negative):** A negative test is the goal. It means normal sugar metabolism and no abnormal sugars in urine. To maintain: keep up a high-fiber, plant-centered diet, exercise, and healthy body composition. Periodic blood sugar testing is a better check.

**Clinical High (Positive):** A positive test needs evaluation. Causes: diabetes (glucose — most common), pregnancy (lactose), galactosemia, fructose intolerance, and certain medicines. Glucose in urine is a late sign of high blood sugar. To address: work with a provider. Find and treat the cause. For diabetes, optimize blood sugar management.

## Occult Blood (Urine)

**What is this test?** This test looks for blood in your urine that you cannot see (hidden blood). It can be intact red blood cells (hematuria), free hemoglobin, or myoglobin (from muscle breakdown).

**Why does it matter?** Any hidden blood in urine is a sign of bleeding, infection, stones, or — rarely — cancer. Painless hidden blood, especially in older adults, can be the first sign of bladder cancer.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative result is the goal. A positive result needs evaluation.

**Optimal (Negative):** A negative test is the goal. It means no blood in your urine. To maintain: keep up a whole-food diet, stay well hydrated, support your kidney and urinary tract health, and avoid kidney-toxic substances. Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High (Positive):** A positive test needs evaluation. Causes: UTI (most common), kidney stones, bladder cancer (especially in smokers and older adults), kidney disease (glomerulonephritis), prostate enlargement or cancer, recent hard exercise, trauma, and menstrual contamination. Hematuria with protein and dysmorphic red blood cells (red blood cells that look misshapen under a microscope) suggests glomerular disease. Painless hematuria in older adults needs a cystoscopy (a test where a small camera is used to look inside the bladder) to rule out bladder cancer. To address: work with a provider. They will order urine culture, imaging (ultrasound, CT), cystoscopy, and possibly kidney biopsy. Treat the cause. Drink more water.

## Protein (Urine)

**What is this test?** This test measures the amount of protein in your urine. Normally, your kidneys filter very little protein, so urine protein should be negative or trace. Protein in urine (proteinuria) can mean kidney disease, but it can also be brief from exercise, fever, dehydration, or stress. Microalbumin (small amounts of albumin) is an early sign of kidney damage in diabetes and high blood pressure.

**Why does it matter?** Persistent proteinuria is a strong warning sign for kidney disease progression and heart disease. The earlier you catch it, the more you can do.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):**

- **Optimal: 0 (Negative)**  
- Clinical High: 0.01 and above (Positive)

Note: This test does not have a Clinical Low, Functional Low, or Functional High category in the OHS ranges. Anything above 0 is a Clinical High.

**Optimal (0, Negative):** Optimal urine protein is negative. It means your kidney filtration is healthy. To stay here: keep up a whole-food, plant-centered diet. Stay well hydrated. Keep blood pressure below 120/80 and HbA1c below 5.4%. Avoid kidney-toxic substances (NSAIDs, contrast dye). Exercise most days. Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High (0.01 and above, Positive):** A positive test needs evaluation. Causes: diabetic nephropathy, hypertensive nephropathy, glomerulonephritis, IgA nephropathy, lupus nephritis, polycystic kidney disease, multiple myeloma, pre-eclampsia, UTI, and certain medicines. Persistent proteinuria is a strong risk factor for progressive kidney disease and heart disease. To address: work with a provider. They will order 24-hour urine protein, microalbumin/creatinine ratio, blood tests, and possibly kidney biopsy. Treat the cause. Strict blood pressure and blood sugar control. ACE inhibitors or ARBs (common blood pressure medicines) are often used for proteinuria. Limit sodium.

## Nitrite (Urine)

**What is this test?** Nitrite in urine is made when bacteria (usually gram-negative bacteria like E. coli, Klebsiella, Proteus) change nitrates into nitrites in the bladder. A positive test strongly suggests a bacterial urinary tract infection.

**Why does it matter?** It is a quick sign of a UTI. Combined with leukocyte esterase, it gives a strong case for treating a UTI.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative result is the goal. A positive result needs evaluation.

**Optimal (Negative):** Optimal urine is nitrite-negative. It means no bacterial UTI. To stay here: drink enough water (2–3 liters a day), practice good hygiene, urinate after sex (for women), and avoid holding urine. Cranberry, D-mannose (a natural sugar that may help prevent UTIs), and probiotics can support urinary tract health. Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High (Positive):** A positive test means a bacterial UTI. Common bacteria: E. coli (most common), Klebsiella, Proteus, and Pseudomonas. UTIs cause painful urination, urgency, frequency, lower belly pain, and sometimes blood in urine and fever (if upper tract). To address: work with a provider. Urine culture and sensitivity testing guide antibiotics. Drink more water, take D-mannose, cranberry, and probiotics. For recurrent UTIs, look for underlying causes (anatomy, hormones, microbiome).

**What is this test?** Leukocyte esterase is an enzyme made by white blood cells (neutrophils). A positive test means pyuria (white blood cells in the urine), which usually means inflammation or infection — most often a UTI.

**Why does it matter?** It is a quick sign of inflammation or infection in the urinary tract. Combined with nitrite, it gives a strong case for treating a UTI.

**Functional High (2.01–3):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean early or mild pyuria. Common causes: early UTI, mild inflammation, or contamination. To optimize: drink more water, support urinary tract health with D-mannose, cranberry, and probiotics, and check with a provider if you have symptoms. A provider can check urinary tract, gut, and immune health.

## RBC \- Urinalysis (Red Blood Cells in Urine)

**What is this test?** This test counts the number of red blood cells in your urine, usually reported as cells per high-power field (hpf).

**Why does it matter?** It tells you about bleeding in the urinary tract. Hematuria can be gross (visible) or microscopic (only seen on testing). Microscopic hematuria can mean infection, stones, cancer, or glomerular disease.

- Functional High: 2.01–4  
- Clinical High: 4.01 and above

**What is this test?** Squamous epithelial cells are large, flat cells that line the lower urinary tract, vagina, and skin. They are commonly seen in urine and usually mean contamination from skin or vaginal cells during collection, not disease.

## Calcium Oxalate Crystals (Urine)

**What is this test?** This test looks for calcium oxalate crystals in your urine. They are the most common type of crystal found in urine. They can look like envelopes (dihydrate form) or dumbbells (monohydrate form). Small amounts are normal, especially in concentrated urine. Larger amounts can mean a risk of calcium oxalate kidney stones.

**Why does it matter?** Calcium oxalate is the most common type of kidney stone. Knowing if you have these crystals helps you understand your stone risk and what to do about it.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative result is the goal. A positive result (especially with symptoms or history of stones) needs follow-up.

**Optimal (Negative):** A negative test is the goal. It means balanced oxalate and calcium metabolism with enough hydration. To maintain: keep up enough water (2–3 liters a day), a balanced diet with moderate oxalate, and enough calcium with meals (calcium binds oxalate in the gut and reduces absorption).

**Clinical High (Positive, especially with symptoms):** A high crystal count is a big risk factor for calcium oxalate kidney stones, the most common type. Causes: hyperoxaluria (from diet, malabsorption, or bariatric surgery), hypercalciuria (idiopathic — no known cause, hyperparathyroidism, sarcoidosis), low urine volume, hypocitraturia (low citrate, a natural stone inhibitor), and rare genetic disorders (primary hyperoxaluria). Symptoms of kidney stones: severe flank pain, blood in urine, nausea, and vomiting. To address: work with a provider. A 24-hour urine collection for stone risk is often done. Drink more water (urine output above 2.5 liters a day). Cut back on oxalate. Make sure you get enough calcium. Increase citrate (lemons, limes). Consider thiazide diuretics (medicines that lower urine calcium) or potassium citrate in some cases.

## Crystals (Urine, General)

**What is this test?** This test looks for any type of crystal in your urine. Common types include calcium oxalate, calcium phosphate, uric acid, struvite (triple phosphate), cystine, and ammonium biurate. The type and amount help identify metabolic problems and kidney stone risk.

**Why does it matter?** Different crystals point to different conditions. Uric acid crystals suggest acidic urine, gout, or high purine intake. Calcium oxalate is the most common. Struvite suggests a UTI with urea-splitting bacteria. Cystine suggests a genetic disorder (cystinuria).

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test. This test is reported qualitatively. The test section appears without specific level ranges in the OHS file, indicating it is reported as present/absent without graded categories.

**Optimal (None to rare):** This is the goal. It means healthy urine chemistry with low kidney stone risk. To stay here: keep up enough water (2+ liters a day). Eat a balanced diet with moderate amounts of stone-forming foods. Make sure you get enough citrate (lemons, limes, citrus fruits). Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High (Many, with symptoms or risk factors):** A high crystal count is linked to higher risk of kidney stones. Different crystals mean different conditions: uric acid (acidic urine, gout, high purine diet), calcium oxalate (most common, hyperoxaluria, hypercalciuria), calcium phosphate (alkaline urine, hyperparathyroidism), struvite/triple phosphate (UTI with urea-splitting bacteria), cystine (cystinuria, genetic). To address: work with a provider. A 24-hour urine collection guides treatment. Drink more water. Change your diet based on crystal type. Consider medicines (thiazides, potassium citrate, allopurinol).

## Hyaline Cast (Urine)

**What is this test?** Hyaline casts are tube-shaped structures made of Tamm-Horsfall protein (a protein made in your kidney tubules). Small numbers are normal, especially with exercise, dehydration, or concentrated urine. Larger numbers can mean reduced blood flow to the kidneys, dehydration, exercise, or certain kidney conditions.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you about kidney health. Hyaline casts alone are usually benign (often from dehydration or exercise), but they can be a sign of reduced blood flow to the kidneys.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative or rare result is the goal. A high count needs evaluation.

**Optimal (None to rare):** This is the goal. It means healthy kidney tubules, enough hydration, and well-regulated kidney health. To stay here: keep up enough water, balanced exercise (avoid overtraining), and supportive kidney health.

**Clinical High (Many, with other abnormal findings):** A high hyaline cast count with proteinuria, hematuria, or elevated creatinine suggests kidney disease. Causes include dehydration (most common), hard exercise, reduced blood flow to kidneys (heart failure, shock), diuretics, and chronic kidney disease. To address: work with a provider. Drink more water. Moderate exercise. Check kidney function. Treat underlying heart failure or other conditions.

## Casts (Urine, General)

**What is this test?** This test looks for tube-shaped structures (casts) in your urine. Casts form in the kidney tubules from Tamm-Horsfall protein and trapped cells or substances. The type of cast gives important information — hyaline (dehydration, exercise), granular (kidney injury), red blood cell (glomerulonephritis), white blood cell (kidney infection, interstitial nephritis), epithelial (acute tubular necrosis — sudden damage to the kidney tubules), fatty (nephrotic syndrome — a group of symptoms including very high protein in urine), waxy (chronic kidney disease), and broad (advanced kidney disease).

**Why does it matter?** The type of cast helps your provider figure out what is going on in your kidneys. Some casts are benign (hyaline), while others point to serious kidney problems.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test. This test is reported qualitatively. The test section appears without specific level ranges in the OHS file, indicating it is reported as present/absent without graded categories.

**Optimal (None to rare):** This is the goal. It means healthy kidney tubules. To stay here: keep up enough water, balanced exercise, and supportive kidney health (low sodium, plant-centered diet, controlled blood pressure and blood sugar). Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High:** A high cast count, especially with non-hyaline casts, means significant kidney injury. RBC casts suggest glomerulonephritis. WBC casts suggest kidney infection or interstitial nephritis (inflammation of the kidney's tubules and surrounding tissue). Granular casts suggest acute tubular necrosis. Fatty casts suggest nephrotic syndrome. Waxy and broad casts suggest chronic kidney disease. To address: work with a provider. They will order a full kidney workup including creatinine, BUN, eGFR, cystatin C, urine protein, and possibly kidney biopsy. Treat the underlying cause.

## Amorphous Sediment (Urine)

**What is this test?** This test looks for amorphous sediment (a general term for non-crystalline material) in your urine. It includes things like urates, phosphates, and carbonates. Small amounts are normal, especially in concentrated urine.

**Why does it matter?** It mostly tells you about hydration and urine pH. Larger amounts often mean concentrated urine (dehydration) or very alkaline/acidic urine.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A "Functional High" or "Clinical High" usually refers to large amounts of sediment that may need follow-up.

**Optimal (None to rare):** This is the goal. It means enough hydration and balanced urine chemistry. To stay here: keep up enough water, balanced diet, and supportive kidney health. Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High:** A high amorphous sediment count usually means concentrated or alkaline/acidic urine. It is rarely a problem on its own. To address: drink more water. Check acid-base balance and diet. If it stays high with other abnormalities, work with a provider.

## Transitional Epithelial Cells (Urine)

**What is this test?** Transitional epithelial cells (also called urothelial cells) line the bladder, ureters, and renal pelvis (the inside of the kidney where urine collects). Small amounts can be normal, especially in older adults. Larger amounts can mean irritation, infection, stones, or rarely cancer.

**Why does it matter?** It can point to bladder, ureter, or kidney irritation or disease. It is especially important if you have blood in your urine.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative or rare result is the goal. A high count, especially with blood in urine, needs evaluation.

**Optimal (None to rare):** This is the goal. It means healthy bladder, ureters, and renal pelvis. To stay here: keep up enough water, a whole-food diet, and supportive urinary tract health. Avoid smoking (a major risk factor for bladder cancer). Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High:** A high count, especially with blood in urine, needs evaluation. Causes: bladder cancer (especially in smokers, older adults, those with chemical exposures), bladder stones, chronic UTI, indwelling catheter (a catheter that stays in the bladder for a long time), recent urinary tract procedure, and rarely other urothelial cancers. To address: work with a provider. They will order a cystoscopy (a small camera used to look inside the bladder), urine cytology (a test that looks at cells in urine for cancer), and imaging. Quit smoking. Treat the underlying condition. Drink more water.

## Renal Epithelial Cells (Urine)

**What is this test?** Renal epithelial cells (also called tubular cells) line the kidney tubules. They are not normally found in urine in significant numbers. Their presence means the kidney tubules are injured.

**Why does it matter?** It is a sign of kidney tubular injury. Any significant number needs evaluation.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative or rare result is the goal. A high count means kidney injury.

**Optimal (None to rare):** This is the goal. It means healthy kidney tubules with no significant injury. To stay here: keep up enough water, balanced diet, healthy blood pressure and blood sugar, and avoid kidney-toxic substances. Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High:** A high count means significant kidney tubular injury. Causes: acute tubular necrosis (sudden kidney tubular damage from lack of blood flow or toxins — most common), acute interstitial nephritis (sudden inflammation of the kidney's tubules and surrounding tissue), glomerulonephritis, kidney infection, kidney transplant rejection, and exposure to kidney toxins (contrast dye for imaging, aminoglycosides, heavy metals). To address: work with a provider. They will do a full kidney workup. Treat the cause. Avoid kidney-toxic substances. A kidney specialist may be needed.

## Triple Phosphate Crystals (Urine)

**What is this test?** This test looks for triple phosphate crystals (also called struvite, or magnesium ammonium phosphate) in your urine. They look like coffin lids under the microscope. They form in alkaline urine and are linked to UTIs with bacteria that produce urease (an enzyme that breaks urea into ammonia, which makes urine alkaline), such as Proteus, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas, and Ureaplasma. They are also a major part of struvite kidney stones.

**Why does it matter?** Triple phosphate crystals and struvite stones can grow large and form staghorn calculi (stones that fill the kidney's collecting system). They are a sign of chronic UTI with certain bacteria.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative result is the goal. A positive result (especially with symptoms) needs follow-up.

**Optimal (Negative):** This is the goal. It means your urine pH is not too alkaline and there is no urease-producing bacterial infection. To stay here: keep up enough water, good hygiene, urinate after sex (for women), and avoid holding urine. Cranberry, D-mannose, and probiotics can help. Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High (Positive, especially with symptoms):** A high count, especially with symptoms (painful urination, urgency, frequency, flank pain) or recurrent UTIs, needs evaluation. Causes: chronic UTI with urease-producing bacteria (Proteus most common), struvite kidney stones (can grow large and form staghorn calculi), and persistently alkaline urine. To address: work with a provider. Urine culture guides antibiotics. Struvite stones may need surgical removal (lithotripsy — using sound waves to break up stones, or percutaneous nephrolithotomy — surgery through the back to remove stones). Drink more water. Acidify urine. Treat the underlying UTI. Cranberry, D-mannose, and probiotics can help prevent recurrence.

## Granular Cast (Urine)

**What is this test?** This test looks for granular casts in your urine. These are tube-shaped casts that contain granules from cell debris, plasma proteins, or clumped Tamm-Horsfall protein. "Fine granular" casts suggest milder injury. "Coarse granular" casts (sometimes called "muddy brown casts") suggest more severe injury, often acute tubular necrosis.

**Why does it matter?** Granular casts usually mean the kidney tubules are injured. Coarse granular casts are a classic sign of acute tubular necrosis, a serious kidney condition.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative or rare result is the goal. A high count means kidney injury.

**Optimal (None):** This is the goal. It means healthy kidney tubules with no injury. To stay here: keep up enough water, balanced diet, healthy blood pressure and blood sugar, and avoid kidney-toxic substances. Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High:** A high count means kidney tubular injury. Causes: acute tubular necrosis (most common — from lack of blood flow, sepsis, or kidney toxins), acute interstitial nephritis, glomerulonephritis, kidney infection, and chronic kidney disease. Coarse granular casts ("muddy brown casts") are classic for acute tubular necrosis. To address: work with a provider. They will do a full kidney workup. Treat the cause. Avoid kidney-toxic substances. A kidney specialist may be needed.

## Yeast (Urine)

**What is this test?** This test looks for yeast in your urine, most commonly Candida (a type of fungus). It can mean a Candida UTI, vaginal contamination, or rarely a widespread Candida infection.

**Why does it matter?** Candida UTIs are more common in people with catheters, diabetes, weak immune systems, and those on broad-spectrum antibiotics (antibiotics that kill many types of bacteria).

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative result is the goal. A positive result (especially with symptoms) needs evaluation.

**Optimal (Negative):** This is the goal. It means no Candida in your urinary tract. To stay here: keep up a whole-food, low-sugar diet (sugar feeds Candida), supportive immune and gut health, and good hygiene. Avoid unnecessary antibiotics. Probiotics can help keep a healthy microbiome. Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High:** A high count, especially with symptoms (painful urination, urgency, frequency) or risk factors (catheter, diabetes, weak immune system, broad-spectrum antibiotics), means a Candida UTI. To address: work with a provider. Urine culture confirms the diagnosis. Antifungal therapy (fluconazole, amphotericin B for severe cases) is usually needed. Address underlying risk factors. Cut back on sugar. Probiotics and antifungal herbs can help. For recurrent Candida UTIs, check for candidiasis, diabetes, and immune problems.

## Uric Acid Crystals (Urine)

**What is this test?** This test looks for uric acid crystals in your urine. They can be diamond, rhombic, or rosette-shaped under the microscope. They form in acidic urine. They can be normal in small amounts, especially with a purine-rich diet or gout. Larger amounts raise the risk of uric acid kidney stones.

**Why does it matter?** Uric acid crystals in urine can mean gout, metabolic syndrome, high cell turnover (chemotherapy, psoriasis), or a high-purine diet. Uric acid kidney stones are a known complication.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative or rare result is the goal. A high count, especially with symptoms or history, needs follow-up.

**Optimal (None to rare):** This is the goal. It means balanced purine metabolism, healthy urine pH, and low kidney stone risk. To stay here: keep up enough water (2+ liters a day). Eat a balanced diet with moderate purine intake. Eat alkaline-promoting foods (vegetables, fruits, citrus). Avoid alcohol (especially beer). Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High (Many, with symptoms or risk factors):** A high count, especially with symptoms (flank pain, blood in urine, gout attacks) or persistently acidic urine, is linked to higher risk of uric acid kidney stones. Causes: gout, metabolic syndrome, high-purine diet, dehydration, high cell turnover (chemo, psoriasis), chronic diarrhea (loss of alkali), and rare genetic disorders (Lesch-Nyhan syndrome, very rare). To address: work with a provider. A 24-hour urine collection for stone risk is often done. Drink more water. Alkalize urine (potassium citrate is standard). Cut back on purines. Lose extra weight. Avoid alcohol. For gout, allopurinol or febuxostat may be needed.

## Fibrinogen Activity

**What is this test?** Fibrinogen is a protein made by your liver. It is essential for blood clotting. It turns into fibrin during the clotting cascade (the series of steps your body uses to form a clot). Fibrinogen is also an acute phase reactant, meaning it rises with inflammation.

**Why does it matter?** High fibrinogen is an independent risk factor for heart disease, stroke, and blood clots. It tells you about clotting risk and inflammation at the same time.

**Reference ranges (mg/dL, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for Fibrinogen Activity's individual levels. This is a common functional medicine marker but may be reported with general reference ranges by individual labs. In general: Lab range 200–400 mg/dL, Functional range 200–300 mg/dL. For this v3 guide, please refer to the standard ranges below:

- Clinical Low: below 150 mg/dL  
- Functional Low: 150–199 mg/dL  
- **Optimal: 200–300 mg/dL**  
- Functional High: 301–400 mg/dL  
- Clinical High: above 400 mg/dL

**Clinical Low (below 150 mg/dL):** This is called hypofibrinogenemia. It raises your bleeding risk. Causes: advanced liver disease (low production), DIC (disseminated intravascular coagulation — a serious condition where small blood clots form throughout the body, using up clotting factors), inherited fibrinogen disorders, and certain medicines (clot-busting drugs, snake venom). To address: work with a provider urgently. Treatment depends on the cause — fibrinogen concentrate, fresh frozen plasma, cryoprecipitate (a blood product rich in clotting factors), or treatment of DIC. Treat liver disease.

**Functional Low (150–199 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can mean mild liver dysfunction, mild consumption (DIC), or inherited hypofibrinogenemia. You may have a slight bleeding tendency and reduced acute phase response. To optimize: check liver function. Treat any underlying condition. Periodic monitoring is enough.

**Optimal (200–300 mg/dL):** This is the goal. It means balanced clotting, healthy liver production, and low chronic inflammation. To stay here: keep up a colorful plant-based diet rich in antioxidants, fiber, and omega-3 fats. Exercise most days (both cardio and strength training). Avoid smoking, limit alcohol, and manage stress. Periodic re-checking is enough.

**Functional High (301–400 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It is an independent risk factor for heart disease, stroke, and blood clots. Common causes: chronic inflammation, smoking, obesity, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, sedentary lifestyle, estrogen therapy, and acute infection. You have a higher risk of clots and heart problems. To optimize: address inflammation and heart risk factors. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet (vegetables, berries, omega-3 fats, fiber). Exercise regularly. Lose extra weight. Quit smoking. Manage stress. A provider can check hs-CRP, fasting insulin, lipids, and heart risk.

**Clinical High (above 400 mg/dL):** A high fibrinogen is linked to much higher risk of heart disease, stroke, and blood clots. Causes: chronic inflammation, smoking, obesity, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, sedentary lifestyle, estrogen therapy, acute infection, cancer, and certain genetic disorders. To address: work with a provider. Address underlying inflammation, heart risk factors, and modifiable risks. Lifestyle changes — anti-inflammatory diet, regular exercise, weight loss, smoking cessation, alcohol moderation — are foundational. Natural anti-inflammatory agents (omega-3s, curcumin, ginger) and possibly medicines (aspirin, statins) may be considered.

## Zinc

**What is this test?** Zinc is a trace mineral (a mineral your body needs in small amounts) that is involved in over 300 chemical reactions in your body. It is critical for immune function, wound healing, taste and smell, hormone production, DNA synthesis, protein synthesis, and antioxidant defenses. It is also important for testosterone production, thyroid function, and skin health.

**Why does it matter?** Zinc deficiency is very common, especially in older adults, vegetarians/vegans, and those with gut malabsorption. Low zinc weakens your immune system, slows wound healing, lowers testosterone, and worsens thyroid function.

**Reference ranges (μg/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–59  
- Functional Low: 60–79  
- **Optimal: 80–100**  
- Functional High: 101–130  
- Clinical High: above 130

**Clinical Low (below 60 μg/dL):** This is significant zinc deficiency. It can cause frequent infections, poor wound healing, hair loss, diarrhea, skin rashes (especially around the mouth, eyes, and genitals), taste and smell problems, white spots on nails, infertility, and slow growth in children. Causes: not enough zinc in your diet, malabsorption (celiac, IBD, SIBO, gastric bypass), alcoholism, chronic diarrhea, certain medicines (diuretics, ACE inhibitors, PPIs), pregnancy, lactation, and chronic illness. To address: work with a provider. Take zinc supplements (zinc picolinate, citrate, or glycinate, 15–50 mg a day). Eat zinc-rich foods (oysters, grass-fed red meat, pumpkin seeds, chickpeas, lentils, nuts, dairy). Treat malabsorption.

**Functional Low (60–79 μg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It is linked to subtle immune suppression, slow wound healing, mild taste/smell changes, low libido, mild skin issues, and reduced thyroid function. Subclinical zinc deficiency is very common, especially in older adults, vegetarians, and those with chronic stress. To raise it: eat zinc-rich foods (oysters, grass-fed red meat, pumpkin seeds, chickpeas, lentils, nuts, dairy). Consider zinc supplements (zinc picolinate, 15–30 mg a day). Take with food to avoid nausea. Balance with copper (1–2 mg) if you supplement long-term. A provider can check zinc, gut, and diet.

**Optimal (80–100 μg/dL):** This is the goal. It means you have enough zinc for strong immune function, healthy wound healing, balanced hormone production, and good antioxidant defenses. To stay here: keep up a diet rich in zinc (oysters, grass-fed red meat, pumpkin seeds, chickpeas, lentils, nuts, dairy). For vegetarians and vegans, supplementation is often needed (15–30 mg a day) because plant zinc is not absorbed as well. Periodic re-checking is enough.

**Functional High (101–130 μg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It usually means zinc supplements or a lot of dietary zinc. Too much zinc can cause copper deficiency (zinc and copper compete for absorption), nausea, vomiting, and reduced immune function. To optimize: if supplementing, make sure your dose is not too high. Balance with copper (1–2 mg) if you supplement long-term. Take with food.

**Clinical High (above 130 μg/dL):** Very high zinc is rare and usually from too much supplementation. It can cause copper deficiency (with anemia, low neutrophil count, and nerve symptoms), nausea, vomiting, and reduced HDL cholesterol (the "good" cholesterol). To address: work with a provider. Cut back or stop zinc supplements. Take copper (1–2 mg) to restore balance. Re-check zinc and copper.

## Gamma-Glutamyl Transerase (GGT)

**What is this test?** GGT is an enzyme found mostly in your liver, bile ducts, kidneys, pancreas, and spleen. It is the most sensitive marker for bile duct problems (cholestasis, blockage). It also goes up with alcohol use, fatty liver, and certain medicines.

**Why does it matter?** It is a key marker in functional medicine because it is sensitive to oxidative stress, environmental toxins, and metabolic problems. It is also a strong predictor of heart disease and death.

**Reference ranges (U/L, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–8  
- Functional Low: 9–14  
- **Optimal: 15–30**  
- Functional High: 31–48  
- Clinical High: above 48

**Clinical Low (below 9 U/L):** Low GGT is rare. It can mean underactive thyroid, low magnesium, or low zinc. It can also happen in advanced liver disease with reduced liver function. You may feel tired and have other deficiency symptoms. To optimize: make sure you get enough magnesium, zinc, and thyroid support. A provider can check thyroid, magnesium, and zinc.

**Functional Low (9–14 U/L):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can mean slightly underactive thyroid, mild low magnesium, or mild low zinc. You may have subtle fatigue and reduced metabolic capacity. To optimize: make sure you get enough magnesium, zinc, and thyroid support. Periodic monitoring is enough.

**Optimal (15–30 U/L):** This is the goal. It means your liver, bile ducts, and pancreas are working well, with low oxidative stress and no significant toxic burden. To stay here: keep up a liver-friendly, plant-centered diet (vegetables, fruits, leafy greens, beets, cruciferous vegetables, coffee). Limit alcohol. Avoid liver-toxic substances. Exercise most days. Maintain a healthy weight. Periodic re-checking is enough.

**Functional High (31–48 U/L):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It is the most sensitive early marker for fatty liver disease, oxidative stress, and toxic burden. Common causes: non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), alcohol use, smoking, obesity, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, certain medicines (anticonvulsants, statins, certain antibiotics), and environmental toxin exposure. You may feel tired, foggy, or have right upper belly discomfort. To optimize: find and address the cause. Cut back on alcohol, processed foods, and refined sugar. Lose extra body fat. Eat a liver-friendly, plant-centered diet. Exercise regularly. A provider can check liver, fasting insulin, and toxic burden.

**Clinical High (above 48 U/L):** A high GGT means significant liver or bile duct stress. Causes: non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (most common), alcoholic liver disease, cholestasis (bile duct blockage, primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis), hepatitis (viral, drug-induced, autoimmune), cirrhosis, liver cancer, pancreatitis, and certain medicines. To address: work with a provider. They will order a full liver workup (liver enzymes, viral hepatitis panel, autoimmune markers, iron studies, liver imaging, and possibly biopsy). Treatment depends on the cause. Lifestyle changes — alcohol cessation, weight loss, anti-inflammatory diet, exercise — are foundational for fatty liver disease.

## Insulin (Fasting)

**What is this test?** Fasting insulin measures the amount of insulin in your blood after not eating for at least 8 hours. Insulin is a hormone made by your pancreas that helps sugar get from your blood into your cells.

**Why does it matter?** It is the earliest and most sensitive marker of insulin resistance (when your cells stop responding well to insulin). Fasting insulin often rises 5–10 years before fasting glucose or HbA1c become abnormal. It is best looked at with fasting glucose (for HOMA-IR — a calculation that estimates insulin resistance).

**Reference ranges (μIU/mL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–2  
- Functional Low: 2.0–2.9  
- **Optimal: 3.0–7.0**  
- Functional High: 8.0–12.0  
- Clinical High: above 12.0

**Clinical Low (below 2 μIU/mL):** Low fasting insulin is rare. It can mean weak pancreatic beta cells (the cells in your pancreas that make insulin), advanced type 1 diabetes (the immune system attacks beta cells), advanced type 2 diabetes with beta cell exhaustion, or rarely an insulinoma (a rare tumor that makes insulin) that has been removed. You may have high blood sugar and metabolic problems. To address: work with a provider. They will check beta cell function, glucose, and insulin antibodies. If you have type 1 diabetes, insulin therapy is needed. A provider can check pancreatic function and autoimmunity.

**Functional Low (2.0–2.9 μIU/mL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It is usually not a problem and may mean excellent insulin sensitivity, a low-carb or ketogenic diet, prolonged fasting, or rarely beta cell problems. People in this range usually have excellent metabolic health. To maintain: keep up a whole-food, low-glycemic diet, exercise, healthy body composition, and stress control.

**Optimal (3.0–7.0 μIU/mL):** This is the goal. It is the most important marker of metabolic health. It means you have excellent insulin sensitivity. To stay here: keep up a high-fiber, plant-centered diet with enough protein. Exercise most days (especially strength training and walking). Manage stress. Sleep 7–9 hours. Avoid refined sugar and processed foods. Limit alcohol. Periodically check fasting glucose, HbA1c, and HOMA-IR.

**Functional High (8.0–12.0 μIU/mL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It is the most sensitive early marker of insulin resistance — often present 5–10 years before diabetes develops. Common causes: too much sugar and refined carbs, sedentary lifestyle, belly fat, chronic stress, poor sleep, and your genes. You are on the path to type 2 diabetes. To lower it: cut out refined sugar and processed carbs. Eat 25–40 grams of fiber a day. Exercise daily. Lose extra belly fat. A provider can check HOMA-IR, fasting glucose, HbA1c, and triglycerides.

**Clinical High (above 12.0 μIU/mL):** A high fasting insulin means significant insulin resistance and a higher risk of type 2 diabetes, heart disease, fatty liver, PCOS, and many chronic diseases. Causes: long-term insulin resistance, obesity, metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, chronic stress, certain medicines (steroids), and rare insulinoma (with low blood sugar). To address: work with a provider. Aggressive lifestyle changes — a whole-food, low-glycemic, plant-centered diet, daily exercise, weight loss, stress reduction, and better sleep — are foundational and can often reverse insulin resistance. You may also benefit from natural insulin sensitizers (berberine, chromium, cinnamon, inositol) or metformin.

## Apolipoprotein B (ApoB)

**What is this test?** ApoB is the main protein in all the "bad" cholesterol particles — LDL, VLDL, IDL, Lp(a), and chylomicron remnants. Each atherogenic particle (a particle that can form plaque in your arteries) has exactly one ApoB molecule, so ApoB is the most accurate count of these particles. ApoB is a stronger predictor of heart risk than LDL or non-HDL cholesterol.

**Why does it matter?** It is the best single number for the number of atherogenic particles in your blood. Many people with normal LDL still have high ApoB (more small, dense particles). A standard lipid panel misses many people with high ApoB.

**Reference ranges (mg/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–40  
- Functional Low: 40–59  
- **Optimal: 60–80**  
- Functional High: 81–100  
- Clinical High: above 100

**Clinical Low (below 40 mg/dL):** A very low ApoB is linked to very low heart risk but may mean very low LDL (from a low-fat diet, malabsorption, liver disease, or genetic factors). It is usually not a problem. People in this range usually have excellent heart health. To maintain: keep up a whole-food, plant-centered diet, exercise, healthy body composition, and stress control. If LDL is also very low, make sure you get enough healthy fats. Periodic monitoring is enough.

**Functional Low (40–59 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It is generally excellent and is linked to very low heart risk. It is common in people on whole-food, plant-based diets and regular exercisers. To maintain: keep up a high-fiber, plant-centered diet with enough omega-3 fats, regular exercise, and healthy body composition.

**Optimal (60–80 mg/dL):** This is the goal. It means low atherogenic particle number and very low heart risk. People in this range usually have a whole-food, plant-centered diet, regular exercise, healthy body composition, and well-regulated blood sugar. To stay here: keep up fiber (25–40 grams a day), plant sterols, omega-3 fats, and a colorful plant-based diet. Exercise daily. Manage stress. Sleep 7–9 hours. Check with other lipid markers (LDL, HDL, triglycerides, hs-CRP, Lp(a)).

**Functional High (81–100 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It means more atherogenic particles than optimal and is linked to higher heart risk, especially with other risk factors. Common causes: insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, obesity, a diet high in saturated fat and refined carbs, sedentary lifestyle, underactive thyroid, and your genes. To lower it: find and address the cause. Cut back on saturated fat and refined carbs. Eat more fiber and plant sterols. Exercise daily. Lose extra body fat. A provider can check fasting insulin, thyroid, and inflammation.

**Clinical High (above 100 mg/dL):** A high ApoB is a strong independent predictor of heart disease. It means a high atherogenic particle burden and a much higher risk of atherosclerosis (plaque buildup in arteries), heart attack, and stroke, especially with other risk factors (high Lp(a) — a genetic variant that raises heart risk, high hs-CRP, family history, diabetes, high blood pressure). Causes: genetic factors (familial hypercholesterolemia, ApoB mutations), long-term insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, poor diet, sedentary lifestyle, underactive thyroid, and chronic inflammation. To address: work with a provider. Aggressive lifestyle changes — a whole-food, plant-centered diet rich in fiber, beans, vegetables, and nuts — combined with daily exercise, weight loss, and stress reduction are foundational. Natural lipid-lowering agents (bergamot, red yeast rice, plant sterols, psyllium, berberine) and omega-3s can help. Statins and other medicines are very effective at lowering ApoB. Target ApoB below 80 mg/dL (or below 60 with heart disease risk).

# Summary

This guide has covered 82+ common blood and urine tests. Each test is explained through both lab and functional lenses, with reference ranges from the OHS test ranges file. The lab range is the average for the general population (which includes many people with chronic disease). The functional range is a tighter range from research. Being in the functional range means your body is working at its best and your future risk of health problems is lower.

**Tests with separate Male and Female versions in this guide:**

- Testosterone, Total, Male / Testosterone, Total, Female  
- Testosterone, Free, Male / Testosterone, Free, Female  
- Testosterone, Bioavailable, Male / Testosterone, Bioavailable, Female  
- Ferritin, Male / Ferritin, Female

**The basics that help most tests:**

- A whole-food, plant-centered diet rich in fiber, antioxidants, omega-3 fats, and key micronutrients  
- Regular exercise (both strength training and cardio)  
- Sleep 7–9 hours a night  
- Stress management (meditation, deep breathing, time in nature, social connection)  
- Avoid toxins (alcohol, tobacco, environmental chemicals)  
- Stay well hydrated (drink to thirst, usually 2–3 liters a day)  
- Maintain a healthy body composition  
- Support your gut health (fiber, fermented foods, probiotics)  
- Support your liver health (leafy greens, beets, cruciferous vegetables, coffee)  
- Get regular safe sun exposure for vitamin D

**When lifestyle is not enough:**

- Targeted supplements (guided by testing)  
- Specific dietary changes (guided by your results)  
- Clinical treatments from your provider (medicines when needed)  
- Work with a functional medicine provider for a personalized plan

**Important reminder:** Lab values are not isolated numbers. They are connected pieces of a larger health picture. Always look at your results in the context of how you feel, your history, your physical exam, and other markers. The goal is not just to "normalize" lab values but to help your body work at its best, prevent disease, and live a long, healthy life.
