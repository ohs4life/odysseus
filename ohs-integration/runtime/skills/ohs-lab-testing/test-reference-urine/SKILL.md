# Urinalysis — Complete — Test Reference

## When to Use
When a customer, employee, or practitioner asks for the meaning, optimal range, or interpretation of any lab test in this group. **Reference only — never substitute for a provider's interpretation.** See `ohs-compliance/disclaimers` (the "share with your provider" framing) for the required patient-facing language.

## Procedure
1. Identify the test the user is asking about.
2. Quote the test name exactly as it appears on the lab report (with the OHS reference range applied).
3. Read out the five-level range (Clinical Low / Functional Low / Optimal / Functional High / Clinical High) and the units.
4. Summarize the "What is this test?" and "Why does it matter?" sections in plain language.
5. For any out-of-range result, recommend the customer share the result with their healthcare provider.

## Pitfalls
- **Do not interpret any test result for a customer.** The lab description here is **reference** content. The customer's provider is the one who interprets.
- **Do not recommend a specific dose of a supplement** based on a test result. The right phrasing is "this marker is associated with [nutrient / lifestyle factors] — your provider can advise on whether to add a supplement, and at what dose."
- **Do not rephrase the FDA structure-function disclaimer away.** If the answer includes any "supports / is associated with" language about a product, also include the disclaimer (see `ohs-compliance/disclaimers`).
- **Do not imply a single test is sufficient.** Most health questions need a pattern of markers, not a single value, to be meaningful.
- **Do not state ranges with more precision than the source.** The five levels in this skill come from `knowledgebase/labs/ohs_lab_test_descriptions_v3.md` (OHS-authored). Do not invent a tighter or different range.
- **Range sex specificity (M / F / B):** respect the M / F / B annotation in each test.

## Anything else

### The five-level range system (every test uses this)
- **Clinical Low** — below the lab range. Often means a real health problem.
- **Functional Low** — in the lab range but on the low end. Not sick, but not at your best.
- **Optimal** — the level where your body works its best. The goal.
- **Functional High** — in the lab range but on the high end. Body is under stress.
- **Clinical High** — above the lab range. Often means a real health problem.

### Sex-specific ranges
**B** = both sexes · **M** = males only · **F** = females only.

## Test Descriptions

### Color (Urine)

**What is this test?** Urine color shows how concentrated your urine is and can give you clues about your hydration, diet, and health. Normal urine is yellow. The yellow color comes from urochromes, which are made when your body breaks down hemoglobin.

**Why does it matter?** It is a quick, free way to check your hydration. Unusual colors (red, brown, black, green, milky) can be a sign of specific conditions, foods, or medicines.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test. This test is reported qualitatively. A normal yellow color is the goal. A "Functional High" or "Clinical High" usually refers to unusual or abnormal colors that need follow-up.

**Optimal (Yellow):** An optimal urine color is pale to straw yellow, which means you are well hydrated and your kidneys are working well. To stay here: drink to thirst (usually 2–3 liters a day, more with exercise and heat). Make sure you have enough electrolytes. Eat a colorful plant-based diet. Check your urine color periodically as a quick hydration check.

**Clinical High (Unusual Colors):** Unusual urine colors need evaluation. Red/brown urine can mean blood (hematuria), myoglobin (from rhabdomyolysis), hemoglobin (from hemolysis), porphyria (a group of rare genetic disorders), beets, or rifampin (an antibiotic). Dark brown/black can mean liver disease, melanoma, or certain medicines. Green/blue can mean a Pseudomonas infection (a type of bacteria), certain medicines, or food dyes. Milky/white can mean chyluria (lymph fluid in urine, from lymphatic blockage) or pyuria (white blood cells in urine, from UTI). To address: work with a provider. Find and treat the cause. Drink more water.

### Appearance (Urine)

**What is this test?** Urine appearance describes how clear your urine is — clear, slightly cloudy, cloudy, or turbid.

**Why does it matter?** It can be a quick sign of infection, crystals, mucus, or other things in the urine.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test. This test is reported qualitatively. A clear appearance is the goal. A "Functional High" or "Clinical High" usually refers to cloudy or turbid urine that may need follow-up.

**Optimal (Clear):** Optimal urine is clear with no visible cloudiness, sediment, or particles. It means your kidneys are working well, there is no infection, and you are well hydrated. To stay here: keep drinking to thirst. Eat a whole-food diet. Support your kidney and bladder health. Periodic checks are enough.

**Clinical High (Cloudy/Turbid):** Cloudy or turbid urine can mean a urinary tract infection (most common), pyuria (white blood cells), hematuria (red blood cells), crystals (uric acid, phosphate, oxalate), chyluria (lymph fluid), mucus, or heavy sediment. To address: work with a provider. They will order a urinalysis and urine culture. Treat the underlying infection. Drink more water. If recurrent, check for chronic UTI, interstitial cystitis (a chronic bladder pain condition), or other conditions.

### Specific Gravity (Urine)

**What is this test?** Urine specific gravity measures how concentrated your urine is compared to water. It shows how well your kidneys can concentrate and dilute urine.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you about your hydration and kidney function. High specific gravity means concentrated urine (often from dehydration). Low specific gravity means dilute urine (often from over-hydration or kidney problems).

**Reference ranges (ratio, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–0.93  
- Functional Low: 0.93–1.0  
- **Optimal: 1.0–1.03**  
- Functional High: 1.03–1.06  
- Clinical High: above 1.06

**Clinical Low (below 0.93):** Very dilute urine can mean over-hydration, diabetes insipidus (kidneys cannot concentrate urine), kidney disease (impaired concentrating ability), or diuretics. Persistent very dilute urine can cause electrolyte imbalances and raise UTI risk. To address: work with a provider. Find and treat the cause. Moderate your fluid intake.

**Functional Low (0.93–1.0):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means over-hydration or mild concentrating problems. You may be drinking too much water and diluting important electrolytes. To optimize: moderate your fluid intake (drink to thirst, usually 2–3 liters a day) and make sure you have enough electrolytes. A provider can check kidney concentrating ability if it stays low.

**Optimal (1.0–1.03):** This is the goal. It means balanced hydration, normal kidney concentrating ability, and well-regulated fluid balance. To stay here: drink to thirst (usually 2–3 liters a day, more with exercise and heat). Make sure you have enough electrolytes (sea salt, electrolytes in water if you drink a lot). Eat a whole-food diet. Periodic checks are a useful hydration marker.

**Functional High (1.03–1.06):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It often means mild dehydration, high protein intake, or concentrated morning urine. You may be slightly dehydrated. To optimize: drink more water and make sure you have enough electrolytes. If it stays high with normal fluid intake, check kidney concentrating ability.

**Clinical High (above 1.06):** Very concentrated urine usually means dehydration. Other causes: high protein intake, certain medicines, heart failure, liver disease, SIADH, and adrenal insufficiency. Very concentrated urine can raise the risk of kidney stones and UTIs. To address: work with a provider. Drink more water. Find and treat the cause. For kidney stone prevention, aim for specific gravity below 1.020 and urine output above 2 liters a day.

### pH (Urine)

**What is this test?** Urine pH measures how acidic or alkaline your urine is.

**Why does it matter?** It reflects your diet (acidic urine with animal protein, alkaline urine with plant foods), acid-base balance, kidney function, and certain infections or medicines. Urine pH affects kidney stone risk — uric acid stones form in acidic urine, and struvite stones form in alkaline urine.

**Reference ranges (pH, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–2.99  
- Functional Low: 3.0–4.99  
- **Optimal: 5.0–8.0**  
- Functional High: 8.01–8.99  
- Clinical High: 9.0 and above

**Clinical Low (below 3.0):** Very acidic urine is unusual. It can mean severe metabolic acidosis, uncontrolled diabetes with ketoacidosis, certain poisonings, prolonged fasting, and certain medicines. Persistent very acidic urine raises the risk of uric acid kidney stones. To optimize: work with a provider to find and treat the cause. Increase plant foods (vegetables, fruits, beans). Cut back on animal protein and processed foods. Consider alkaline water or potassium citrate.

**Functional Low (3.0–4.99):** This is in the lab range but on the acidic side. It often means a high-protein, low-vegetable diet or mild metabolic acidosis. You may have slightly higher risk of uric acid stones and bone loss over time. To optimize: eat more plant foods (vegetables, fruits, beans). Cut back on animal protein. Eat an alkaline-promoting diet.

**Optimal (5.0–8.0):** This is the goal. It means balanced acid-base status, healthy kidney function, and a plant-prominent diet. To stay here: keep up a plant-centered diet rich in vegetables, fruits, leafy greens, and beans. Limit processed foods and excess animal protein. Drink plenty of water. Periodically re-check, especially with diet changes.

**Functional High (8.01–8.99):** This is in the lab range but on the alkaline side. It often means a plant-heavy diet (usually healthy), a recent meal, or certain medicines (acetazolamide, potassium citrate). It can also mean a UTI with urea-splitting bacteria. To optimize: keep a balanced diet. If it stays very alkaline and you have symptoms, check for UTI.

**Clinical High (9.0 and above):** Very alkaline urine can mean a UTI with urea-splitting bacteria (Proteus, Klebsiella), renal tubular acidosis type 1, milk-alkali syndrome, certain medicines, or long-term vomiting. Alkaline urine raises the risk of struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate) kidney stones. To address: work with a provider. Treat any UTI. Acidify urine with vitamin C, cranberry, or betaine HCl in some cases. Cut back on excess plant intake if it is very alkaline.

### Reducing Substances (Urine)

**What is this test?** This test looks for reducing agents in your urine. The most common is glucose (sugar), which suggests diabetes. Other reducing agents include fructose, galactose, lactose, certain medicines, and ascorbic acid (vitamin C).

**Why does it matter?** Positive reducing substances in urine can mean diabetes, pregnancy-related sugar in urine, or rare metabolic disorders.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative result is the goal. A positive result needs evaluation.

**Optimal (Negative):** A negative test is the goal. It means normal sugar metabolism and no abnormal sugars in urine. To maintain: keep up a high-fiber, plant-centered diet, exercise, and healthy body composition. Periodic blood sugar testing is a better check.

**Clinical High (Positive):** A positive test needs evaluation. Causes: diabetes (glucose — most common), pregnancy (lactose), galactosemia, fructose intolerance, and certain medicines. Glucose in urine is a late sign of high blood sugar. To address: work with a provider. Find and treat the cause. For diabetes, optimize blood sugar management.

### Occult Blood (Urine)

**What is this test?** This test looks for blood in your urine that you cannot see (hidden blood). It can be intact red blood cells (hematuria), free hemoglobin, or myoglobin (from muscle breakdown).

**Why does it matter?** Any hidden blood in urine is a sign of bleeding, infection, stones, or — rarely — cancer. Painless hidden blood, especially in older adults, can be the first sign of bladder cancer.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative result is the goal. A positive result needs evaluation.

**Optimal (Negative):** A negative test is the goal. It means no blood in your urine. To maintain: keep up a whole-food diet, stay well hydrated, support your kidney and urinary tract health, and avoid kidney-toxic substances. Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High (Positive):** A positive test needs evaluation. Causes: UTI (most common), kidney stones, bladder cancer (especially in smokers and older adults), kidney disease (glomerulonephritis), prostate enlargement or cancer, recent hard exercise, trauma, and menstrual contamination. Hematuria with protein and dysmorphic red blood cells (red blood cells that look misshapen under a microscope) suggests glomerular disease. Painless hematuria in older adults needs a cystoscopy (a test where a small camera is used to look inside the bladder) to rule out bladder cancer. To address: work with a provider. They will order urine culture, imaging (ultrasound, CT), cystoscopy, and possibly kidney biopsy. Treat the cause. Drink more water.

### Protein (Urine)

**What is this test?** This test measures the amount of protein in your urine. Normally, your kidneys filter very little protein, so urine protein should be negative or trace. Protein in urine (proteinuria) can mean kidney disease, but it can also be brief from exercise, fever, dehydration, or stress. Microalbumin (small amounts of albumin) is an early sign of kidney damage in diabetes and high blood pressure.

**Why does it matter?** Persistent proteinuria is a strong warning sign for kidney disease progression and heart disease. The earlier you catch it, the more you can do.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):**

- **Optimal: 0 (Negative)**  
- Clinical High: 0.01 and above (Positive)

Note: This test does not have a Clinical Low, Functional Low, or Functional High category in the OHS ranges. Anything above 0 is a Clinical High.

**Optimal (0, Negative):** Optimal urine protein is negative. It means your kidney filtration is healthy. To stay here: keep up a whole-food, plant-centered diet. Stay well hydrated. Keep blood pressure below 120/80 and HbA1c below 5.4%. Avoid kidney-toxic substances (NSAIDs, contrast dye). Exercise most days. Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High (0.01 and above, Positive):** A positive test needs evaluation. Causes: diabetic nephropathy, hypertensive nephropathy, glomerulonephritis, IgA nephropathy, lupus nephritis, polycystic kidney disease, multiple myeloma, pre-eclampsia, UTI, and certain medicines. Persistent proteinuria is a strong risk factor for progressive kidney disease and heart disease. To address: work with a provider. They will order 24-hour urine protein, microalbumin/creatinine ratio, blood tests, and possibly kidney biopsy. Treat the cause. Strict blood pressure and blood sugar control. ACE inhibitors or ARBs (common blood pressure medicines) are often used for proteinuria. Limit sodium.

### Nitrite (Urine)

**What is this test?** Nitrite in urine is made when bacteria (usually gram-negative bacteria like E. coli, Klebsiella, Proteus) change nitrates into nitrites in the bladder. A positive test strongly suggests a bacterial urinary tract infection.

**Why does it matter?** It is a quick sign of a UTI. Combined with leukocyte esterase, it gives a strong case for treating a UTI.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative result is the goal. A positive result needs evaluation.

**Optimal (Negative):** Optimal urine is nitrite-negative. It means no bacterial UTI. To stay here: drink enough water (2–3 liters a day), practice good hygiene, urinate after sex (for women), and avoid holding urine. Cranberry, D-mannose (a natural sugar that may help prevent UTIs), and probiotics can support urinary tract health. Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High (Positive):** A positive test means a bacterial UTI. Common bacteria: E. coli (most common), Klebsiella, Proteus, and Pseudomonas. UTIs cause painful urination, urgency, frequency, lower belly pain, and sometimes blood in urine and fever (if upper tract). To address: work with a provider. Urine culture and sensitivity testing guide antibiotics. Drink more water, take D-mannose, cranberry, and probiotics. For recurrent UTIs, look for underlying causes (anatomy, hormones, microbiome).

### Leukocyte Esterase (Urine)

**What is this test?** Leukocyte esterase is an enzyme made by white blood cells (neutrophils). A positive test means pyuria (white blood cells in the urine), which usually means inflammation or infection — most often a UTI.

**Why does it matter?** It is a quick sign of inflammation or infection in the urinary tract. Combined with nitrite, it gives a strong case for treating a UTI.

**Reference ranges (cells/μL, both sexes):**

- **Optimal: 0–2**  
- Functional High: 2.01–3  
- Clinical High: 3.01 and above

Note: This test does not have a Clinical Low or Functional Low category in the OHS ranges.

**Optimal (0–2):** This is the goal. It means no pyuria or infection. To stay here: drink enough water, practice good hygiene, and support your immune and urinary tract health. Cranberry, D-mannose, and probiotics can help. Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Functional High (2.01–3):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean early or mild pyuria. Common causes: early UTI, mild inflammation, or contamination. To optimize: drink more water, support urinary tract health with D-mannose, cranberry, and probiotics, and check with a provider if you have symptoms. A provider can check urinary tract, gut, and immune health.

**Clinical High (3.01 and above):** A positive test means pyuria, most often from a bacterial UTI. Symptoms: painful urination, urgency, frequency, lower belly pain, and sometimes fever and flank pain (if upper tract). To address: work with a provider. Urine culture and sensitivity testing guide antibiotics. Drink more water. Take D-mannose, cranberry, and probiotics. For recurrent UTIs, look for underlying causes (anatomy, hormones, microbiome, interstitial cystitis). A provider can check immune function, gut health, and urinary microbiome.

### Squamous Epithelial Cells (Urine)

**What is this test?** Squamous epithelial cells are large, flat cells that line the lower urinary tract, vagina, and skin. They are commonly seen in urine and usually mean contamination from skin or vaginal cells during collection, not disease.

**Why does it matter?** It mostly tells you about how well the urine sample was collected. High numbers usually mean poor clean-catch technique, not a disease.

**Reference ranges (cells/hpf, both sexes):**

- **Optimal: 0–5**  
- Functional High: 5.01–10  
- Clinical High: 10.01 and above

Note: This test does not have a Clinical Low or Functional Low category in the OHS ranges.

**Optimal (0–5):** This is the goal. It means a clean-catch collection. To stay here: keep up proper clean-catch technique (clean the genital area, collect midstream). Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Functional High (5.01–10):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It usually means mild contamination from skin or vaginal cells. It is not a sign of disease. To optimize: make sure you use proper clean-catch technique. Re-test with careful collection.

**Clinical High (10.01 and above):** This usually means poor collection technique (contaminated sample) rather than disease. Rarely, very high counts can mean squamous cell carcinoma of the bladder or urethra, but this is uncommon. To address: re-test with careful clean-catch technique. If it stays high and you have symptoms (bleeding, pain), work with a provider for further evaluation.

### Bacteria (Urine)

**What is this test?** This test looks for bacteria in your urine. It can mean a urinary tract infection (significant bacteriuria) or contamination from poor collection technique.

**Why does it matter?** Significant bacteria with pyuria and symptoms means a UTI. Bacteria without pyuria or symptoms often just means contamination.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative result is the goal. A positive result needs evaluation.

**Optimal (Negative):** This is the goal. It means no significant bacteriuria. To stay here: drink enough water, practice good hygiene, urinate after sex (for women), and avoid holding urine. Cranberry, D-mannose, and probiotics can help. Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High (Positive with pyuria and symptoms):** A significant bacterial count (usually above 10⁵ CFU/mL — colony-forming units per milliliter) with pyuria and symptoms means a UTI. Common bacteria: E. coli (most common), Klebsiella, Proteus, Enterococcus, and Staphylococcus saprophyticus. To address: work with a provider. Urine culture and sensitivity testing guide antibiotics. Drink more water, take D-mannose, cranberry, and probiotics. For recurrent UTIs, look for underlying causes (anatomy, hormones, microbiome).

### Calcium Oxalate Crystals (Urine)

**What is this test?** This test looks for calcium oxalate crystals in your urine. They are the most common type of crystal found in urine. They can look like envelopes (dihydrate form) or dumbbells (monohydrate form). Small amounts are normal, especially in concentrated urine. Larger amounts can mean a risk of calcium oxalate kidney stones.

**Why does it matter?** Calcium oxalate is the most common type of kidney stone. Knowing if you have these crystals helps you understand your stone risk and what to do about it.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative result is the goal. A positive result (especially with symptoms or history of stones) needs follow-up.

**Optimal (Negative):** A negative test is the goal. It means balanced oxalate and calcium metabolism with enough hydration. To maintain: keep up enough water (2–3 liters a day), a balanced diet with moderate oxalate, and enough calcium with meals (calcium binds oxalate in the gut and reduces absorption).

**Clinical High (Positive, especially with symptoms):** A high crystal count is a big risk factor for calcium oxalate kidney stones, the most common type. Causes: hyperoxaluria (from diet, malabsorption, or bariatric surgery), hypercalciuria (idiopathic — no known cause, hyperparathyroidism, sarcoidosis), low urine volume, hypocitraturia (low citrate, a natural stone inhibitor), and rare genetic disorders (primary hyperoxaluria). Symptoms of kidney stones: severe flank pain, blood in urine, nausea, and vomiting. To address: work with a provider. A 24-hour urine collection for stone risk is often done. Drink more water (urine output above 2.5 liters a day). Cut back on oxalate. Make sure you get enough calcium. Increase citrate (lemons, limes). Consider thiazide diuretics (medicines that lower urine calcium) or potassium citrate in some cases.

### Crystals (Urine, General)

**What is this test?** This test looks for any type of crystal in your urine. Common types include calcium oxalate, calcium phosphate, uric acid, struvite (triple phosphate), cystine, and ammonium biurate. The type and amount help identify metabolic problems and kidney stone risk.

**Why does it matter?** Different crystals point to different conditions. Uric acid crystals suggest acidic urine, gout, or high purine intake. Calcium oxalate is the most common. Struvite suggests a UTI with urea-splitting bacteria. Cystine suggests a genetic disorder (cystinuria).

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test. This test is reported qualitatively. The test section appears without specific level ranges in the OHS file, indicating it is reported as present/absent without graded categories.

**Optimal (None to rare):** This is the goal. It means healthy urine chemistry with low kidney stone risk. To stay here: keep up enough water (2+ liters a day). Eat a balanced diet with moderate amounts of stone-forming foods. Make sure you get enough citrate (lemons, limes, citrus fruits). Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High (Many, with symptoms or risk factors):** A high crystal count is linked to higher risk of kidney stones. Different crystals mean different conditions: uric acid (acidic urine, gout, high purine diet), calcium oxalate (most common, hyperoxaluria, hypercalciuria), calcium phosphate (alkaline urine, hyperparathyroidism), struvite/triple phosphate (UTI with urea-splitting bacteria), cystine (cystinuria, genetic). To address: work with a provider. A 24-hour urine collection guides treatment. Drink more water. Change your diet based on crystal type. Consider medicines (thiazides, potassium citrate, allopurinol).

### Hyaline Cast (Urine)

**What is this test?** Hyaline casts are tube-shaped structures made of Tamm-Horsfall protein (a protein made in your kidney tubules). Small numbers are normal, especially with exercise, dehydration, or concentrated urine. Larger numbers can mean reduced blood flow to the kidneys, dehydration, exercise, or certain kidney conditions.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you about kidney health. Hyaline casts alone are usually benign (often from dehydration or exercise), but they can be a sign of reduced blood flow to the kidneys.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative or rare result is the goal. A high count needs evaluation.

**Optimal (None to rare):** This is the goal. It means healthy kidney tubules, enough hydration, and well-regulated kidney health. To stay here: keep up enough water, balanced exercise (avoid overtraining), and supportive kidney health.

**Clinical High (Many, with other abnormal findings):** A high hyaline cast count with proteinuria, hematuria, or elevated creatinine suggests kidney disease. Causes include dehydration (most common), hard exercise, reduced blood flow to kidneys (heart failure, shock), diuretics, and chronic kidney disease. To address: work with a provider. Drink more water. Moderate exercise. Check kidney function. Treat underlying heart failure or other conditions.

### Casts (Urine, General)

**What is this test?** This test looks for tube-shaped structures (casts) in your urine. Casts form in the kidney tubules from Tamm-Horsfall protein and trapped cells or substances. The type of cast gives important information — hyaline (dehydration, exercise), granular (kidney injury), red blood cell (glomerulonephritis), white blood cell (kidney infection, interstitial nephritis), epithelial (acute tubular necrosis — sudden damage to the kidney tubules), fatty (nephrotic syndrome — a group of symptoms including very high protein in urine), waxy (chronic kidney disease), and broad (advanced kidney disease).

**Why does it matter?** The type of cast helps your provider figure out what is going on in your kidneys. Some casts are benign (hyaline), while others point to serious kidney problems.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test. This test is reported qualitatively. The test section appears without specific level ranges in the OHS file, indicating it is reported as present/absent without graded categories.

**Optimal (None to rare):** This is the goal. It means healthy kidney tubules. To stay here: keep up enough water, balanced exercise, and supportive kidney health (low sodium, plant-centered diet, controlled blood pressure and blood sugar). Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High:** A high cast count, especially with non-hyaline casts, means significant kidney injury. RBC casts suggest glomerulonephritis. WBC casts suggest kidney infection or interstitial nephritis (inflammation of the kidney's tubules and surrounding tissue). Granular casts suggest acute tubular necrosis. Fatty casts suggest nephrotic syndrome. Waxy and broad casts suggest chronic kidney disease. To address: work with a provider. They will order a full kidney workup including creatinine, BUN, eGFR, cystatin C, urine protein, and possibly kidney biopsy. Treat the underlying cause.

### Amorphous Sediment (Urine)

**What is this test?** This test looks for amorphous sediment (a general term for non-crystalline material) in your urine. It includes things like urates, phosphates, and carbonates. Small amounts are normal, especially in concentrated urine.

**Why does it matter?** It mostly tells you about hydration and urine pH. Larger amounts often mean concentrated urine (dehydration) or very alkaline/acidic urine.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A "Functional High" or "Clinical High" usually refers to large amounts of sediment that may need follow-up.

**Optimal (None to rare):** This is the goal. It means enough hydration and balanced urine chemistry. To stay here: keep up enough water, balanced diet, and supportive kidney health. Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High:** A high amorphous sediment count usually means concentrated or alkaline/acidic urine. It is rarely a problem on its own. To address: drink more water. Check acid-base balance and diet. If it stays high with other abnormalities, work with a provider.

### Transitional Epithelial Cells (Urine)

**What is this test?** Transitional epithelial cells (also called urothelial cells) line the bladder, ureters, and renal pelvis (the inside of the kidney where urine collects). Small amounts can be normal, especially in older adults. Larger amounts can mean irritation, infection, stones, or rarely cancer.

**Why does it matter?** It can point to bladder, ureter, or kidney irritation or disease. It is especially important if you have blood in your urine.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative or rare result is the goal. A high count, especially with blood in urine, needs evaluation.

**Optimal (None to rare):** This is the goal. It means healthy bladder, ureters, and renal pelvis. To stay here: keep up enough water, a whole-food diet, and supportive urinary tract health. Avoid smoking (a major risk factor for bladder cancer). Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High:** A high count, especially with blood in urine, needs evaluation. Causes: bladder cancer (especially in smokers, older adults, those with chemical exposures), bladder stones, chronic UTI, indwelling catheter (a catheter that stays in the bladder for a long time), recent urinary tract procedure, and rarely other urothelial cancers. To address: work with a provider. They will order a cystoscopy (a small camera used to look inside the bladder), urine cytology (a test that looks at cells in urine for cancer), and imaging. Quit smoking. Treat the underlying condition. Drink more water.

### Renal Epithelial Cells (Urine)

**What is this test?** Renal epithelial cells (also called tubular cells) line the kidney tubules. They are not normally found in urine in significant numbers. Their presence means the kidney tubules are injured.

**Why does it matter?** It is a sign of kidney tubular injury. Any significant number needs evaluation.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative or rare result is the goal. A high count means kidney injury.

**Optimal (None to rare):** This is the goal. It means healthy kidney tubules with no significant injury. To stay here: keep up enough water, balanced diet, healthy blood pressure and blood sugar, and avoid kidney-toxic substances. Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High:** A high count means significant kidney tubular injury. Causes: acute tubular necrosis (sudden kidney tubular damage from lack of blood flow or toxins — most common), acute interstitial nephritis (sudden inflammation of the kidney's tubules and surrounding tissue), glomerulonephritis, kidney infection, kidney transplant rejection, and exposure to kidney toxins (contrast dye for imaging, aminoglycosides, heavy metals). To address: work with a provider. They will do a full kidney workup. Treat the cause. Avoid kidney-toxic substances. A kidney specialist may be needed.

### Triple Phosphate Crystals (Urine)

**What is this test?** This test looks for triple phosphate crystals (also called struvite, or magnesium ammonium phosphate) in your urine. They look like coffin lids under the microscope. They form in alkaline urine and are linked to UTIs with bacteria that produce urease (an enzyme that breaks urea into ammonia, which makes urine alkaline), such as Proteus, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas, and Ureaplasma. They are also a major part of struvite kidney stones.

**Why does it matter?** Triple phosphate crystals and struvite stones can grow large and form staghorn calculi (stones that fill the kidney's collecting system). They are a sign of chronic UTI with certain bacteria.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative result is the goal. A positive result (especially with symptoms) needs follow-up.

**Optimal (Negative):** This is the goal. It means your urine pH is not too alkaline and there is no urease-producing bacterial infection. To stay here: keep up enough water, good hygiene, urinate after sex (for women), and avoid holding urine. Cranberry, D-mannose, and probiotics can help. Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High (Positive, especially with symptoms):** A high count, especially with symptoms (painful urination, urgency, frequency, flank pain) or recurrent UTIs, needs evaluation. Causes: chronic UTI with urease-producing bacteria (Proteus most common), struvite kidney stones (can grow large and form staghorn calculi), and persistently alkaline urine. To address: work with a provider. Urine culture guides antibiotics. Struvite stones may need surgical removal (lithotripsy — using sound waves to break up stones, or percutaneous nephrolithotomy — surgery through the back to remove stones). Drink more water. Acidify urine. Treat the underlying UTI. Cranberry, D-mannose, and probiotics can help prevent recurrence.

### Granular Cast (Urine)

**What is this test?** This test looks for granular casts in your urine. These are tube-shaped casts that contain granules from cell debris, plasma proteins, or clumped Tamm-Horsfall protein. "Fine granular" casts suggest milder injury. "Coarse granular" casts (sometimes called "muddy brown casts") suggest more severe injury, often acute tubular necrosis.

**Why does it matter?** Granular casts usually mean the kidney tubules are injured. Coarse granular casts are a classic sign of acute tubular necrosis, a serious kidney condition.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative or rare result is the goal. A high count means kidney injury.

**Optimal (None):** This is the goal. It means healthy kidney tubules with no injury. To stay here: keep up enough water, balanced diet, healthy blood pressure and blood sugar, and avoid kidney-toxic substances. Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High:** A high count means kidney tubular injury. Causes: acute tubular necrosis (most common — from lack of blood flow, sepsis, or kidney toxins), acute interstitial nephritis, glomerulonephritis, kidney infection, and chronic kidney disease. Coarse granular casts ("muddy brown casts") are classic for acute tubular necrosis. To address: work with a provider. They will do a full kidney workup. Treat the cause. Avoid kidney-toxic substances. A kidney specialist may be needed.

### Yeast (Urine)

**What is this test?** This test looks for yeast in your urine, most commonly Candida (a type of fungus). It can mean a Candida UTI, vaginal contamination, or rarely a widespread Candida infection.

**Why does it matter?** Candida UTIs are more common in people with catheters, diabetes, weak immune systems, and those on broad-spectrum antibiotics (antibiotics that kill many types of bacteria).

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative result is the goal. A positive result (especially with symptoms) needs evaluation.

**Optimal (Negative):** This is the goal. It means no Candida in your urinary tract. To stay here: keep up a whole-food, low-sugar diet (sugar feeds Candida), supportive immune and gut health, and good hygiene. Avoid unnecessary antibiotics. Probiotics can help keep a healthy microbiome. Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High:** A high count, especially with symptoms (painful urination, urgency, frequency) or risk factors (catheter, diabetes, weak immune system, broad-spectrum antibiotics), means a Candida UTI. To address: work with a provider. Urine culture confirms the diagnosis. Antifungal therapy (fluconazole, amphotericin B for severe cases) is usually needed. Address underlying risk factors. Cut back on sugar. Probiotics and antifungal herbs can help. For recurrent Candida UTIs, check for candidiasis, diabetes, and immune problems.

### Uric Acid Crystals (Urine)

**What is this test?** This test looks for uric acid crystals in your urine. They can be diamond, rhombic, or rosette-shaped under the microscope. They form in acidic urine. They can be normal in small amounts, especially with a purine-rich diet or gout. Larger amounts raise the risk of uric acid kidney stones.

**Why does it matter?** Uric acid crystals in urine can mean gout, metabolic syndrome, high cell turnover (chemotherapy, psoriasis), or a high-purine diet. Uric acid kidney stones are a known complication.

**Reference ranges (qualitative, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively. A negative or rare result is the goal. A high count, especially with symptoms or history, needs follow-up.

**Optimal (None to rare):** This is the goal. It means balanced purine metabolism, healthy urine pH, and low kidney stone risk. To stay here: keep up enough water (2+ liters a day). Eat a balanced diet with moderate purine intake. Eat alkaline-promoting foods (vegetables, fruits, citrus). Avoid alcohol (especially beer). Periodic urinalysis is enough.

**Clinical High (Many, with symptoms or risk factors):** A high count, especially with symptoms (flank pain, blood in urine, gout attacks) or persistently acidic urine, is linked to higher risk of uric acid kidney stones. Causes: gout, metabolic syndrome, high-purine diet, dehydration, high cell turnover (chemo, psoriasis), chronic diarrhea (loss of alkali), and rare genetic disorders (Lesch-Nyhan syndrome, very rare). To address: work with a provider. A 24-hour urine collection for stone risk is often done. Drink more water. Alkalize urine (potassium citrate is standard). Cut back on purines. Lose extra weight. Avoid alcohol. For gout, allopurinol or febuxostat may be needed.
