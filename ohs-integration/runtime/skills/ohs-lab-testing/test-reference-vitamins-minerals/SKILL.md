# Vitamins, Iron, and Zinc — Test Reference

## When to Use
When a customer, employee, or practitioner asks for the meaning, optimal range, or interpretation of any lab test in this group. **Reference only — never substitute for a provider's interpretation.** See `ohs-compliance/disclaimers` (the "share with your provider" framing) for the required patient-facing language.

## Procedure
1. Identify the test the user is asking about.
2. Quote the test name exactly as it appears on the lab report (with the OHS reference range applied).
3. Read out the five-level range (Clinical Low / Functional Low / Optimal / Functional High / Clinical High) and the units.
4. Summarize the "What is this test?" and "Why does it matter?" sections in plain language.
5. For any out-of-range result, recommend the customer share the result with their healthcare provider.

## Pitfalls
- **Do not interpret any test result for a customer.** The lab description here is **reference** content. The customer's provider is the one who interprets.
- **Do not recommend a specific dose of a supplement** based on a test result. The right phrasing is "this marker is associated with [nutrient / lifestyle factors] — your provider can advise on whether to add a supplement, and at what dose."
- **Do not rephrase the FDA structure-function disclaimer away.** If the answer includes any "supports / is associated with" language about a product, also include the disclaimer (see `ohs-compliance/disclaimers`).
- **Do not imply a single test is sufficient.** Most health questions need a pattern of markers, not a single value, to be meaningful.
- **Do not state ranges with more precision than the source.** The five levels in this skill come from `knowledgebase/labs/ohs_lab_test_descriptions_v3.md` (OHS-authored). Do not invent a tighter or different range.
- **Range sex specificity (M / F / B):** respect the M / F / B annotation in each test.

## Verification

## Anything else

### The five-level range system (every test uses this)
- **Clinical Low** — below the lab range. Often means a real health problem.
- **Functional Low** — in the lab range but on the low end. Not sick, but not at your best.
- **Optimal** — the level where your body works its best. The goal.
- **Functional High** — in the lab range but on the high end. Body is under stress.
- **Clinical High** — above the lab range. Often means a real health problem.

### Sex-specific ranges
**B** = both sexes · **M** = males only · **F** = females only.

## Test Descriptions

### Vitamin B12

**What is this test?** Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is a vitamin that helps your body make red blood cells, DNA, and nerve coverings. It is also key for energy and methylation (a process that helps your body turn on and off genes, make neurotransmitters, and process toxins). Methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine are more sensitive tests for B12 function.

**Why does it matter?** Low B12 causes anemia, nerve damage, brain fog, sad mood, and high homocysteine (which raises heart risk). Vegans and vegetarians are at high risk. Stomach acid declines with age, so older adults are at risk too.

**Reference ranges (pg/mL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–200  
- Functional Low: 201–399  
- **Optimal: 400–950**  
- Functional High: 951–1100  
- Clinical High: 1101 and above

**Clinical Low (below 201 pg/mL):** This causes megaloblastic anemia (a type of anemia with large red blood cells), nerve damage in the hands and feet, memory loss, depression, fatigue, swollen tongue, and high homocysteine. Causes: not enough B12 in your diet (vegans, vegetarians), malabsorption (pernicious anemia — a condition where the body cannot absorb B12, celiac, IBD, SIBO, H. pylori, gastric bypass), certain medicines (PPIs, metformin, H2 blockers), and rare genetic disorders. To address: work with a provider. B12 supplements are key — injections for severe deficiency or malabsorption, sublingual (under the tongue) or oral for milder cases. Eat B12-rich foods (grass-fed red meat, fish, eggs, dairy, fortified nutritional yeast). Treat the cause. MMA and homocysteine are better tests.

**Functional Low (201–399 pg/mL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It is linked to subtle fatigue, brain fog, mood changes, nerve symptoms, and high homocysteine. Many people in this range have high MMA (a more sensitive marker) and would benefit from supplements. Causes: not enough B12 in your diet, mild malabsorption, certain medicines (PPIs, metformin), and aging (less stomach acid). To raise it: eat B12-rich foods (animal products or fortified nutritional yeast for vegans). Consider B12 supplements (methylcobalamin or hydroxocobalamin, 500–1000 mcg a day sublingual). A provider can check MMA, homocysteine, and gut health.

**Optimal (400–950 pg/mL):** This is the goal. It means you are getting enough B12, absorbing it well, and your methylation is working well. To stay here: keep up a whole-food diet with B12 (animal products or fortified foods for vegans). For vegans and those with malabsorption, consider ongoing B12 supplements (500–1000 mcg a day methylcobalamin). Periodically re-check along with MMA and homocysteine.

**Functional High (951–1100 pg/mL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It usually means you are taking supplements, eat a lot of B12-rich foods, or rarely it can mean liver disease, certain cancers, or kidney disease. In most cases of supplementation, this is harmless. If very high without supplements, a provider can check for liver disease or certain blood disorders. Continue your current B12 plan.

**Clinical High (1101 pg/mL and above):** Very high B12 is rare and usually from high-dose supplements. Without supplements, it can be linked to liver disease, kidney disease, certain cancers (especially blood cancers), and rarely other conditions. To address: work with a provider to check for underlying conditions if B12 is very high without supplements. Cut back on supplement dose if it is too high.

### Vitamin D (25-OH Vitamin D)

**What is this test?** Vitamin D is a vitamin and a prohormone (your body turns it into a hormone). It is critical for bone health, immune function, mood, hormone regulation, and cell health. Your skin makes vitamin D from sunlight. You can also get it from food (fatty fish, fortified dairy, egg yolks) and supplements. The 25-OH form is the best marker of your vitamin D status.

**Why does it matter?** Vitamin D is one of the most common nutritional deficiencies. Low vitamin D is linked to weak bones, frequent infections, autoimmune disease, depression, fatigue, and higher risk of many chronic diseases (cancer, heart disease, diabetes).

**Reference ranges (ng/mL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–40  
- Functional Low: 41–59  
- **Optimal: 60–90**  
- Functional High: 91–109  
- Clinical High: 110 and above

**Clinical Low (below 41 ng/mL):** This is vitamin D deficiency or insufficiency. It causes rickets in children, osteomalacia in adults (soft bones), osteoporosis, higher fracture risk, frequent infections, autoimmune disease, depression, fatigue, and higher risk of chronic disease. Causes: not enough sun exposure, dark skin, malabsorption (celiac, IBD, gastric bypass), obesity (vitamin D gets trapped in fat), kidney or liver disease, certain medicines (steroids, anticonvulsants), and aging. To address: work with a provider. Take vitamin D3 supplements (2000–5000 IU a day, sometimes more). Eat vitamin D-rich foods (fatty fish, fortified dairy, egg yolks). Treat malabsorption and obesity.

**Functional Low (41–59 ng/mL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It is linked to subtle fatigue, mood changes, frequent infections, bone loss, and higher long-term disease risk. The OHS functional range is tighter than the conventional lab range — this level may not yet be a clinical concern but is not yet optimal. To optimize: take vitamin D3 supplements (2000–4000 IU a day) with a fat-containing meal for better absorption. Eat vitamin D-rich foods (fatty fish, fortified dairy, egg yolks). Get safe sun exposure (15–30 minutes of midday sun on bare skin). A provider can check gut, liver, and kidney if your levels stay low.

**Optimal (60–90 ng/mL):** This is the goal. It means your bones, immune system, mood, and long-term health are well-supported. To stay here: keep up a whole-food diet rich in vitamin D (fatty fish, fortified dairy, egg yolks), safe sun exposure, and supplements if needed (1000–2000 IU a day for maintenance). Co-factors for vitamin D are magnesium, vitamin K2, and zinc — make sure you have enough. Periodically re-check, especially in winter or with lifestyle changes.

**Functional High (91–109 ng/mL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It usually means high-dose supplements and is generally safe. To optimize: watch for signs of too much (nausea, vomiting, constipation, weakness, kidney stones). Check calcium and PTH (parathyroid hormone). Cut back on dose if needed. Keep taking co-factors (magnesium, K2, zinc) for healthy calcium balance.

**Clinical High (110 ng/mL and above):** Very high vitamin D (usually from too much supplementation) can cause hypercalcemia (high blood calcium). Symptoms: nausea, vomiting, constipation, weakness, confusion, kidney stones, and irregular heart beats. To address: stop vitamin D supplements and work with a provider. Stop calcium supplements. Drink plenty of water. Time usually clears mild-moderate elevations. Severe toxicity may need hospital care. Re-check vitamin D and calcium.

### Iron, Total (Serum Iron)

**What is this test?** Serum iron measures how much iron is in your blood, mostly attached to a protein called transferrin. It changes a lot during the day and with meals, so it is best looked at with other iron tests (TIBC, transferrin saturation, ferritin).

**Why does it matter?** It tells you about your iron status. Low iron causes anemia, fatigue, brain fog, and hair loss. High iron can damage your liver, heart, and pancreas (see hemochromatosis).

**Reference ranges (μg/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–49  
- Functional Low: 50–99  
- **Optimal: 100–150**  
- Functional High: 151–180  
- Clinical High: 181 and above

**Clinical Low (below 50 μg/dL):** This means iron deficiency — the most common nutritional deficiency in the world. Causes: not enough iron in your diet, malabsorption (celiac, IBD, SIBO, H. pylori), chronic blood loss (gut, menstrual, ulcers), pregnancy, growth spurts, and chronic inflammation. You may feel tired, foggy, lose hair, have brittle nails, restless legs, crave non-food items (pica), and have trouble exercising. To address: work with a provider. Eat iron-rich foods (grass-fed red meat, organ meats, dark leafy greens, lentils, fortified cereals) with vitamin C for better absorption. Take iron supplements (iron bisglycinate is gentle on the gut).

**Functional Low (50–99 μg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early iron deficiency, not enough iron in your diet, mild malabsorption, or chronic inflammation. You may feel tired, foggy, lose hair, or have trouble exercising. To raise it: eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C. Cut back on coffee and tea with meals (tannins block iron absorption). Consider iron supplements if ferritin is also low. A provider can check ferritin, TIBC, transferrin saturation, and gut health.

**Optimal (100–150 μg/dL):** This is the goal. It means you are getting enough iron, absorbing it well, and have no chronic blood loss. To stay here: keep up a whole-food diet with heme iron (iron from animal sources: grass-fed red meat, organ meats, sardines) and plant iron (dark leafy greens, lentils, fortified cereals), with vitamin C. Avoid coffee and tea with meals. Periodically re-check, especially for menstruating women, pregnant women, and those with gut problems.

**Functional High (151–180 μg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean a lot of iron in your diet, iron supplements, hemochromatosis (a genetic disorder where your body absorbs too much iron), liver disease, hemolysis (when red blood cells are destroyed), or low B6. You may have more oxidative stress over time (oxidative stress is cell damage from unstable molecules called free radicals). To optimize: cut back on iron supplements if you take too many. Limit heme iron. Eat more iron-absorption blockers (coffee, tea, calcium with meals). Check ferritin and transferrin saturation. A provider can check ferritin, transferrin saturation, liver, and the HFE gene (for hemochromatosis).

**Clinical High (181 μg/dL and above):** This needs evaluation. Causes: iron supplements, hemochromatosis (HFE gene mutations), liver disease, hemolytic anemia, sideroblastic anemia (a rare disorder where the body cannot use iron to make hemoglobin), lead poisoning, and acute iron poisoning. Iron overload damages the liver, heart, pancreas, and joints. To address: work with a provider. Treatment may include phlebotomy (blood removal) for hemochromatosis, iron chelation (medicines that bind and remove iron), and dietary changes. Avoid vitamin C with meals if you have iron overload.

### Iron Binding Capacity (TIBC)

**What is this test?** TIBC (Total Iron Binding Capacity) measures how well your blood can carry iron. It mainly reflects transferrin, the main protein that carries iron in your blood. High TIBC usually means your body is trying to grab more iron (iron deficiency). Low TIBC usually means your body has plenty of iron or has inflammation, liver disease, or malnutrition.

**Why does it matter?** It helps figure out if your iron is low because of deficiency, inflammation, or overload. It is most useful when looked at with serum iron and transferrin saturation.

**Reference ranges (μg/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–124  
- Functional Low: 125–200  
- **Optimal: 201–400**  
- Functional High: 401–450  
- Clinical High: 451 and above

**Clinical Low (below 125 μg/dL):** This means iron overload, chronic inflammation, liver disease, malnutrition, or protein loss. Common causes: hemochromatosis, chronic inflammation, liver disease, nephrotic syndrome (a kidney disorder that causes protein loss in urine), and severe malnutrition. You may have iron overload, fatigue, and organ damage. To address: work with a provider. Treat the cause. For hemochromatosis, phlebotomy is the standard treatment. For inflammation, address the underlying cause. For liver disease, support your liver.

**Functional Low (125–200 μg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early iron overload, low-grade chronic inflammation, or mild liver problems. To optimize: address any inflammation or iron overload. Cut back on iron supplements if you take too many. Limit heme iron. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet. A provider can check ferritin, transferrin saturation, liver, and the HFE gene.

**Optimal (201–400 μg/dL):** This is the goal. It means balanced iron metabolism with enough transferrin and no iron overload or deficiency. To stay here: keep up a whole-food diet, exercise, sleep 7–9 hours, manage stress, avoid smoking, limit alcohol, and maintain a healthy gut. Periodically re-check, especially if you have risk factors for iron problems.

**Functional High (401–450 μg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It often means early iron deficiency (your body is making more transferrin to grab scarce iron), not enough iron in your diet, or mild malabsorption. You may feel tired or have trouble exercising. To optimize: eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C. Cut back on coffee and tea with meals. A provider can check ferritin, transferrin saturation, and gut health.

**Clinical High (451 μg/dL and above):** This is a classic finding in iron deficiency anemia. Causes: iron deficiency (most common), pregnancy, birth control pills, and acute hepatitis. You may feel tired, foggy, lose hair, and have trouble exercising. To address: work with a provider. Take iron supplements (iron bisglycinate or ferrous sulfate) with vitamin C. Eat iron-rich foods. Check for chronic blood loss (gut, menstrual).

### % Saturation (Transferrin Saturation)

**What is this test?** Transferrin saturation is your serum iron divided by your TIBC. It shows what percentage of your transferrin is full of iron. It is the main screening test for iron overload (hemochromatosis).

**Why does it matter?** It tells you how full your iron taxis are. Low saturation means your body is short on iron. High saturation means your body has too much iron.

**Reference ranges (%, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–14  
- Functional Low: 15–19  
- **Optimal: 20–45**  
- Functional High: 46–54  
- Clinical High: 55 and above

**Clinical Low (below 15%):** This means iron deficiency. It is one of the earliest and most specific signs of iron deficiency, often showing up before anemia. Causes: not enough iron in your diet, malabsorption, chronic blood loss, pregnancy, and growth spurts. To address: work with a provider. Take iron supplements with vitamin C. Eat iron-rich foods. A provider can check ferritin, gut health, and sources of blood loss.

**Functional Low (15–19%):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early iron deficiency or low iron stores before anemia develops. You may feel tired, foggy, lose hair, have restless legs, or have trouble exercising. To optimize: eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C. Cut back on coffee and tea with meals. Consider iron supplements if ferritin is also low. A provider can check ferritin, gut, and causes of iron loss.

**Optimal (20–45%):** This is the goal. It means balanced iron metabolism with enough iron stores and no overload. To stay here: keep up a whole-food diet with heme and plant iron, with vitamin C. Avoid coffee and tea with meals. Periodically re-check, especially for menstruating women, pregnant women, and those with gut problems.

**Functional High (46–54%):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean a lot of iron in your diet, iron supplements, hemochromatosis, liver disease, or hemolysis. You may have more oxidative stress over time. To optimize: cut back on iron supplements if you take too many. Limit heme iron. Eat more iron-absorption blockers. A provider can check ferritin, liver, and the HFE gene.

**Clinical High (55% and above):** This is the screening cutoff for hemochromatosis (HFE gene mutations — C282Y, H63D). Sustained saturation above 45% needs genetic testing. Causes: hereditary hemochromatosis (most common in people of Northern European descent), iron supplements, liver disease, hemolytic anemia, and sideroblastic anemia. Iron overload damages the liver, heart, pancreas, and joints. To address: work with a provider. They will order genetic testing for HFE, ferritin, and liver tests. Treatment is phlebotomy (regular blood removal) for hemochromatosis. Cut back on iron intake. Avoid vitamin C with meals.

### Ferritin, Male

**What is this test?** Ferritin is a protein that stores iron in your body. It reflects your total iron stores. It is the best single marker of iron status, especially for iron deficiency. Ferritin is also an acute phase reactant, meaning it goes up with inflammation, infection, and liver disease — so a "normal" ferritin can hide iron deficiency when there is inflammation.

**Why does it matter?** Low ferritin is the earliest sign of iron deficiency, even before anemia shows up. It causes fatigue, brain fog, hair loss, brittle nails, and restless legs. High ferritin can mean iron overload (hemochromatosis), inflammation, or liver disease.

**Reference ranges (ng/mL, males only):**

- Clinical Low: 0–12.99  
- Functional Low: 13.0–50.99  
- **Optimal: 51.0–100.99**  
- Functional High: 101.0–300.99  
- Clinical High: 301.0 and above

**Clinical Low (below 13.0 ng/mL):** This is diagnostic for iron deficiency, even before anemia develops. It is the earliest and most sensitive sign of iron depletion. Causes: not enough iron in your diet, malabsorption (celiac, IBD, SIBO, H. pylori, gastric bypass), chronic blood loss (gut, ulcers), and chronic inflammation. You may feel tired, foggy, lose hair, have brittle nails, restless legs, crave non-food items (pica), and have trouble exercising. To address: work with a provider. Take iron supplements (iron bisglycinate is gentle on the gut, 25–50 mg a day) with vitamin C. Eat heme iron (iron from animal sources: grass-fed red meat, organ meats, sardines) and plant iron (dark leafy greens, lentils, fortified cereals) with vitamin C. Check for chronic blood loss (gut).

**Functional Low (13.0–50.99 ng/mL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It means your iron stores are too low for your body to work at its best, even before anemia develops. It is linked to fatigue, brain fog, hair loss, brittle nails, restless legs, exercise intolerance, and reduced thyroid function (your body needs iron to turn T4 into T3). To raise it: eat iron-rich foods (heme and plant sources) with vitamin C. Consider iron supplements (iron bisglycinate, 18–36 mg a day). A provider can check ferritin, iron studies, thyroid, and gut health.

**Optimal (51.0–100.99 ng/mL):** This is the goal. It means you have enough iron for your body to work at its best — energy, thinking, thyroid function, and exercise tolerance. To stay here: keep up a whole-food diet rich in heme iron (grass-fed red meat, organ meats, sardines) and plant iron (dark leafy greens, lentils, fortified cereals) with vitamin C. Avoid coffee and tea with meals. Periodic re-testing is a good idea.

**Functional High (101.0–300.99 ng/mL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. Causes: iron supplements, high dietary iron, inflammation (acute phase reactant), liver disease, metabolic syndrome, alcohol use, and hemochromatosis (HFE gene mutations). Ferritin above 100 with normal iron studies and no inflammation is usually harmless. With inflammation, high ferritin can hide iron deficiency. To optimize: look for the cause. Cut back on iron supplements if needed. Limit alcohol. A provider can check fasting insulin, liver, transferrin saturation, and the HFE gene.

**Clinical High (301.0 ng/mL and above):** A high ferritin in men needs evaluation. Causes: hemochromatosis (most common in men of Northern European descent — HFE gene mutations C282Y, H63D), liver disease, metabolic syndrome, inflammation, cancer, and iron supplements. Hemochromatosis causes iron overload with damage to the liver, heart, pancreas, and joints. Symptoms: fatigue, joint pain, belly pain, bronze skin, diabetes, and heart problems. To address: work with a provider. They will order genetic testing for HFE, transferrin saturation, liver tests, and iron studies. Treatment for hemochromatosis is phlebotomy (regular blood removal). Cut back on iron intake. Avoid vitamin C with meals. Avoid alcohol.

### Ferritin (Combined Reference)

**Note:** Ferritin has different reference ranges for males and females. See "Ferritin, Male" and "Ferritin, Female" sections above for the full male and female descriptions with their specific reference ranges.

**Why different ranges?** Men and women have different iron needs and losses. Women of menstrual age lose iron each month through menstruation, so the Clinical High threshold is lower in women. After menopause, women's ranges move closer to men's.

### Zinc

**What is this test?** Zinc is a trace mineral (a mineral your body needs in small amounts) that is involved in over 300 chemical reactions in your body. It is critical for immune function, wound healing, taste and smell, hormone production, DNA synthesis, protein synthesis, and antioxidant defenses. It is also important for testosterone production, thyroid function, and skin health.

**Why does it matter?** Zinc deficiency is very common, especially in older adults, vegetarians/vegans, and those with gut malabsorption. Low zinc weakens your immune system, slows wound healing, lowers testosterone, and worsens thyroid function.

**Reference ranges (μg/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–59  
- Functional Low: 60–79  
- **Optimal: 80–100**  
- Functional High: 101–130  
- Clinical High: above 130

**Clinical Low (below 60 μg/dL):** This is significant zinc deficiency. It can cause frequent infections, poor wound healing, hair loss, diarrhea, skin rashes (especially around the mouth, eyes, and genitals), taste and smell problems, white spots on nails, infertility, and slow growth in children. Causes: not enough zinc in your diet, malabsorption (celiac, IBD, SIBO, gastric bypass), alcoholism, chronic diarrhea, certain medicines (diuretics, ACE inhibitors, PPIs), pregnancy, lactation, and chronic illness. To address: work with a provider. Take zinc supplements (zinc picolinate, citrate, or glycinate, 15–50 mg a day). Eat zinc-rich foods (oysters, grass-fed red meat, pumpkin seeds, chickpeas, lentils, nuts, dairy). Treat malabsorption.

**Functional Low (60–79 μg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It is linked to subtle immune suppression, slow wound healing, mild taste/smell changes, low libido, mild skin issues, and reduced thyroid function. Subclinical zinc deficiency is very common, especially in older adults, vegetarians, and those with chronic stress. To raise it: eat zinc-rich foods (oysters, grass-fed red meat, pumpkin seeds, chickpeas, lentils, nuts, dairy). Consider zinc supplements (zinc picolinate, 15–30 mg a day). Take with food to avoid nausea. Balance with copper (1–2 mg) if you supplement long-term. A provider can check zinc, gut, and diet.

**Optimal (80–100 μg/dL):** This is the goal. It means you have enough zinc for strong immune function, healthy wound healing, balanced hormone production, and good antioxidant defenses. To stay here: keep up a diet rich in zinc (oysters, grass-fed red meat, pumpkin seeds, chickpeas, lentils, nuts, dairy). For vegetarians and vegans, supplementation is often needed (15–30 mg a day) because plant zinc is not absorbed as well. Periodic re-checking is enough.

**Functional High (101–130 μg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It usually means zinc supplements or a lot of dietary zinc. Too much zinc can cause copper deficiency (zinc and copper compete for absorption), nausea, vomiting, and reduced immune function. To optimize: if supplementing, make sure your dose is not too high. Balance with copper (1–2 mg) if you supplement long-term. Take with food.

**Clinical High (above 130 μg/dL):** Very high zinc is rare and usually from too much supplementation. It can cause copper deficiency (with anemia, low neutrophil count, and nerve symptoms), nausea, vomiting, and reduced HDL cholesterol (the "good" cholesterol). To address: work with a provider. Cut back or stop zinc supplements. Take copper (1–2 mg) to restore balance. Re-check zinc and copper.
