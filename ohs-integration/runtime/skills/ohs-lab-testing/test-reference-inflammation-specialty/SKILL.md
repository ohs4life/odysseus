# Inflammation and Specialty Cardiac / Tumor Markers — Test Reference

## When to Use
When a customer, employee, or practitioner asks for the meaning, optimal range, or interpretation of any lab test in this group. **Reference only — never substitute for a provider's interpretation.** See `ohs-compliance/disclaimers` (the "share with your provider" framing) for the required patient-facing language.

## Procedure
1. Identify the test the user is asking about.
2. Quote the test name exactly as it appears on the lab report (with the OHS reference range applied).
3. Read out the five-level range (Clinical Low / Functional Low / Optimal / Functional High / Clinical High) and the units.
4. Summarize the "What is this test?" and "Why does it matter?" sections in plain language.
5. For any out-of-range result, recommend the customer share the result with their healthcare provider.

## Pitfalls
- **Do not interpret any test result for a customer.** The lab description here is **reference** content. The customer's provider is the one who interprets.
- **Do not recommend a specific dose of a supplement** based on a test result. The right phrasing is "this marker is associated with [nutrient / lifestyle factors] — your provider can advise on whether to add a supplement, and at what dose."
- **Do not rephrase the FDA structure-function disclaimer away.** If the answer includes any "supports / is associated with" language about a product, also include the disclaimer (see `ohs-compliance/disclaimers`).
- **Do not imply a single test is sufficient.** Most health questions need a pattern of markers, not a single value, to be meaningful.
- **Do not state ranges with more precision than the source.** The five levels in this skill come from `knowledgebase/labs/ohs_lab_test_descriptions_v3.md` (OHS-authored). Do not invent a tighter or different range.
- **Range sex specificity (M / F / B):** respect the M / F / B annotation in each test.

## Anything else

### The five-level range system (every test uses this)
- **Clinical Low** — below the lab range. Often means a real health problem.
- **Functional Low** — in the lab range but on the low end. Not sick, but not at your best.
- **Optimal** — the level where your body works its best. The goal.
- **Functional High** — in the lab range but on the high end. Body is under stress.
- **Clinical High** — above the lab range. Often means a real health problem.

### Sex-specific ranges
**B** = both sexes · **M** = males only · **F** = females only.

## Test Descriptions

### Apolipoprotein B (ApoB)

**What is this test?** ApoB is the main protein in all the "bad" cholesterol particles — LDL, VLDL, IDL, Lp(a), and chylomicron remnants. Each atherogenic particle (a particle that can form plaque in your arteries) has exactly one ApoB molecule, so ApoB is the most accurate count of these particles. ApoB is a stronger predictor of heart risk than LDL or non-HDL cholesterol.

**Why does it matter?** It is the best single number for the number of atherogenic particles in your blood. Many people with normal LDL still have high ApoB (more small, dense particles). A standard lipid panel misses many people with high ApoB.

**Reference ranges (mg/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–40  
- Functional Low: 40–59  
- **Optimal: 60–80**  
- Functional High: 81–100  
- Clinical High: above 100

**Clinical Low (below 40 mg/dL):** A very low ApoB is linked to very low heart risk but may mean very low LDL (from a low-fat diet, malabsorption, liver disease, or genetic factors). It is usually not a problem. People in this range usually have excellent heart health. To maintain: keep up a whole-food, plant-centered diet, exercise, healthy body composition, and stress control. If LDL is also very low, make sure you get enough healthy fats. Periodic monitoring is enough.

**Functional Low (40–59 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It is generally excellent and is linked to very low heart risk. It is common in people on whole-food, plant-based diets and regular exercisers. To maintain: keep up a high-fiber, plant-centered diet with enough omega-3 fats, regular exercise, and healthy body composition.

**Optimal (60–80 mg/dL):** This is the goal. It means low atherogenic particle number and very low heart risk. People in this range usually have a whole-food, plant-centered diet, regular exercise, healthy body composition, and well-regulated blood sugar. To stay here: keep up fiber (25–40 grams a day), plant sterols, omega-3 fats, and a colorful plant-based diet. Exercise daily. Manage stress. Sleep 7–9 hours. Check with other lipid markers (LDL, HDL, triglycerides, hs-CRP, Lp(a)).

**Functional High (81–100 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It means more atherogenic particles than optimal and is linked to higher heart risk, especially with other risk factors. Common causes: insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, obesity, a diet high in saturated fat and refined carbs, sedentary lifestyle, underactive thyroid, and your genes. To lower it: find and address the cause. Cut back on saturated fat and refined carbs. Eat more fiber and plant sterols. Exercise daily. Lose extra body fat. A provider can check fasting insulin, thyroid, and inflammation.

**Clinical High (above 100 mg/dL):** A high ApoB is a strong independent predictor of heart disease. It means a high atherogenic particle burden and a much higher risk of atherosclerosis (plaque buildup in arteries), heart attack, and stroke, especially with other risk factors (high Lp(a) — a genetic variant that raises heart risk, high hs-CRP, family history, diabetes, high blood pressure). Causes: genetic factors (familial hypercholesterolemia, ApoB mutations), long-term insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, poor diet, sedentary lifestyle, underactive thyroid, and chronic inflammation. To address: work with a provider. Aggressive lifestyle changes — a whole-food, plant-centered diet rich in fiber, beans, vegetables, and nuts — combined with daily exercise, weight loss, and stress reduction are foundational. Natural lipid-lowering agents (bergamot, red yeast rice, plant sterols, psyllium, berberine) and omega-3s can help. Statins and other medicines are very effective at lowering ApoB. Target ApoB below 80 mg/dL (or below 60 with heart disease risk).

# Summary

This guide has covered 82+ common blood and urine tests. Each test is explained through both lab and functional lenses, with reference ranges from the OHS test ranges file. The lab range is the average for the general population (which includes many people with chronic disease). The functional range is a tighter range from research. Being in the functional range means your body is working at its best and your future risk of health problems is lower.

**Tests with separate Male and Female versions in this guide:**

- Testosterone, Total, Male / Testosterone, Total, Female  
- Testosterone, Free, Male / Testosterone, Free, Female  
- Testosterone, Bioavailable, Male / Testosterone, Bioavailable, Female  
- Ferritin, Male / Ferritin, Female

**The basics that help most tests:**

- A whole-food, plant-centered diet rich in fiber, antioxidants, omega-3 fats, and key micronutrients  
- Regular exercise (both strength training and cardio)  
- Sleep 7–9 hours a night  
- Stress management (meditation, deep breathing, time in nature, social connection)  
- Avoid toxins (alcohol, tobacco, environmental chemicals)  
- Stay well hydrated (drink to thirst, usually 2–3 liters a day)  
- Maintain a healthy body composition  
- Support your gut health (fiber, fermented foods, probiotics)  
- Support your liver health (leafy greens, beets, cruciferous vegetables, coffee)  
- Get regular safe sun exposure for vitamin D

**When lifestyle is not enough:**

- Targeted supplements (guided by testing)  
- Specific dietary changes (guided by your results)  
- Clinical treatments from your provider (medicines when needed)  
- Work with a functional medicine provider for a personalized plan

**Important reminder:** Lab values are not isolated numbers. They are connected pieces of a larger health picture. Always look at your results in the context of how you feel, your history, your physical exam, and other markers. The goal is not just to "normalize" lab values but to help your body work at its best, prevent disease, and live a long, healthy life.

### Fibrinogen Activity

**What is this test?** Fibrinogen is a protein made by your liver. It is essential for blood clotting. It turns into fibrin during the clotting cascade (the series of steps your body uses to form a clot). Fibrinogen is also an acute phase reactant, meaning it rises with inflammation.

**Why does it matter?** High fibrinogen is an independent risk factor for heart disease, stroke, and blood clots. It tells you about clotting risk and inflammation at the same time.

**Reference ranges (mg/dL, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for Fibrinogen Activity's individual levels. This is a common functional medicine marker but may be reported with general reference ranges by individual labs. In general: Lab range 200–400 mg/dL, Functional range 200–300 mg/dL. For this v3 guide, please refer to the standard ranges below:

- Clinical Low: below 150 mg/dL  
- Functional Low: 150–199 mg/dL  
- **Optimal: 200–300 mg/dL**  
- Functional High: 301–400 mg/dL  
- Clinical High: above 400 mg/dL

**Clinical Low (below 150 mg/dL):** This is called hypofibrinogenemia. It raises your bleeding risk. Causes: advanced liver disease (low production), DIC (disseminated intravascular coagulation — a serious condition where small blood clots form throughout the body, using up clotting factors), inherited fibrinogen disorders, and certain medicines (clot-busting drugs, snake venom). To address: work with a provider urgently. Treatment depends on the cause — fibrinogen concentrate, fresh frozen plasma, cryoprecipitate (a blood product rich in clotting factors), or treatment of DIC. Treat liver disease.

**Functional Low (150–199 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can mean mild liver dysfunction, mild consumption (DIC), or inherited hypofibrinogenemia. You may have a slight bleeding tendency and reduced acute phase response. To optimize: check liver function. Treat any underlying condition. Periodic monitoring is enough.

**Optimal (200–300 mg/dL):** This is the goal. It means balanced clotting, healthy liver production, and low chronic inflammation. To stay here: keep up a colorful plant-based diet rich in antioxidants, fiber, and omega-3 fats. Exercise most days (both cardio and strength training). Avoid smoking, limit alcohol, and manage stress. Periodic re-checking is enough.

**Functional High (301–400 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It is an independent risk factor for heart disease, stroke, and blood clots. Common causes: chronic inflammation, smoking, obesity, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, sedentary lifestyle, estrogen therapy, and acute infection. You have a higher risk of clots and heart problems. To optimize: address inflammation and heart risk factors. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet (vegetables, berries, omega-3 fats, fiber). Exercise regularly. Lose extra weight. Quit smoking. Manage stress. A provider can check hs-CRP, fasting insulin, lipids, and heart risk.

**Clinical High (above 400 mg/dL):** A high fibrinogen is linked to much higher risk of heart disease, stroke, and blood clots. Causes: chronic inflammation, smoking, obesity, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, sedentary lifestyle, estrogen therapy, acute infection, cancer, and certain genetic disorders. To address: work with a provider. Address underlying inflammation, heart risk factors, and modifiable risks. Lifestyle changes — anti-inflammatory diet, regular exercise, weight loss, smoking cessation, alcohol moderation — are foundational. Natural anti-inflammatory agents (omega-3s, curcumin, ginger) and possibly medicines (aspirin, statins) may be considered.
