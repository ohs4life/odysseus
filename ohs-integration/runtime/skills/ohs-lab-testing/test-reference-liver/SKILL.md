# Liver and Protein Markers — Test Reference

## When to Use
When a customer, employee, or practitioner asks for the meaning, optimal range, or interpretation of any lab test in this group. **Reference only — never substitute for a provider's interpretation.** See `ohs-compliance/disclaimers` (the "share with your provider" framing) for the required patient-facing language.

## Procedure
1. Identify the test the user is asking about.
2. Quote the test name exactly as it appears on the lab report (with the OHS reference range applied).
3. Read out the five-level range (Clinical Low / Functional Low / Optimal / Functional High / Clinical High) and the units.
4. Summarize the "What is this test?" and "Why does it matter?" sections in plain language.
5. For any out-of-range result, recommend the customer share the result with their healthcare provider.

## Pitfalls
- **Do not interpret any test result for a customer.** The lab description here is **reference** content. The customer's provider is the one who interprets.
- **Do not recommend a specific dose of a supplement** based on a test result. The right phrasing is "this marker is associated with [nutrient / lifestyle factors] — your provider can advise on whether to add a supplement, and at what dose."
- **Do not rephrase the FDA structure-function disclaimer away.** If the answer includes any "supports / is associated with" language about a product, also include the disclaimer (see `ohs-compliance/disclaimers`).
- **Do not imply a single test is sufficient.** Most health questions need a pattern of markers, not a single value, to be meaningful.
- **Do not state ranges with more precision than the source.** The five levels in this skill come from `knowledgebase/labs/ohs_lab_test_descriptions_v3.md` (OHS-authored). Do not invent a tighter or different range.
- **Range sex specificity (M / F / B):** respect the M / F / B annotation in each test.

## Anything else

### The five-level range system (every test uses this)
- **Clinical Low** — below the lab range. Often means a real health problem.
- **Functional Low** — in the lab range but on the low end. Not sick, but not at your best.
- **Optimal** — the level where your body works its best. The goal.
- **Functional High** — in the lab range but on the high end. Body is under stress.
- **Clinical High** — above the lab range. Often means a real health problem.

### Sex-specific ranges
**B** = both sexes · **M** = males only · **F** = females only.

## Test Descriptions

### Protein, Total

**What is this test?** Total protein measures all the protein in your blood. There are two main kinds: albumin (made by your liver) and globulin (made by your liver and immune system). Proteins do many jobs — they carry hormones, fight infection, and keep fluid in your blood vessels.

**Why does it matter?** It gives you a quick look at your nutrition, liver function, immune function, and kidney health. Low protein can mean malnutrition, malabsorption, or liver or kidney problems. High protein can mean chronic inflammation, infection, or a blood disorder.

**Reference ranges (g/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–6.0  
- Functional Low: 6.01–6.49  
- **Optimal: 6.5–7.5**  
- Functional High: 7.51–8.0  
- Clinical High: 8.01 and above

**Clinical Low (below 6.0 g/dL):** This is rare. It can mean malnutrition, malabsorption (celiac, IBD, SIBO), advanced liver disease, kidney disease (losing protein in urine), severe burns, chronic infections, cancer, or rare genetic disorders. You may have swelling, muscle loss, fatigue, and slow healing. To raise it: work with a provider. Eat 0.8–1.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight (more if you are active or older). Good sources: beans, fish, eggs, poultry, Greek yogurt, bone broth. Address the underlying cause (gut, liver, kidney).

**Functional Low (6.01–6.49 g/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means you are not eating enough protein, your gut is not absorbing food well, or you have chronic inflammation. You may feel tired, recover slowly, or get sick often. To raise it: add more protein to your meals (beans, fish, eggs, poultry, Greek yogurt, nuts, seeds). Make sure you eat enough calories. A provider can check your gut, liver, and inflammation.

**Optimal (6.5–7.5 g/dL):** This is the goal. It means your body is making enough protein, your liver is working well, and you have a balanced immune system. To stay here: eat 0.8–1.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight spread across meals. Eat a colorful plant-based diet. Periodically re-check, especially with major diet changes or illness.

**Functional High (7.51–8.0 g/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It is often from high globulins (immune proteins), which can mean chronic inflammation, chronic infection, or a high-protein diet. Common causes: long-term infections (viral, dental, gut), autoimmune disease, chronic inflammation, and a high-protein or high-dairy diet. To optimize: address any hidden infections or inflammation. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet (vegetables, berries, omega-3 fats). Cut back on alcohol. Manage stress. A provider can check your inflammation, gut, and immune markers.

**Clinical High (8.01 g/dL and above):** This is uncommon and needs evaluation. Causes: chronic inflammation, chronic infections, autoimmune disease (lupus, RA, sarcoidosis), multiple myeloma, Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia, dehydration, or rarely liver disease. To address: work with a provider. They may order a protein electrophoresis (SPEP) and other tests. Treat the cause. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet.

### Albumin

**What is this test?** Albumin is the most common protein in your blood. Your liver makes it. Albumin carries hormones, vitamins, and medicines around your body. It also keeps fluid in your blood vessels and acts as an antioxidant.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you about your liver function, nutrition, and inflammation. Low albumin is a strong warning sign for serious illness and is linked to higher death rates in hospitalized patients. Higher albumin within the normal range is linked to better health and longer life.

**Reference ranges (g/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–3.5  
- Functional Low: 3.6–3.9  
- **Optimal: 4.0–4.9**  
- Functional High: 5.0–5.1  
- Clinical High: 5.2 and above

**Clinical Low (below 3.5 g/dL):** This is called hypoalbuminemia. It is a warning sign for serious illness, longer hospital stays, and worse outcomes from surgery. Causes: advanced liver disease (low production), kidney disease (losing albumin in urine), severe malnutrition, malabsorption, chronic inflammation, severe burns, and rare genetic conditions. You may have swelling, fatigue, and poor healing. To optimize: work with a provider to treat the cause. Eat 1.2–1.5 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight. Focus on easy-to-digest proteins. Support your liver and gut.

**Functional Low (3.6–3.9 g/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means you are not eating enough protein, your gut is not absorbing food well, your liver is sluggish, or you have chronic inflammation. You may feel tired and recover slowly. To raise it: eat more protein (beans, fish, eggs, poultry, Greek yogurt, nuts, seeds). Make sure you eat enough calories. A provider can check your gut, liver, and inflammation.

**Optimal (4.0–4.9 g/dL):** This is the goal. It means your liver is working well, your nutrition is good, and you have low inflammation. Higher albumin in this range is linked to better health and longer life. To stay here: eat 0.8–1.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight. Eat a colorful plant-based diet with anti-inflammatory foods. Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (5.0–5.1 g/dL):** This is uncommon and usually harmless. It often comes from a very high protein intake or mild dehydration. It is usually a good sign. No changes needed. Make sure you drink enough water.

**Clinical High (5.2 g/dL and above):** This is rare. It is usually from severe dehydration or, less often, a very high protein intake. To address: drink more water. If you eat a lot of protein, make sure your kidneys are working well. Most often, no changes are needed.

### Globulin

**What is this test?** Globulins are a group of proteins made by your liver and immune system. They include antibodies (which fight infection), complement proteins, and transport proteins.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you about your immune function, inflammation, and liver health. Low globulins can mean a weak immune system. High globulins can mean chronic inflammation, infection, or autoimmune disease.

**Reference ranges (g/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–1.8  
- Functional Low: 1.81–2.0  
- **Optimal: 2.01–3.2**  
- Functional High: 3.21–3.7  
- Clinical High: 3.71 and above

**Clinical Low (below 1.8 g/dL):** This is rare. It can mean immune deficiency, advanced liver disease (low production), kidney disease (losing globulins in urine), severe malnutrition, or rare genetic disorders (agammaglobulinemia). You may get sick often. To optimize: work with a provider to find the cause. Eat enough protein. Support your liver and gut. A provider can check your immune function, gut, and liver.

**Functional Low (1.81–2.0 g/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can mean mild immune suppression, low protein intake, or sluggish liver function. You may get sick more easily. To raise it: eat enough protein. Support your gut (where 70–80% of your immune system lives). Add immune-supporting foods: zinc, vitamin D, vitamin C, glutathione (from broccoli sprouts, garlic, onions). A provider can check your gut, liver, and immune markers.

**Optimal (2.01–3.2 g/dL):** This is the goal. It means a balanced immune system — strong but not over-active. To stay here: eat a colorful plant-based diet rich in antioxidants, fiber, and immune-supporting nutrients (zinc, vitamin D, vitamin C). Sleep 7–9 hours. Manage stress. Exercise most days. Maintain a healthy gut.

**Functional High (3.21–3.7 g/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It often means your immune system is activated. Common causes: chronic inflammation, chronic infections (viral, dental, gut, sinus), autoimmune disease, and a high-protein or high-dairy diet. You may feel tired, have achy joints, brain fog, or get sick often. To optimize: address any hidden infections or inflammation. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet. Cut back on alcohol. Support your gut. Manage stress. A provider can check your inflammation, gut, and immune markers.

**Clinical High (3.71 g/dL and above):** This needs evaluation. Causes: chronic inflammation, chronic infections (HIV, hepatitis, TB, EBV), autoimmune disease (lupus, RA, sarcoidosis), liver disease, multiple myeloma, Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia, and other paraproteinemias. High globulins with symptoms (fatigue, weight loss, night sweats, bone pain) need urgent care. To address: work with a provider. They may order a protein electrophoresis (SPEP) and other tests. Treat the cause.

### Albumin/Globulin Ratio (A/G Ratio)

**What is this test?** The A/G ratio is your albumin divided by your globulin. It shows the balance between your two main protein groups. A higher ratio usually means better health.

**Why does it matter?** It is a simple way to see if your body favors health (high albumin) or inflammation (high globulin). A low ratio is a sign of chronic inflammation or infection. A high ratio is usually a sign of good health.

**Reference ranges (ratio, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–1.0  
- Functional Low: 1.1–1.4  
- **Optimal: 1.41–2.0**  
- Functional High: 2.01–2.5  
- Clinical High: 2.51 and above

**Clinical Low (below 1.0):** This is a serious warning sign. It usually means chronic inflammation, infection, autoimmune disease, liver problems, or a blood cancer. Causes: chronic viral infections (HIV, hepatitis, EBV), autoimmune disease (lupus, RA), multiple myeloma, liver cirrhosis, kidney disease, and chronic inflammation. You may feel tired, have joint pain, and be at higher risk of serious illness. To optimize: work with a provider to find the cause. Treat any infection, autoimmune disease, or cancer. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet.

**Functional Low (1.1–1.4):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means chronic low-grade inflammation, hidden infections (dental, gut, sinus), or mild liver dysfunction. You may feel tired, have brain fog, joint stiffness, or get sick often. To optimize: address any hidden infections or inflammation. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet. Support your gut and liver. Manage stress. A provider can check your inflammation, gut, liver, and immune markers.

**Optimal (1.41–2.0):** This is the goal. It means balanced proteins, low inflammation, healthy liver, and a well-regulated immune system. To stay here: keep up a whole-food, plant-based diet rich in antioxidants, fiber, and lean protein. Sleep 7–9 hours. Manage stress. Exercise most days. Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (2.01–2.5):** This is usually a great sign. It usually means high albumin (good nutrition and liver function) and low-normal globulins. It is a sign of robust health. If it is very high (\>2.5), it can sometimes mean low globulins (immune deficiency). If you get sick often, a provider can check your immune markers.

**Clinical High (2.51 and above):** This is rare. It can mean very low globulins (immune deficiency) or very high albumin. Causes: hypogammaglobulinemia, multiple myeloma (rare, as globulins are usually high), or genetic immune deficiencies. To address: work with a provider to check your immune markers and consider further tests.

### Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP)

**What is this test?** ALP is an enzyme found mostly in your liver, bones, intestines, and kidneys. It helps with bone growth and bile flow. Bile is a fluid your liver makes to help digest fat.

**Why does it matter?** High ALP can mean liver or bone disease. Low ALP can mean malnutrition, an underactive thyroid, or a rare genetic condition. ALP is naturally higher in kids and teens (during growth spurts) and during pregnancy.

**Reference ranges (U/L, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–34  
- Functional Low: 35–69  
- **Optimal: 70–115**  
- Functional High: 116–144  
- Clinical High: 145 and above

**Clinical Low (below 35 U/L):** This is on the low end. It can mean an underactive thyroid, severe malnutrition, celiac disease, pernicious anemia, low B12 or folate, low zinc, or a rare genetic disorder (hypophosphatasia). You may feel tired, have joint stiffness, and weak bones. To optimize: work with a provider. Make sure you get enough protein, B12, folate, zinc, and magnesium. Support your thyroid (iodine, selenium, stress control).

**Functional Low (35–69 U/L):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can mean a slightly underactive thyroid, mild malnutrition, malabsorption, or low bone turnover. You may feel tired, have joint stiffness, or have weaker bones. To optimize: make sure you get enough protein, B12, folate, zinc, magnesium, and vitamin D. Do weight-bearing exercise for bone health. A provider can check your thyroid, gut, and nutrients.

**Optimal (70–115 U/L):** This is the goal. It means your liver and bones are working well. To stay here: eat a whole-food diet with protein, calcium, magnesium, vitamin D, and zinc. Do weight-bearing exercise. Limit alcohol. Avoid toxins that hurt the liver. Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (116–144 U/L):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean mild liver stress, increased bone turnover, or healing. Common causes: fatty liver disease, mild bile flow problems, healing bone fracture, intense exercise, certain medicines, low vitamin D (which can cause high parathyroid hormone), or rarely Paget disease (a bone disorder). To optimize: address the cause. Cut back on alcohol, processed foods, and sugar. Eat liver-friendly foods (leafy greens, beets, cruciferous vegetables, coffee). A provider can check your liver and bones.

**Clinical High (145 U/L and above):** This needs evaluation. Causes: bile duct problems (blockage, primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis), hepatitis, cirrhosis, bone disorders (Paget disease, osteomalacia, healing fractures, bone tumors), certain cancers (liver, bone, spread from other cancers), hyperparathyroidism, and certain medicines. To address: work with a provider. They will order liver imaging, GGT, 5'-nucleotidase, and possibly bone-specific ALP. Treat the cause. Cut out alcohol.

### AST (Aspartate Aminotransferase)

**What is this test?** AST is an enzyme (a protein that speeds up chemical reactions) found in your liver, heart, muscles, kidneys, brain, and red blood cells. It leaks into your blood when these tissues are damaged.

**Why does it matter?** It is one of the main markers for liver injury, but it can also be high with muscle injury or heart attack. ALT is a more liver-specific marker, so AST is usually looked at with ALT.

**Reference ranges (U/L, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–9  
- Functional Low: 10–12  
- **Optimal: 13–24**  
- Functional High: 25–35  
- Clinical High: 36 and above

**Clinical Low (below 10 U/L):** This is on the low end. It can mean low B6 (pyridoxine), because AST needs B6 to work. It can also happen in advanced liver disease with reduced liver function, and in kidney failure. You may feel tired and have other B6 deficiency symptoms (tingling in hands and feet, anemia, mood changes). To optimize: make sure you get enough B6 (chickpeas, tuna, salmon, chicken, bananas, potatoes, sunflower seeds). A provider can check B6, liver, and kidney.

**Functional Low (10–12 U/L):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means subclinical B6 deficiency, which is very common. Symptoms can be subtle fatigue, mood changes, anemia, and tingling in hands and feet. To optimize: eat B6-rich foods (chickpeas, tuna, salmon, chicken, bananas, potatoes, sunflower seeds). Consider B6 supplements (10–50 mg a day of P-5-P, the active form). A provider can check B6.

**Optimal (13–24 U/L):** This is the goal. It means your liver and muscles are healthy, you have enough B6, and there is little tissue damage. To stay here: keep up a liver-friendly, plant-centered diet (vegetables, fruits, leafy greens, beets, cruciferous vegetables). Limit alcohol. Avoid liver-toxic substances. Exercise most days. Periodically re-check, especially with new medicines or symptoms.

**Functional High (25–35 U/L):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It often means low-grade liver stress, mild fatty liver, muscle injury (recent hard exercise), alcohol use, certain medicines (statins for cholesterol, acetaminophen/Tylenol), or metabolic dysfunction. You may feel tired, foggy, or have right upper belly discomfort. To optimize: find and address the cause. Cut back on alcohol, refined sugar, and processed foods. Eat a liver-friendly, plant-centered diet. Exercise regularly. A provider can check liver, fasting insulin, and muscles.

**Clinical High (36 U/L and above):** High AST means liver, muscle, or other tissue injury. Causes: liver disease (hepatitis, fatty liver, cirrhosis, drug-induced liver injury, liver cancer), muscle injury (rhabdomyolysis, myositis, recent hard exercise, statins), heart attack, hemolysis, and certain genetic conditions. AST higher than ALT suggests alcoholic liver disease or muscle injury. ALT higher than AST suggests non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). To address: work with a provider. Find and treat the cause. Cut back on alcohol, processed foods, and liver-toxic medicines. Eat a liver-friendly, plant-centered diet and exercise.

### ALT (Alanine Aminotransferase)

**What is this test?** ALT is an enzyme found mostly in your liver. It is the most specific and sensitive marker for liver injury.

**Why does it matter?** It is the main marker for liver damage. High ALT is one of the most common abnormal lab findings and often signals fatty liver disease. It is usually looked at with AST.

**Reference ranges (U/L, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–5  
- Functional Low: 6–9  
- **Optimal: 10–23**  
- Functional High: 24–27  
- Clinical High: 28 and above

**Clinical Low (below 6 U/L):** This is on the low end. It can mean low B6 (pyridoxine), because ALT needs B6 to work. It can also happen in advanced liver disease with reduced liver function. To optimize: make sure you get enough B6 (chickpeas, tuna, salmon, chicken, bananas, potatoes, sunflower seeds). A provider can check B6 and liver.

**Functional Low (6–9 U/L):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means subclinical B6 deficiency, which is very common. To optimize: eat B6-rich foods. Consider B6 supplements (10–50 mg a day of P-5-P). A provider can check B6.

**Optimal (10–23 U/L):** This is the goal. It means your liver is healthy, your metabolic function is good, and there is little cell turnover. To stay here: keep up a liver-friendly, plant-centered diet (vegetables, fruits, leafy greens, beets, cruciferous vegetables, coffee). Limit alcohol. Avoid liver-toxic substances. Exercise most days. Maintain a healthy weight (belly fat drives fatty liver). Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (24–27 U/L):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It is the most sensitive early marker for non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), which affects up to 30% of adults. Causes: insulin resistance, obesity, metabolic syndrome, alcohol, certain medicines, viral hepatitis, and autoimmune hepatitis. You may feel tired, foggy, or have right upper belly discomfort. To optimize: find and address the cause. Cut back on refined sugar, processed foods, and alcohol. Eat a liver-friendly, plant-centered diet. Exercise regularly. Lose extra body fat. A provider can check fasting insulin, HOMA-IR (a calculation that estimates insulin resistance), liver imaging, and viral hepatitis.

**Clinical High (28 U/L and above):** High ALT means significant liver injury. Causes: NAFLD and NASH (non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, a more serious form of fatty liver — most common), alcoholic liver disease, viral hepatitis (B, C), drug-induced liver injury (acetaminophen, statins, certain herbs), autoimmune hepatitis, hemochromatosis, Wilson disease (a genetic disorder where copper builds up in the liver), alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (a genetic disorder that can cause liver and lung disease), and liver cancer. To address: work with a provider. Full workup usually includes viral hepatitis panel, autoimmune markers, iron studies, liver imaging, and possibly biopsy. Treatment depends on the cause. Lifestyle changes (diet, exercise, weight loss, alcohol cessation) are foundational. For NAFLD/NASH, losing 7–10% of your body weight can reverse the disease.

### Bilirubin (Direct / Conjugated)

**What is this test?** Bilirubin is a yellow pigment made when your body breaks down old red blood cells. Your liver processes bilirubin, attaches (conjugates) it to a sugar to make it water-soluble, and sends it out in bile (a fluid your liver makes to help digest fat). Direct (conjugated) bilirubin is the water-soluble form that has been processed by your liver.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you how well your liver and bile ducts are working. High direct bilirubin often means liver or bile duct disease.

**Reference ranges (mg/dL, both sexes):** The OHS range file does not have specific numeric values for this test's levels. This test is reported qualitatively as negative or positive. A negative result is the goal. A positive result (any detectable direct bilirubin in the blood) is a Clinical High that needs evaluation.

**Optimal (Negative):** A negative direct bilirubin is the goal. It means your liver is processing bilirubin well and your antioxidant capacity is good. To stay here: keep up a liver-friendly, plant-centered diet. Limit alcohol. Exercise most days. Periodically re-check.

**Clinical High (Positive):** A positive direct bilirubin means significant liver or bile duct disease. Causes: cholestasis (bile duct blockage, primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis), hepatitis (viral, alcoholic, autoimmune, drug-induced), cirrhosis, and inherited disorders (Dubin-Johnson, Rotor syndromes). To address: work with a provider. They will order imaging (ultrasound, MRCP — a special MRI that looks at bile ducts) and a full liver workup. Treat the cause. Limit alcohol and liver-toxic substances.

### Bilirubin, Total

**What is this test?** Total bilirubin is your direct (conjugated) plus indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin. Indirect bilirubin is the fat-soluble form that has not been processed by your liver yet.

**Why does it matter?** It is a useful marker of liver function, hemolysis, and bile duct health. Mildly high bilirubin is often benign (Gilbert syndrome), but it can also signal liver disease or hemolysis.

**Reference ranges (mg/dL, both sexes):**

- **Optimal: 0–1.5**  
- Functional High: 1.6–2.5  
- Clinical High: 2.6 and above

Note: This test does not have a Clinical Low or Functional Low category in the OHS ranges.

**Optimal (0–1.5 mg/dL):** This is the goal. It means your liver is working well, you have normal red blood cell turnover, and good antioxidant capacity. To stay here: keep up a liver-friendly, plant-centered diet. Limit alcohol. Exercise most days. Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (1.6–2.5 mg/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean Gilbert syndrome (a common, harmless genetic condition affecting 5–10% of people, with mildly high unconjugated bilirubin that rises with stress, fasting, or illness), mild hemolysis, or early liver disease. Gilbert syndrome is benign and does not need treatment. To optimize: support your liver with a plant-centered diet. Limit alcohol. Drink plenty of water. A provider can check liver, gallbladder, and hemolysis markers.

**Clinical High (2.6 mg/dL and above):** High total bilirubin means liver, bile duct, or hemolytic disease. Causes: hemolytic anemia, viral hepatitis, alcoholic liver disease, drug-induced liver injury, cirrhosis, cholestasis, primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, and inherited disorders (Gilbert, Dubin-Johnson, Rotor — usually benign with mild elevation). To address: work with a provider. Full workup includes liver enzymes, viral hepatitis panel, autoimmune markers, hemolysis markers, and imaging. Treat the cause. Limit alcohol and liver-toxic substances.

### Gamma-Glutamyl Transerase (GGT)

**What is this test?** GGT is an enzyme found mostly in your liver, bile ducts, kidneys, pancreas, and spleen. It is the most sensitive marker for bile duct problems (cholestasis, blockage). It also goes up with alcohol use, fatty liver, and certain medicines.

**Why does it matter?** It is a key marker in functional medicine because it is sensitive to oxidative stress, environmental toxins, and metabolic problems. It is also a strong predictor of heart disease and death.

**Reference ranges (U/L, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–8  
- Functional Low: 9–14  
- **Optimal: 15–30**  
- Functional High: 31–48  
- Clinical High: above 48

**Clinical Low (below 9 U/L):** Low GGT is rare. It can mean underactive thyroid, low magnesium, or low zinc. It can also happen in advanced liver disease with reduced liver function. You may feel tired and have other deficiency symptoms. To optimize: make sure you get enough magnesium, zinc, and thyroid support. A provider can check thyroid, magnesium, and zinc.

**Functional Low (9–14 U/L):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can mean slightly underactive thyroid, mild low magnesium, or mild low zinc. You may have subtle fatigue and reduced metabolic capacity. To optimize: make sure you get enough magnesium, zinc, and thyroid support. Periodic monitoring is enough.

**Optimal (15–30 U/L):** This is the goal. It means your liver, bile ducts, and pancreas are working well, with low oxidative stress and no significant toxic burden. To stay here: keep up a liver-friendly, plant-centered diet (vegetables, fruits, leafy greens, beets, cruciferous vegetables, coffee). Limit alcohol. Avoid liver-toxic substances. Exercise most days. Maintain a healthy weight. Periodic re-checking is enough.

**Functional High (31–48 U/L):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It is the most sensitive early marker for fatty liver disease, oxidative stress, and toxic burden. Common causes: non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), alcohol use, smoking, obesity, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, certain medicines (anticonvulsants, statins, certain antibiotics), and environmental toxin exposure. You may feel tired, foggy, or have right upper belly discomfort. To optimize: find and address the cause. Cut back on alcohol, processed foods, and refined sugar. Lose extra body fat. Eat a liver-friendly, plant-centered diet. Exercise regularly. A provider can check liver, fasting insulin, and toxic burden.

**Clinical High (above 48 U/L):** A high GGT means significant liver or bile duct stress. Causes: non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (most common), alcoholic liver disease, cholestasis (bile duct blockage, primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis), hepatitis (viral, drug-induced, autoimmune), cirrhosis, liver cancer, pancreatitis, and certain medicines. To address: work with a provider. They will order a full liver workup (liver enzymes, viral hepatitis panel, autoimmune markers, iron studies, liver imaging, and possibly biopsy). Treatment depends on the cause. Lifestyle changes — alcohol cessation, weight loss, anti-inflammatory diet, exercise — are foundational for fatty liver disease.
