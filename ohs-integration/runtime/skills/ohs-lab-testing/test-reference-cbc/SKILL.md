# Complete Blood Count (CBC) — Cell Counts and Indices — Test Reference

## When to Use
When a customer, employee, or practitioner asks for the meaning, optimal range, or interpretation of any lab test in this group. **Reference only — never substitute for a provider's interpretation.** See `ohs-compliance/disclaimers` (the "share with your provider" framing) for the required patient-facing language.

## Procedure
1. Identify the test the user is asking about.
2. Quote the test name exactly as it appears on the lab report (with the OHS reference range applied).
3. Read out the five-level range (Clinical Low / Functional Low / Optimal / Functional High / Clinical High) and the units.
4. Summarize the "What is this test?" and "Why does it matter?" sections in plain language.
5. For any out-of-range result, recommend the customer share the result with their healthcare provider.

## Pitfalls
- **Do not interpret any test result for a customer.** The lab description here is **reference** content. The customer's provider is the one who interprets.
- **Do not recommend a specific dose of a supplement** based on a test result. The right phrasing is "this marker is associated with [nutrient / lifestyle factors] — your provider can advise on whether to add a supplement, and at what dose."
- **Do not rephrase the FDA structure-function disclaimer away.** If the answer includes any "supports / is associated with" language about a product, also include the disclaimer (see `ohs-compliance/disclaimers`).
- **Do not imply a single test is sufficient.** Most health questions need a pattern of markers, not a single value, to be meaningful.
- **Do not state ranges with more precision than the source.** The five levels in this skill come from `knowledgebase/labs/ohs_lab_test_descriptions_v3.md` (OHS-authored). Do not invent a tighter or different range.
- **Range sex specificity (M / F / B):** respect the M / F / B annotation in each test.

## Anything else

### The five-level range system (every test uses this)
- **Clinical Low** — below the lab range. Often means a real health problem.
- **Functional Low** — in the lab range but on the low end. Not sick, but not at your best.
- **Optimal** — the level where your body works its best. The goal.
- **Functional High** — in the lab range but on the high end. Body is under stress.
- **Clinical High** — above the lab range. Often means a real health problem.

### Sex-specific ranges
**B** = both sexes · **M** = males only · **F** = females only.

## Test Descriptions

### Hemoglobin

**What is this test?** Hemoglobin is the protein in your red blood cells that carries oxygen from your lungs to the rest of your body. It contains iron, which is what makes your blood red.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you how well your blood can carry oxygen. Low hemoglobin (anemia) means your tissues are not getting enough oxygen. You feel tired, weak, short of breath, and foggy. High hemoglobin can be from dehydration, training at altitude, or rare blood disorders.

**Reference ranges (g/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–12.9  
- Functional Low: 13.0–14.5  
- **Optimal: 14.6–16.6**  
- Functional High: 16.7–17.1  
- Clinical High: 17.2 and above

**Clinical Low (below 13.0 g/dL):** This is called anemia. Your body is not getting enough oxygen. You feel tired, weak, short of breath, foggy, and look pale. Causes: iron deficiency (most common), low B12, low folate, chronic blood loss (gut, menstrual), kidney disease, chronic inflammation, bone marrow problems, and underactive thyroid. To address: work with a provider. Treatment depends on the cause — iron (iron bisglycinate or ferrous sulfate), B12 (especially for older adults and vegans), folate, or treatment of the underlying condition. Eat iron-rich foods (grass-fed red meat, organ meats, dark leafy greens, lentils, fortified cereals) with vitamin C for better absorption.

**Functional Low (13.0–14.5 g/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early iron deficiency, low B12 or folate, chronic inflammation, or a slightly underactive thyroid. You may feel tired, have brain fog, hair loss, or trouble exercising. To raise it: find and treat the cause. Eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C. Make sure you get enough B12 (animal products or supplements for vegans) and folate (leafy greens, beans, avocado). A provider can check ferritin, B12, folate, thyroid, and gut health.

**Optimal (14.6–16.6 g/dL):** This is the goal. It means your blood is carrying oxygen well. To stay here: keep up a nutrient-dense diet with iron, B12, folate, and vitamin C. Stay hydrated. Periodically re-check, especially for women with heavy periods.

**Functional High (16.7–17.1 g/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can be from dehydration, living at high altitude, intense endurance training, or too much iron. Very high hemoglobin can make your blood thick and raise the risk of clots. To optimize: drink more water. Cut back on iron supplements if you take them. If you are very high and not athletic or dehydrated, a provider can check for sleep apnea or rare blood disorders.

**Clinical High (17.2 g/dL and above):** Very high hemoglobin needs evaluation. Causes: dehydration, polycythemia vera (a blood cancer where the body makes too many red blood cells), secondary polycythemia (from smoking, COPD, sleep apnea, living at altitude, congenital heart disease), testosterone therapy, or rare tumors that make erythropoietin (a hormone that tells your body to make red blood cells). To address: work with a provider. Stop smoking. Treat sleep apnea. Adjust testosterone therapy if applicable. Phlebotomy (blood removal) may be needed for polycythemia vera. Drink more water.

### Hematocrit

**What is this test?** Hematocrit is the percentage of your blood that is made of red blood cells. The rest is plasma (the liquid part of blood). It is closely tied to hemoglobin.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you the same thing as hemoglobin — how well your blood carries oxygen. Low hematocrit means anemia. High hematocrit means thick blood, which can raise the risk of clots.

**Reference ranges (%, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–35.0  
- Functional Low: 35.01–37.4  
- **Optimal: 37.41–44.0**  
- Functional High: 44.01–44.99  
- Clinical High: 45.0 and above

**Clinical Low (below 35.0%):** This is anemia. It means your blood cannot carry enough oxygen. You feel tired, weak, short of breath, and foggy. Causes: iron deficiency, low B12, low folate, chronic blood loss, kidney disease, chronic inflammation, bone marrow problems, and underactive thyroid. To address: work with a provider. Treatment depends on the cause. Eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C.

**Functional Low (35.01–37.4%):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early iron deficiency, low B12 or folate, chronic inflammation, or mild anemia. You may feel tired or have trouble exercising. To raise it: find and treat the cause. Eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C. Make sure you get enough B12 and folate. A provider can check ferritin, B12, folate, thyroid, and gut health.

**Optimal (37.41–44.0%):** This is the goal. It means your blood is carrying oxygen well and your bone marrow is working properly. To stay here: keep up a nutrient-dense diet with iron, B12, folate, and vitamin C. Stay hydrated. Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (44.01–44.99%):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can be from dehydration, endurance training, high altitude, or too much iron. Very high hematocrit can make blood thick and raise clot risk. To optimize: drink more water. Cut back on iron supplements if needed. If endurance-trained, this is often a normal adaptation.

**Clinical High (45.0% and above):** Very high hematocrit needs evaluation. Causes: polycythemia vera, secondary polycythemia (smoking, COPD, sleep apnea, altitude, congenital heart disease), dehydration, and testosterone therapy. Symptoms: headache, dizziness, blurry vision, and higher clot risk. To address: work with a provider. Stop smoking. Treat sleep apnea. Adjust testosterone therapy. Phlebotomy may be needed. Drink more water.

### White Blood Cell Count (WBC)

**What is this test?** WBC is the total number of white blood cells in your blood. White blood cells are your immune system's soldiers. They fight infection, clean up damaged cells, and remember past invaders. There are 5 main types: neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, and basophils.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you how active your immune system is. Low WBC means your immune system may be weak. High WBC can mean infection, inflammation, or stress. Higher WBC within the normal range is linked to more inflammation and higher heart risk.

**Reference ranges (K/μL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–3.3  
- Functional Low: 3.4–4.4  
- **Optimal: 4.5–8.5**  
- Functional High: 8.6–10.8  
- Clinical High: 10.9 and above

**Clinical Low (below 3.4 K/μL):** This is called leukopenia. Your immune system is weak. You get sick easily. Causes: viral infections, autoimmune disease (lupus, RA), bone marrow disorders, certain medicines (chemotherapy, clozapine, methimazole), severe infections (sepsis, HIV), low B12, folate, copper, or zinc, and an overactive spleen. To address: work with a provider. Support your immune system with a nutrient-dense diet, sleep, stress control, and key nutrients (zinc, vitamin D, vitamin C, B12, folate).

**Functional Low (3.4–4.4 K/μL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can be from chronic viral infections (EBV, CMV, COVID), chronic stress, low nutrients, or mild bone marrow suppression. You may get sick more easily, heal slowly, or feel tired. To optimize: address any chronic viral infections. Support your immune system with a nutrient-dense diet, sleep, and stress control. Make sure you get enough zinc, vitamin D, vitamin C, B12, and folate. A provider can check viral load, gut, and nutrients.

**Optimal (4.5–8.5 K/μL):** This is the goal. It means a balanced immune system with low chronic inflammation and good resilience. To stay here: keep up a colorful plant-based diet rich in antioxidants, exercise, sleep 7–9 hours, manage stress, avoid smoking, limit alcohol, and maintain a healthy gut.

**Functional High (8.6–10.8 K/μL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It often means low-grade chronic inflammation, hidden infection, smoking, belly fat, stress, intense exercise, or chronic allergen exposure. Higher WBC in this range is linked to higher heart risk. To optimize: address any inflammation or infection. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet (vegetables, berries, omega-3 fats). Quit smoking. Lose extra belly fat. Exercise moderately. A provider can check inflammation, gut, teeth, and sinuses.

**Clinical High (10.9 K/μL and above):** This means significant immune activation. Causes: bacterial or viral infection, inflammation (autoimmune disease, IBD, vasculitis — inflammation of blood vessels), tissue damage (surgery, trauma, burns), stress response, smoking, certain medicines (steroids, lithium, beta-agonists — medicines that open the airways), and blood cancers (leukemia, lymphoma). To address: work with a provider. They may do a differential, peripheral smear (looking at blood cells under a microscope), and possibly bone marrow biopsy. Treat the cause.

### Red Blood Cell Count (RBC)

**What is this test?** RBC is the number of red blood cells you have per volume of blood. It is closely tied to hemoglobin and hematocrit.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you how well your blood can carry oxygen. Low RBC is a form of anemia. High RBC can be from dehydration, training, or rare blood disorders.

**Reference ranges (M/μL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–3.76  
- Functional Low: 3.77–4.09  
- **Optimal: 4.1–5.1**  
- Functional High: 5.11–5.28  
- Clinical High: 5.29 and above

**Clinical Low (below 3.77 M/μL):** This is anemia. Your body is not getting enough oxygen. Causes: iron deficiency, low B12, low folate, chronic blood loss, kidney disease, chronic inflammation, bone marrow disorders, and underactive thyroid. To address: work with a provider. Treatment depends on the cause. Eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C.

**Functional Low (3.77–4.09 M/μL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early iron deficiency, low B12 or folate, chronic inflammation, or mild anemia. You may feel tired or have trouble exercising. To raise it: find and treat the cause. Eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C. A provider can check ferritin, B12, folate, thyroid, and gut health.

**Optimal (4.1–5.1 M/μL):** This is the goal. It means your body is making red blood cells well and your iron, B12, and folate are good. To stay here: keep up a nutrient-dense diet with iron, B12, folate, and vitamin C. Stay hydrated. Periodically re-check, especially for menstruating women or men on testosterone therapy.

**Functional High (5.11–5.28 M/μL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can be from dehydration, endurance training, high altitude, or too much iron. Very high RBC can make blood thick. To optimize: drink more water. Cut back on iron supplements if needed.

**Clinical High (5.29 M/μL and above):** This needs evaluation. Causes: polycythemia vera, secondary polycythemia (smoking, COPD, sleep apnea, altitude, congenital heart disease), dehydration, testosterone therapy, or rare tumors. To address: work with a provider. Stop smoking. Treat sleep apnea. Adjust testosterone therapy. Phlebotomy may be needed. Drink more water.

### MCV (Mean Corpuscular Volume)

**What is this test?** MCV measures the average size of your red blood cells. Small red blood cells (low MCV) and large red blood cells (high MCV) can both be signs of problems.

**Why does it matter?** It helps your provider figure out what kind of anemia (or other problem) you may have. Low MCV usually means iron deficiency. High MCV usually means B12 or folate deficiency, or other issues.

**Reference ranges (fL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–79.9  
- Functional Low: 80.0–83.9  
- **Optimal: 84.0–95.0**  
- Functional High: 95.1–100.0  
- Clinical High: 100.1 and above

**Clinical Low (below 80.0 fL):** This means microcytic anemia — your red blood cells are too small. It impairs oxygen delivery and causes fatigue, brain fog, and trouble exercising. Causes: iron deficiency (most common), thalassemia (a genetic blood disorder), sideroblastic anemia (a rare disorder where the body cannot use iron properly), anemia of chronic disease, lead poisoning, and low copper. To address: work with a provider. Take iron (iron bisglycinate or ferrous sulfate) with vitamin C. Eat iron-rich foods. A provider can check ferritin, TIBC, and gut health.

**Functional Low (80.0–83.9 fL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early iron deficiency, mild thalassemia trait, or low copper. You may feel tired or have trouble exercising. To raise it: eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C. Consider iron supplements if ferritin is low. Make sure you get enough copper (shellfish, nuts, seeds, dark chocolate). A provider can check ferritin, iron studies, and hemoglobin electrophoresis (a test for thalassemia).

**Optimal (84.0–95.0 fL):** This is the goal. It means your red blood cells are the right size, and your iron, B12, folate, and thyroid are good. To stay here: keep up a nutrient-dense diet with iron, B12, folate, and vitamin C. Avoid too much alcohol. Keep a healthy gut. Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (95.1–100.0 fL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean early B12 or folate deficiency, underactive thyroid, alcohol use, certain medicines (methotrexate, hydroxyurea), liver disease, or high red blood cell turnover. You may feel tired, foggy, or have memory issues. To optimize: make sure you get enough B12 (animal products or supplements for vegans), folate (leafy greens, beans, avocado), and thyroid support. Cut back on alcohol. A provider can check B12, folate, methylmalonic acid, homocysteine, and thyroid.

**Clinical High (100.1 fL and above):** This is macrocytic anemia — your red blood cells are too big. Causes: B12 deficiency (most common), folate deficiency, underactive thyroid, liver disease, alcoholism, certain medicines (methotrexate, hydroxyurea, zidovudine, chemo), myelodysplastic syndrome (a bone marrow disorder), and rare genetic conditions. Symptoms: fatigue, brain fog, memory loss, neuropathy (tingling/numbness in B12 deficiency), and glossitis (swollen, smooth tongue). To address: work with a provider. Take B12 (injections for severe deficiency, sublingual or oral for milder). Take folate. Address the cause (thyroid, alcohol, medicines).

### MCH (Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin)

**What is this test?** MCH measures the average amount of hemoglobin in each red blood cell. It is closely tied to MCV.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you how much hemoglobin is in each cell. Low MCH means hypochromic anemia (cells are pale). It usually means iron deficiency.

**Reference ranges (pg, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–27.0  
- Functional Low: 27.1–27.9  
- **Optimal: 28.0–32.0**  
- Functional High: 32.1–33.0  
- Clinical High: 33.1 and above

**Clinical Low (below 27.0 pg):** This means hypochromic anemia — your red blood cells do not have enough hemoglobin. Causes: iron deficiency (most common), thalassemia, sideroblastic anemia, anemia of chronic disease, and lead poisoning. To address: work with a provider. Take iron with vitamin C. Eat iron-rich foods. A provider can check ferritin, iron studies, and gut health.

**Functional Low (27.1–27.9 pg):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early iron deficiency or mild thalassemia trait. To raise it: eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C. Consider iron supplements if ferritin is low. Check for thalassemia if it stays low. A provider can check ferritin, iron studies, and hemoglobin electrophoresis.

**Optimal (28.0–32.0 pg):** This is the goal. It means your red blood cells have the right amount of hemoglobin. To stay here: keep up a nutrient-dense diet with iron, B12, folate, and vitamin C. Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (32.1–33.0 pg):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can mean high iron, B12 or folate excess, macrocytosis, or high red blood cell turnover. It is usually not a problem on its own. To optimize: if you take iron supplements, watch your dose. Make sure your nutrition is balanced. If it stays high, check B12, folate, and reticulocyte count (a measure of new red blood cells).

**Clinical High (33.1 pg and above):** This is uncommon and usually happens with macrocytic anemia from B12 or folate deficiency, underactive thyroid, liver disease, or alcoholism. To address: work with a provider. Treat the cause (B12, folate, thyroid, alcohol, liver).

### MCHC (Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin Concentration)

**What is this test?** MCHC measures how concentrated hemoglobin is in your red blood cells. It is closely tied to MCH and MCV.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you how "packed" with hemoglobin each cell is. Low MCHC means hypochromic anemia (cells are pale). High MCHC can mean hereditary spherocytosis (a genetic condition where red blood cells are round instead of disc-shaped).

**Reference ranges (g/dL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–31.9  
- Functional Low: 32.0–32.5  
- **Optimal: 32.6–35.0**  
- Functional High: 35.1–36.0  
- Clinical High: 36.1 and above

**Clinical Low (below 32.0 g/dL):** This means hypochromic anemia. Causes: iron deficiency (most common), thalassemia, sideroblastic anemia, anemia of chronic disease, and lead poisoning. To address: work with a provider. Take iron with vitamin C. Eat iron-rich foods. A provider can check ferritin, iron studies, and gut health.

**Functional Low (32.0–32.5 g/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It often means early iron deficiency or mild thalassemia trait. To raise it: eat iron-rich foods with vitamin C. Consider iron supplements if ferritin is low. A provider can check ferritin and iron studies.

**Optimal (32.6–35.0 g/dL):** This is the goal. It means your red blood cells have the right amount of hemoglobin. To stay here: keep up a nutrient-dense diet with iron, B12, folate, and vitamin C. Avoid too much alcohol. Keep a healthy gut. Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (35.1–36.0 g/dL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It is usually harmless. It can mean dehydration or high iron. To optimize: drink more water. Watch your iron supplement dose if you take them. If it stays very high, check for hereditary spherocytosis (a benign genetic condition).

**Clinical High (36.1 g/dL and above):** This is rare. It can mean hereditary spherocytosis, severe dehydration, or very high iron. To address: work with a provider. Treat the cause. For hereditary spherocytosis, the spleen may need to be removed in severe cases. For dehydration, drink more water.

### RDW (Red Cell Distribution Width)

**What is this test?** RDW measures how much your red blood cells vary in size. A high RDW means your red blood cells are different sizes (some big, some small). A low RDW means they are all about the same size.

**Why does it matter?** A high RDW is a sign of mixed anemia, recent blood loss, or nutrient problems. It is also an independent predictor of heart disease and death.

**Reference ranges (%, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–7.99  
- Functional Low: 8.0–10.0  
- **Optimal: 10.01–14.0**  
- Functional High: 14.01–15.0  
- Clinical High: 15.01 and above

**Clinical Low (below 8.0%):** A low RDW means your red blood cells are all about the same size. This is usually a good sign. No changes needed. To maintain: keep up a nutrient-dense diet with iron, B12, and folate.

**Functional Low (8.0–10.0%):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It is usually a sign of healthy red blood cell production. To maintain: keep up a nutrient-dense diet with iron, B12, and folate.

**Optimal (10.01–14.0%):** This is the goal. It means your red blood cells are uniform in size and well-made. It is also linked to lower heart risk and longer life. To stay here: keep up a nutrient-dense diet with iron, B12, folate, and vitamin C. Exercise, sleep, and manage stress. Periodically re-check.

**Functional High (14.01–15.0%):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It means your red blood cells vary in size. Common causes: early iron deficiency, low B12 or folate, mixed anemia, recent blood loss, or chronic inflammation. You may feel tired or have higher long-term heart risk. To optimize: find and treat the cause. Eat more iron, B12, and folate. A provider can check ferritin, B12, folate, and inflammation.

**Clinical High (15.01% and above):** A high RDW raises your risk of heart disease, death, and chronic illness. Common causes: iron deficiency anemia (most common), B12 or folate deficiency, mixed deficiencies, chronic blood loss, bone marrow disorders, chronic inflammation, liver disease, kidney disease, and recent blood transfusion. To address: work with a provider. Find and treat the cause. You may need iron, B12, or folate. Eat a nutrient-dense, anti-inflammatory diet.

### Platelet Count

**What is this test?** Platelets are small blood cells that help your blood clot. They also play a role in wound healing and inflammation.

**Why does it matter?** Platelets are needed to stop bleeding. Low platelets (thrombocytopenia) raise your risk of bleeding. High platelets (thrombocytosis) raise your risk of clots and can signal inflammation.

**Reference ranges (K/μL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–74  
- Functional Low: 75–149  
- **Optimal: 150–400**  
- Functional High: 401–454  
- Clinical High: 455 and above

**Clinical Low (below 75 K/μL):** This is called thrombocytopenia. Your blood does not clot well, so you bleed or bruise easily. Causes: bone marrow disorders (leukemia, aplastic anemia, myelodysplastic syndrome), autoimmune destruction (ITP — immune thrombocytopenia, lupus), viral infections (HIV, hepatitis C, EBV, COVID), medicines (heparin, chemo, some antibiotics), severe infections (sepsis), low B12, folate, or copper, liver disease, and rare genetic conditions. To address: work with a provider. Treatment depends on the cause — treat the underlying condition, corticosteroids for ITP, platelet transfusions for severe bleeding risk. Support your bone marrow with B12, folate, iron, and zinc.

**Functional Low (75–149 K/μL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can mean mild bone marrow suppression, chronic viral infections (EBV, COVID), low nutrients, or mild autoimmune activity. You may bruise easily, heal slowly, or feel tired. To raise it: find and treat the cause. Support your bone marrow with a nutrient-dense diet with B12, folate, iron, zinc, and vitamin C. A provider can check viral load, gut, and autoimmunity.

**Optimal (150–400 K/μL):** This is the goal. It means balanced clotting and low chronic inflammation. To stay here: keep up a colorful plant-based diet rich in antioxidants, exercise, sleep 7–9 hours, manage stress, avoid smoking, limit alcohol, and maintain a healthy gut.

**Functional High (401–454 K/μL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It often means low-grade chronic inflammation, iron deficiency, infection, smoking, belly fat, or chronic stress. Higher platelets in this range are linked to higher heart and clot risk. To optimize: address any inflammation or iron deficiency. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet (vegetables, berries, omega-3 fats). Quit smoking. Lose extra belly fat. Exercise regularly. A provider can check inflammation, ferritin, and gut health.

**Clinical High (455 K/μL and above):** This is called thrombocytosis. It raises your risk of clots. Causes: reactive (iron deficiency, infection, inflammation, surgery, cancer, splenectomy), primary (essential thrombocythemia — a bone marrow disorder), and certain medicines. To address: work with a provider. Find and treat the cause. Treatment may include low-dose aspirin, hydroxyurea (a medicine that lowers platelet count), or other medicines. Address iron deficiency if present.

### MPV (Mean Platelet Volume)

**What is this test?** MPV measures the average size of your platelets. Larger platelets are younger and more active, which means higher clot risk. Smaller platelets are older and less active.

**Why does it matter?** Larger platelets are more reactive and raise your risk of clots and heart problems. MPV gives you extra information beyond just the platelet count.

**Reference ranges (fL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–9.3  
- Functional Low: 9.4–10.5  
- **Optimal: 10.6–11.9**  
- Functional High: 12.0–12.2  
- Clinical High: 12.3 and above

**Clinical Low (below 9.3 fL):** This means smaller, older platelets. It is usually a sign of lower clot risk. There is usually no concern. To maintain: keep up a healthy lifestyle.

**Functional Low (9.4–10.5 fL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It is usually harmless. To maintain: keep up a healthy lifestyle.

**Optimal (10.6–11.9 fL):** This is the goal. It means balanced platelet size with low clot risk. To stay here: keep up a whole-food, anti-inflammatory diet, exercise, sleep 7–9 hours, manage stress, avoid smoking, and limit alcohol.

**Functional High (12.0–12.2 fL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It means larger, more reactive platelets, which raises your clot and heart risk, especially if your platelet count is also high or you have diabetes, high blood pressure, or you smoke. Common causes: low-grade chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, smoking, and recent heart attack or stroke. To optimize: address inflammation, insulin resistance, and heart risk factors. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet, exercise, lose extra weight, and manage stress. A provider can check inflammation, fasting insulin, and heart risk.

**Clinical High (12.3 fL and above):** This means very large, hyperactive platelets. It is linked to a much higher risk of clots. Causes: essential thrombocythemia, other bone marrow disorders, recent heart attack or stroke, uncontrolled diabetes, severe inflammation, and certain cancers. To address: work with a provider. They may order a workup and consider low-dose aspirin, heart risk reduction, and treatment of the underlying cause. Eat a heart-healthy, anti-inflammatory diet.

### Absolute Neutrophils

**What is this test?** Neutrophils are the most common white blood cells. They are your body's first responders to bacterial and fungal infections. The absolute count is calculated from the WBC and the neutrophil percentage.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you how strong your body's first-line defense is. Low neutrophils raise your risk of bacterial and fungal infections. High neutrophils often mean bacterial infection, inflammation, or stress.

**Reference ranges (cells/μL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–1,499  
- Functional Low: 1,500–1,999  
- **Optimal: 2,000–5,000**  
- Functional High: 5,001–7,800  
- Clinical High: 7,801 and above

**Clinical Low (below 1,500/μL):** This is called neutropenia. You are at high risk of bacterial and fungal infections. Causes: chemo, radiation, bone marrow disorders, severe viral infections, autoimmune disease (lupus, RA), certain medicines (clozapine, methimazole, sulfa drugs), low B12, folate, or copper, and an overactive spleen. To address: work with a provider. They may give growth factors (G-CSF — a medicine that helps your bone marrow make more neutrophils), antibiotics for protection, and treat the cause. Support your bone marrow with B12, folate, zinc, and vitamin D.

**Functional Low (1,500–1,999/μL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can be from chronic viral infections (EBV, COVID), chronic stress, mild bone marrow suppression, or low nutrients. You may get sick more easily, heal slowly, or feel tired. To raise it: address any chronic viral infections. Support your immune system with a nutrient-dense diet, sleep, and stress control. Make sure you get enough zinc, vitamin D, vitamin C, B12, and folate.

**Optimal (2,000–5,000/μL):** This is the goal. It means balanced innate immune function with appropriate response to infection and low chronic inflammation. To stay here: keep up a whole-food diet, exercise, sleep 7–9 hours, manage stress, avoid smoking, limit alcohol, and maintain a healthy gut.

**Functional High (5,001–7,800/μL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It often means low-grade chronic inflammation, hidden infection, smoking, belly fat, chronic stress, or intense exercise. Higher neutrophils in this range are linked to higher heart risk. To optimize: address inflammation and any hidden infection. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet, quit smoking, lose extra belly fat, and exercise moderately. A provider can check inflammation, gut, teeth, and sinuses.

**Clinical High (7,801/μL and above):** This means significant immune activation. Most often, it is from a bacterial infection. Other causes: severe inflammation (autoimmune disease, vasculitis), tissue damage (surgery, trauma, burns), certain medicines (steroids, lithium, beta-agonists), and bone marrow disorders (chronic myeloid leukemia, polycythemia vera). To address: work with a provider. Treat the underlying infection or inflammation. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet.

### Absolute Lymphocytes

**What is this test?** Lymphocytes are white blood cells that include T cells, B cells, and natural killer (NK) cells. They are key to fighting viruses, making antibodies, and immune memory. The absolute count is calculated from the WBC and the lymphocyte percentage.

**Why does it matter?** It tells you how strong your adaptive immune system is. Low lymphocytes raise your risk of viral infections. High lymphocytes can mean recent viral infection, chronic viral infection, or certain cancers.

**Reference ranges (cells/μL, both sexes):**

- Clinical Low: 0–849  
- Functional Low: 850–998  
- **Optimal: 999–2,999**  
- Functional High: 3,000–3,900  
- Clinical High: 3,901 and above

**Clinical Low (below 850/μL):** This is called lymphopenia. You are at higher risk of viral and other infections. Causes: HIV/AIDS, severe viral infections (COVID, flu, EBV), chemo, radiation, bone marrow disorders, autoimmune disease (lupus, RA), severe malnutrition, certain medicines (steroids, immunosuppressants), and rare genetic conditions. To address: work with a provider. Treat the cause. Support your immune system with a nutrient-dense diet, sleep, stress control, and key nutrients (zinc, vitamin D, vitamin C, B12, folate).

**Functional Low (850–998/μL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It can be from chronic viral infections (EBV, COVID, HIV), chronic stress, malnutrition, or mild bone marrow suppression. You may get sick more easily, heal slowly, or feel tired. To raise it: address any chronic viral infections. Support your immune system with a nutrient-dense diet, sleep, and stress control. Make sure you get enough zinc, vitamin D, vitamin C, B12, and folate.

**Optimal (999–2,999/μL):** This is the goal. It means a balanced adaptive immune system that responds well to viruses and other invaders. To stay here: keep up a whole-food diet, exercise, sleep 7–9 hours, manage stress, avoid smoking, limit alcohol, and maintain a healthy gut.

**Functional High (3,000–3,900/μL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. It can be from recent viral infection (during recovery), chronic viral infections, autoimmune disease, smoking, or chronic inflammation. You may have mild immune activation. To optimize: address any chronic viral infection or inflammation. Eat an anti-inflammatory diet, quit smoking, exercise, and manage stress. A provider can check viral load, gut, and autoimmunity.

**Clinical High (3,901/μL and above):** This means significant lymphocyte growth. Most often, it is from a viral infection (especially during recovery). Other causes: chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), other lymphoproliferative disorders (conditions where too many lymphocytes are made), certain infections (whooping cough, mono), and autoimmune disease. To address: work with a provider. They may do a peripheral smear, flow cytometry (a test that looks at cell markers), and possibly bone marrow biopsy. Treat the cause.

### Absolute Eosinophils

**What is this test?** Eosinophils are white blood cells that play a big role in allergies, parasite infections, and some autoimmune diseases. The absolute count is calculated from the WBC and the eosinophil percentage.

- **Optimal: 101–300**  
- Functional High: 300.1–499.9  
- Clinical High: 500 and above

**Clinical Low (below 15.0/μL):** Very low eosinophils are usually not a problem. It is normal in healthy people without allergies or parasites. It can be from steroids or acute stress. No changes needed. To stay healthy: keep up a whole-food diet, manage allergies and stress, and treat any chronic infections.

**Functional Low (15.0–100.9/μL):** This is in the lab range but on the low side. It is usually harmless. To stay healthy: keep up a whole-food, anti-inflammatory diet, exercise, and a healthy lifestyle.

**Functional High (300.1–499.9/μL):** This is in the lab range but on the high side. Common causes: allergies (food, environmental, seasonal), asthma, eczema, mild parasite infection, certain medicines, and autoimmune disease. You may have allergic symptoms (sneezing, itching, rashes), gut issues, or skin problems. To optimize: find and address allergens (food sensitivity testing, elimination diet). Support gut health. Cut down on allergen exposure. A provider can check gut, food sensitivities, and immune function.

**Clinical High (500/μL and above):** This is called eosinophilia. It means significant allergic, parasitic, or autoimmune activity. Causes: severe allergies, asthma, eczema, parasite infections (worms, protozoa), certain autoimmune diseases (eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis, hypereosinophilic syndrome), certain cancers (lymphoma, leukemia), adrenal insufficiency, and certain medicines. To address: work with a provider. Find and treat the cause — parasite treatment, allergy management, autoimmune treatment, or evaluation for hypereosinophilic syndrome.

### Absolute Nucleated RBC (NRBC)

**What is this test?** Nucleated red blood cells (NRBCs) are immature red blood cells that still have a nucleus (the control center of the cell). They are normally only in the bone marrow and in newborns. If they show up in an adult's blood, it is a warning sign.

**Why does it matter?** It is a sign that your bone marrow is under stress and is releasing red blood cells before they are ready. Any NRBCs in an adult's blood is a serious finding.

**Reference ranges (cells/μL, both sexes):**

- **Optimal: 0–0.9 (Negative)**  
- Clinical High: 1 and above

Note: This test does not have a Clinical Low, Functional Low, or Functional High category in the OHS ranges. Anything above 0 is a Clinical High.

**Optimal (0–0.9, Negative):** A negative NRBC is the goal. It means your bone marrow is working well. To maintain: keep up a nutrient-dense diet with iron, B12, folate, and vitamin C. Avoid smoking, limit alcohol, and maintain a healthy lifestyle.

**Clinical High (1/μL and above, Any NRBC):** The presence of NRBCs in adult blood is a serious finding. It means your bone marrow is under stress. Causes: severe hemolytic anemia, severe blood loss with bone marrow response, severe infection or sepsis, low oxygen (heart failure, severe lung disease), bone marrow disorders, splenectomy (spleen removal), and post-chemotherapy recovery. To address: work with a provider for full workup. Treat the cause. Support your bone marrow with a nutrient-dense diet, adequate rest, and key nutrients (iron, B12, folate).
